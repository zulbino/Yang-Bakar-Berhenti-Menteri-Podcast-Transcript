# Interview (English)

**Co-Host 1:** The Minister Who Resigned Podcast, Episode 22. Peace and greetings to everyone. Welcome to all our new viewers. Don't forget to follow Mohd Rafizi Ramli's social media and our new Spotify channel, which has now reached almost 600 followers.

**Rafizi Ramli:** That's still very few. Talking as if it's been less than a month, it's embarrassing.

**Co-Host 1:** Alright, we take that back. Greetings to our viewers, including Jasni in Alor Setar and Ramanan in Shah Alam. Is our director with us?

**Director:** Yes, I'm here.

**Co-Host 1:** Alright, YB. Let's head straight into the first segment as usual.

**Rafizi Ramli:** This week we'll try to wrap up quickly. I'm quite tired because I've been managing the shop for two weeks. Besides, there weren't many big issues last week. People might have overreacted to certain things, but there weren't many large-scale or high-impact issues.

**Co-Host 1:** Alright, YB. First topic: "The nose of the National Monument statue is too pointed." Were you in the Dewan Rakyat during this debate, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I wasn't there. This was a debate brought up by the Member of Parliament for Sabak Bernam. He mentioned that the memorial features bronze statues of soldiers that resemble Europeans. If he hadn't pointed it out, I wouldn't have noticed either. But is he implying that the noses of Malays and Malaysians aren't pointed?

**Co-Host 1:** That might be the implicit message.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I think the reaction from Malaysians is the same. There are many other things that should have been brought to Parliament instead. Sometimes I find it hard to understand certain MPs. Speaking time in debates is very short, and getting a slot is difficult because dozens of people are on the waiting list. During my first term, there were times when I spoke at 11 p.m., or even at 2 a.m. The opportunity to speak is very limited, just two or three minutes. So, I don't understand how, in such a short window, the main thing on their mind is a bronze statue's nose being too pointed. At the very least, speak about issues concerning the residents in their constituency.

**Co-Host 1:** Maybe he had just finished jogging there yesterday?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It's hard to jog there. It's obvious you're not a jogger. That area is hilly. While hiking is suitable there, jogging is best done on a flat track.

**Co-Host 1:** Alright, YB. Next: "Shop owner mopping the floor, all due to an uncertain economy."

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is from a Facebook post by Rosziah Ismail. Who actually took this photo?

**Co-Host 1:** Were you actually cleaning the floor, YB, or was it just a political gimmick?

**Rafizi Ramli:** No, I was genuinely mopping the floor. The shop was handed over to us three days before the launch date. A normal business might wait a month for prep work before launching. But this is a social enterprise. Having to pay a month's rent without generating any revenue would burden the business. That's why I set a very tight timeline. 

Kitchen goods and equipment arrived two days before the launch. The photo was taken the day before the opening. During the chaos when everyone was focused on the kitchen area, nobody was cleaning the floor. So, my wife instructed me to mop the floor.

**Co-Host 1:** Some parties are making comparisons between you and Azmin Ali.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I'm not sure, but Azmin's situation is different; his status is higher at the Masjid al-Haram. Actually, during that hectic period, I didn't notice anyone taking photos. We had to get the shop ready in under 24 hours. But as usual, the national troll army has that kind of track record. They've always liked to criticize me because of my campaign against Najib Razak. These are the remnants of *wala'* followers—whether they are still paid or not, I'm not sure. 

The connotation that "a shop owner mops the floor because the economy is uncertain" shows that there is flawed thinking among certain parties. Assuming that a business owner mopping the floor only happens during an economic downturn is a wrong assumption. Real business owners will indeed roll up their sleeves and do hands-on work from the early stages so that employees cannot mislead them. If we read the history of major businesses, the original founders performed various tasks themselves. Thinking that a shop owner mopping the floor is a sign of an uncertain economy is a shallow view.

**Co-Host 2:** Was that photo taken before the shop opened?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, around 5 or 6 p.m. the day before the opening. It was quite hectic then because we needed to prepare ingredients and install appliances for the next day. The staff said they wanted to finish kitchen matters first before mopping the floor, but if we waited for that, it might only be done by 3 or 4 a.m. The next morning the shop was already opening, whereas renovation work at the site had only been completed a few days prior. So, I went straight to cleaning the floor. In fact, yesterday I was washing chicken because customer response was overwhelming and the chicken supply ran out earlier than expected.

**Co-Host 1:** Next YB: "The danger of racial stereotyping in corruption." Did you notice this issue, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Regarding the statement comparing Malaysian drivers with Singaporean drivers detained for bribery, claiming a difference in governance between "Type C" and "Type M"?

**Co-Host 1:** Yes, the Singaporean media portal report listed the names of the drivers and police officers involved.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Were the Malaysian drivers who gave bribes all Malay, and were the police officers Malay?

**Co-Host 1:** It involved various races.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Stereotypes like this happen often. From a mathematical standpoint, in school we learned about normal distribution or the bell curve. This is actually a law of nature. In any sample or population, the distribution will follow that bell curve. There is a small group on the far left, the majority group in the middle, and a small group on the far right. 

If we understand this law of normal distribution, stereotyping shouldn't exist. On the issue of corruption, the same distribution curve applies to any race—Malay, Chinese, or Indian. The majority in every race are honest people, while corrupt individuals lie at the extreme ends of the curve. Individuals who like to use racial stereotypes actually fail to understand this basic principle of normal distribution, because they take a sample from the tail of the curve and generalize the entire group.

**Co-Host 1:** Next, regarding the Terengganu State Government's vehicle loan scheme for State Legislative Assembly Members (ADUNs).

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is regarding the approval of a vehicle loan scheme for Terengganu ADUNs being increased from RM40,000 to RM200,000. Why did this matter need to be brought and approved in the State Executive Council (MMKN)?

**Co-Host 1:** Because it involves ADUNs and was approved in the State Legislative Assembly (DUN).

**Rafizi Ramli:** What is the need for the State or Federal Government to provide such a vehicle loan scheme? In the past, government loan schemes like LPPSA were created when commercial bank interest rates were very high, around 9% to 10%, while government schemes offered a 4% rate. However, nowadays, interest rates for housing or hire-purchase loans from commercial banks are already around 2% to 4%. So, if any ADUN wants to buy a new vehicle, they can take out a loan directly from a commercial bank.

**Co-Host 2:** The justification given by the State Government is that if calculated for all 32 ADUNs with a maximum amount of RM200,000 each, the total is only RM6.4 million, not RM16 million as claimed by some parties.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Using the RM6.4 million figure as a justification is still unreasonable financially and morally. First, in terms of financial capacity, the Terengganu State Government has one of the lowest self-generated revenue collections in Malaysia—around RM185 million annually from land tax and state revenue. Its annual budget, worth over RM1 billion, largely depends on federal grants and disbursements. Given the state's limited financial position, every single cent of public money is precious and counts toward the deficit.

Second, from a moral standpoint, many people in Terengganu, especially low-income groups and students, need assistance. ADUNs already earn far higher incomes than ordinary citizens. If they can afford to buy a vehicle worth RM200,000, they should take out a commercial bank loan themselves. If that RM6.4 million were allocated for educational aid, say RM200 a month (RM1,200 a year) per family, that amount could assist about 5,300 families in Terengganu. Moral considerations should prioritize the welfare of 5,300 ordinary families over providing the luxury of a special loan to 32 ADUNs.

**Co-Host 1:** Next: "Tengku Zafrul joined PKR for the struggle, not for a position." How many Malaysians actually believe that?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Tengku Zafrul himself believes it. What other answer could he give? However, in politics, the public doesn't judge words alone; they look at actions and long-term track records. Most people situated in the middle of the normal distribution curve hold divided views on politicians in general. 

Even more so for figures with a background of being directly appointed ("parachuted") as a Minister, then given a seat to contest in Kuala Selangor, and now named to contest in Ampang. In any party, the perception of a "golden child" and express appointments will generate dissatisfaction among the grassroots who have served for decades. In Selangor UMNO previously, this happened, and the same potential for turmoil could carry over into PKR. For me, it's simple: if he wants to join the struggle, let us all go down to the ground together and serve the people.

**Co-Host 1:** What if someone argues he is sincere because he gave up a corporate CEO position earning hundreds of thousands a month?

**Rafizi Ramli:** He didn't give up a CEO position to take to the streets, build a movement from the ground up, sacrifice his career at a young age, and face legal action or lockups like earlier reformists went through. He gave up a corporate position to immediately hold a Ministerial position with various perks. I do not consider a direct transition from corporate to a Minister post without a long record of advocating for marginalized citizens to be a real political sacrifice.

**Co-Host 2:** There is a view stating that Malaysia needs more politicians with technocratic backgrounds. What is your view, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** We indeed need policymakers who possess professional knowledge and backgrounds. However, there is an unintended consequence if we assume that all technocrats will automatically make good ministers. 

A good leader requires both: technocratic skills and political accountability. Someone cannot be an effective politician if they only have knowledge without an understanding of the pulse of the people. Conversely, if someone is purely a politician without any career background or specialized expertise, they will rely entirely on briefings from officials.

That is why I frequently advise young people in the party not to become full-time politicians at too young an age. Work in the industry first, build a career and skills, and at the same time serve the community. When a technocrat is directly appointed without having to go through an election process or answer directly to voters, they tend to make decisions from an "ivory tower" without feeling full accountability to the people.

**Co-Host 1:** Alright, YB. Next issue: a statement by an ADUN in Kedah regarding the distribution of Gardenia bread during campaigning in Sabah.

**Rafizi Ramli:** The Kuala Ketil ADUN, who is also an Executive Councillor (Exco) in the Kedah State Government. Such remarks belittling Sabahans should not come from an elected representative. It reflects a low regard for the people of Sabah. My advice to voters in Sabah is to evaluate such statements maturely when the state election arrives. As an Exco member, he should be more careful with his words.

**Co-Host 1:** Moving on to the "One-Week Post-Mortem of Kesum and Fleximart" segment.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Management-wise, if we are a leader or manager, we need to understand operations from A to Z. My experience managing companies since the age of 26 taught me that if we don't learn and do the ground-level work ourselves, we won't understand actual operational realities. That's why on opening day, I stayed at the shop all day to monitor operational flow.

Alhamdulillah, the response from the public was very encouraging. More importantly, it opened public discourse on the concept of a social enterprise. My intention since youth has been to see more young Bumiputera youth enter business. Based on demographic distribution and socioeconomic history, there is a tendency among high-achieving Bumiputera middle-class individuals to choose salaried corporate or civil service careers over taking risks in business. The household income gap actually stems from employment types, where a large portion of the Malay middle class relies on salaried jobs.

**Co-Host 2:** Many young people want to do business, but the main challenge is capital mobilization. How does this social enterprise model solve that issue?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Capital mobilization is indeed a major challenge. Conventional business models require large capital, bank loans that are hard to secure, or equity investors demanding very high returns. On the other hand, government grant models risk creating "grantpreneurs" who rely on assistance without building business resilience.

In Islam, there is the concept of *Musyarakah* (partnership) which aligns with the principles of a social market economy—that is, balancing profit-seeking with social objectives. Through Kesum and Fleximart, I provided early capital through Cumulative Unsecured Loan Stock (CULS) instruments with a fixed minimum return rate of only about 2.5% (around RM500 per month for investors). 

The goal is to demonstrate the existence of a new asset class in the form of social enterprise investments. Bumiputera middle-class individuals and professionals with savings can invest in these social enterprises. This investment provides stable and safe returns, while excess business profits are redistributed to employees and young management teams. This lowers the entry barrier for young people to start businesses without being burdened by excessive capitalist profit margins.

In terms of Kesum's operations, since we sell meals like fried chicken nasi lemak at RM5.00 to RM5.50 (with raw ingredient costs around RM3.00–RM3.50 and indirect costs around RM1.00), the business margin is very thin. This business relies entirely on high sales volume and operational efficiency. The process from order to table (plating) must be done in under 5 minutes with optimal kitchen staffing (a 3-person kitchen crew). Physical efficiency and kitchen design need to be improved over time to eliminate operational bottlenecks.

**Co-Host 2:** Some buyers uploaded receipts on social media claiming that this RM5.00 price could affect other local traders. What is your explanation, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Kesum is a social experiment. In reality, the raw ingredient cost for a plate of nasi lemak with fried chicken is around RM3.00 to RM3.50. When a regular business sells it at RM12.00, the gross margin is around 300%. Kesum does not carry the burden of high margins for excessive owner profits; instead, it caps investor returns at RM500 a month. This proves that with operational efficiency and the right capital structure, quality food can be sold at an affordable price.

**Co-Host 1:** How does this relate to the Progressive Wage Policy and the issues facing the SME sector?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The main problem facing Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in Malaysia is high employee turnover and difficulty attracting quality talent. Most graduates choose to work for large corporate companies in urban centers. SMEs spend time and costs training new workers, only for employees to job-hop after a few months.

The Progressive Wage Policy was designed as an equitable opt-in scheme. The government provides financial incentives (up to RM200 a month per employee) to employers for a 2-year period, provided the employer raises starting wages and provides upskilling training. After 2 years, the productivity gains resulting from that training allow employers to sustainably maintain those higher wage brackets.

Addressing criticism from certain parties like Tong Kooi Hong regarding the participation of 21,000 workers compared to the initial target of 50,000: these 21,000 workers involve around 700 employers who are genuinely committed to raising their wage structures and company productivity. The government could easily hit a 100% target populistically by handing out cash unconditionally, but that is unsustainable and does not resolve structural economic issues. This policy aims to improve public wage levels and SME competitiveness step by step.

**Co-Host 1:** Next is regarding political developments ahead of the Sabah State Election (PRN) and the party's approach.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Regarding Pakatan Harapan Secretary-General Saifuddin Nasution's statement that "elections are about winning" and that any winning strategy is good: if from the start the main objective was purely to win by any means necessary, the slogan "Lawan Tetap Lawan" (Fight On) would never have existed. 

My stance in PKR is based on principles and scientific data. In 2016, when we formed Pakatan Harapan without PAS, conventional wisdom at the time claimed PH would suffer a crushing defeat because, without PAS, Malay votes could not be secured. However, based on survey data and principles of fighting corruption and upholding diversity, we stood firm on those principles. That commitment to principle attracted swing voters.

Regarding the Sabah state election, parachuting candidates or naturalizing figures from other parties—to the extent of sidelining local PKR branch heads who have served for decades—might offer short-term advantages under the "win at all costs" theory. But from a party-building perspective, it destroys the foundational pillars of the party in Sabah. Out of 26 PKR branches in Sabah, only two or three branch heads were selected as candidates. This weakens grassroot motivation and loyalty in the long run.

**Co-Host 1:** What about the issue of Sabah's 40% federal revenue entitlement claim?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The claim for 40% of federal revenue derived from Sabah is a right enshrined under Articles 112C and 112D of the Federal Constitution. The Federal Government appealed against certain technical aspects of the High Court ruling, rather than denying that constitutional right outright. 

As Prime Minister, Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim has had to manage this historical legacy issue left unresolved by previous administrations. However, the top priority for both the Federal and State Governments must be ensuring that the rights of Sabahans are restored and the state's socioeconomic development is thoroughly supported.

**Co-Host 2:** What is your view on ministries using influencers/KOLs to publicize policies, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** When I led the Ministry of Economy, I did not support using large amounts of public funds to pay social media influencers. Some influencer fees reached RM80,000 for a single video post. Using influencers is unsustainable because their loyalty lies with whoever pays them. The best approach for any public administrator is to communicate policy information directly, factually, and prudently through official channels without wasting public money.

**Co-Host 1:** Final question from a viewer: regarding the review of the Administrative Assistant salary scheme in the civil service, which is claimed to be lagging behind.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Matters regarding public service schemes and civil servant salary structures fall under the jurisdiction of the Public Service Department (JPA) and the Ministry of Finance, led by the Prime Minister. I am not directly involved in the administration of public service schemes. My suggestion is for association representatives to bring this matter directly to the leadership of the Ministry of Finance or JPA for further consideration.

**Co-Host 1:** Thank you YB for the explanations tonight. We'll see you again in the next episode. Peace be upon you and good night.
