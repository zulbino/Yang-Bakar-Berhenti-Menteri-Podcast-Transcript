**Rafizi:** First, regarding the bloat question — what's usually said out there is that we have the most civil servants compared to other countries. I think, again, as I say, we have to be objective, because the definition of civil servant differs from country to country. For example, our civil servants include police officers, military personnel and teachers. In other countries, when they say civil servant, it only refers to PTD administrators. In the United States, for example, it doesn't include security forces and military. So I think, while we need to accept that we should ideally size the civil service to be as efficient as possible, we can't apply a blanket approach to everything. So if you ask me, adding civil servants by adding teachers, even though the percentage of civil servants to population becomes high, I don't think there's anything wrong with it. The more teachers, the more doctors, the more nurses we have, the better. That's why I usually don't jump on the bandwagon that simply says, "oh we have too many civil servants" — across the board. It's the same with any organization, especially large ones. When you have a big organization, and in this matter of civil servants, the biggest employer in this country is the government. What's important is that the right manning is given to the most important and productive sector. That's actually why the process in the civil service is all controlled, it's not that easy. For example, for every ministry or agency to add people, they need approval from JPA, the Public Service Department. And I can tell you, after one year here, it's not easy to get additions — that's why we here still have to double up, that's why one person does this, the same person does that too. At the agency level, it depends on the specific agencies — some agencies have their own board of directors, so that process is the same, adding or hiring follows their own rules. So that's from the governance of staffing perspective. Another way we have control is in terms of allocation, because a department or agency can't add people arbitrarily because it needs to secure an operating allocation. So that operating allocation is also controlled to prevent agencies from adding arbitrarily. As for civil servants now — we have projections based on our population growth, which services within the civil service need to be increased, who needs to be hired more. For example, every department has someone responsible for task A, task B, task C, then another department also has people A, B, C doing the same things everywhere. Back in the 1980s, that indeed had to be done manually, so it had to be at every single location, recorded manually and so on. But now, with technology and so on, wherever things can be modernized so that these tasks can be done by a smaller group, and that allows this staffing to be redirected to other sectors that need it more. So I think that process is ongoing. To answer Faiz's earlier question about whether they can't just be merged into fewer agencies — same thing, and I caution those of us outside too, there's no one-size-fits-all. For example, if we take Bumiputera agencies — firstly, I think we need to accept the background of each agency when it was established. It was established because there was a need for it, because its mandate was specifically for something that had a need at that time. The problem is, after 20 years, 30 years, has it succeeded in carrying out its mandate, or has it drifted off course. So if we take a specific agency as an example — MARA, for example — its target group is quite different. Its target group and the programs it runs are largely about educating the population, schools and universities. In terms of its assistance and empowerment, much of it is also in the form of building small entrepreneurs. But what about entrepreneurs at what people call the growth stage? They've gone through the business, Bumiputera, then reached a certain stage, but to get listed, for example, it needs to be leveled up. And in the normal business world, that process usually involves a private equity firm, a PE firm, because a PE firm will come in, take equity, and build it together up to a certain value. Once the value is right, then it can be listed. When Ekuinas was established, that function was seen as necessary within the Bumiputera ecosystem, because that expertise didn't exist in any other agency. That's why, from time to time, we have different agencies. So while I agree that we always need to ensure there's alignment in terms of functions so they don't overlap, each focusing on its target group and delivering the mandate for which they were created in the first place — we need to be careful not to just say, "let's merge everything," because size can become a source of corruption, size is also a source for bureaucracy and red tape. It's true, one big agency, but it also combines inefficiency with a lack of skills in sectors it's supposed to have. In the end you have one big giant but the effect is worse. That, I think, within my own ministry, is our focus, and I'm sure other ministries too — you bring them back to the mandate and make sure they do what they are mandated as efficiently as possible.
