**Rafizi:** Actually, the Sales and Service Tax (SST) is not a new thing. It is a tax that has been around for a long time, and year after year during the Budget process, the government has the right to determine which sectors, types of services, or goods are subject to tax.

**Faiz:** Now there are 15, right? From 12 to 15?

**Rafizi:** Yes, 15—adding three new ones. From 12 to 15, as well as setting the rates. So it isn't something that only happened during the Unity Government; this has been happening all along. For companies that newly qualify to pay tax, I do understand it takes time to really understand the difference. **Faiz:** Could you explain a bit of an example of that infrastructure?

So our concept: the government clears land, builds modern agricultural facilities, and provides equipment. This is not cash; participants come to work and farm the site. This concept—which to a certain extent is about giving the fishing rod, not the fish—is what we are developing for Bumiputera programs under Ministry of Economy agencies such as Teraju, Ekuinas, and Yayasan Peneraju. I spent the last 7–8 months fixing this after finding that audit results of previous programs were very unsatisfactory. Some things were abused, grants didn't reach target groups, and if continued, Bumiputera entrepreneurs would continue to complain about not receiving help.

On the manning issue of whether it is bloated or not: I understand the tendency of private business owners to question workforce size. We have to look at it objectively in terms of manning and agenda. Government departments and agencies are not the same as the private sector because there are societal responsibilities and functions that do not exist in the private sector, including scrutinizing unintended consequences before making decisions.

Having said that, all agencies under the Ministry of Economy went through that process. We have parameters for right-sizing and targeting optimal manning. There are two ways: cut the manning, or sweat the asset. Sweat the asset means if there are 100 staff, their output needs to be increased much higher with the same workforce size.

The best example is in the Ministry of Economy itself. Previously, the Economic Planning Unit (EPU) only formulated policies without implementing programs. When converted into the Ministry of Economy, for instance in the poverty eradication division which only has 12–13 people, staff who previously just made policy now have to get hands-on in eradicating poverty and running programs. This group could easily ask for another 20 people, but our approach is: with the same amount of people and resources, you now have to do double than what you did before.

Likewise with other agencies. If there's a division with obvious redundancy or fat, we will do the necessary to right-size it according to procedures and contract of employment. **Rafizi:** Regarding allegations that the public service is bloated: it is often mentioned outside that we have a large number of civil servants compared to other countries. We have to be objective, because the definition of civil servant differs by country. In Malaysia, civil servants include police, military, teachers, and doctors. The more teachers, doctors, and nurses we have, the better. I don't jump on the bandwagon of just listening to public perceptions.

Manual tasks that can be replaced by device systems do not need added positions, so that allocations to hire new positions are focused on police, teachers, nurses, and doctors.

After a year here, I know it's not easy to get additional positions; that's why we here have to double up. As for agencies, control is exercised through Boards of Directors and approval of operating expenditure (Opex) allocations, which are closely supervised.
