# Interview (English)

**Moderator:** Alright, peace be upon you, and welcome back to the hottest podcast in Malaysia—"Yang Bakar Menteri". In this second episode, I have moved locations. As for the third episode, I don't even know where I will be sitting.

Before we begin, YB, we have a special guest for this episode, namely Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai, a renowned economist known for his criticisms of our country's economic model. I want to highlight the first episode a bit because many people commented and criticized it. Some said our podcast is scripted. I want to ask the professor himself: Professor, your coming here, is it scripted or not?

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** Not at all. Even I don't know what the questions are or how we are going to discuss them.

**Moderator:** You heard the answer yourself from Professor Dr. Barjoyai Bardai that this podcast is not scripted. If you want to know whether it is scripted or not, come here. We want to open the floor and give the full opportunity to the professor. Please go ahead, Prof.

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** Thank you, Mr. Moderator. Yang Berhormat YB, Minister of Economy, Dato'—not yet, Dato' from King King King.

First, I want to start with a broader topic. Now, I am listening to the term Libertarian Economy. It is said that in Malaysia, we are heading towards a Libertarian Economy. Basically, a Libertarian Economy means a free-enterprise, market-based system. And because of that, we become ashamed to provide subsidies to households. In other words, we have to abolish subsidies at one point. So, is this our direction for the future?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Oh, he's not giving me a chance! Hahaha.

I'm not sure how many people really understand "libertarian." An economy that is fair to an extreme, where doing business is done to the point that it produces inefficiencies. But at the same time, the government must also have a baseline to focus on the groups that need support.

So the issue now is how best you do that with the constraints we have and also the stage of our economy. So, to answer directly what Prof asked, it is not libertarian. I just feel that we need to move towards a lot more efficient social assistance and social protection. So, how best to move away, that's the question about subsidies, right?

When we look at this issue of subsidies, *by and large*, one subsidy, if given to a large company, it is called an incentive, but to the people, it is called a subsidy. We know that. Yes, when there is an investment, we give incentives and so on. We would like to move away from that. In an economy that is based more on *creativity*, *content value*, and investments that come because of that rather than because we give *incentives*, for example. That's one part. And I think the focus on restructuring the economy—the question of competitiveness, the question of value-add, moving into more value creation in high-growth, high-value sectors—indirectly will move us away from the need to always give the carrot.

But on the issue of subsidies as well, if bulk subsidies go to *the group that actually does not need it*, or the sector that does not need it, and we have a way to really target and transfer that in the form of social assistance and social protection, I don't think that's going libertarian. That, I think, is sticking to how best we protect the vulnerable group. And I think that's what we are doing now—how efficiently we are using resources.

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** Okay, now slightly related to that. If, for example, our goal is household happiness, it must take into account *the household*. When we have numbers like GDP or per capita income, it doesn't make sense to them. Today, for example, our economy is rising. We are in an economic cycle where the economy is rising, and we expect that until 2025 or maybe 2026, we will still rise. But the households below say, "Whatever they are talking about, we don't feel it." They say the economy is rising, but they are suffering. So, how do we rationalize that?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That is why, *between my focus and the ministry from the beginning*, I understand that with all the *noise*, *especially the political noise*, it is not easy to see the focus and clarity in what we are trying to do. Our focus is on household income and wages. It is not easy.

It's precisely on that basis, Prof. For us, for example, *based on our trajectory*, like Prof said, *we will be given the condition to grow on average 4% per year. Then the ringgit is around 4.50 in 2 to 3 years. By 2027 or 2028, we will be a high-income country.* We will be considered a developed country. But the disparity in society is important. That's one of the things I did at the beginning—whether the public understands it or not—is to put a focus on quarterly wage statistics. Because when we look at quarterly wage statistics, you can see, when we contextualize it, the country can grow by 5% GDP, but the fact is 50% of our formal workers are paid below RM2,600.

There's a disconnect somewhere. So, sometimes people will accuse me of being a libertarian, but there are also people who say that I'm a socialist because we are pushing to intervene in the wage setting straight away. Because if we let this go and do not focus as a government to raise wages, then there will forever be a *disconnect* between economic growth and what families feel.

And this takes a lot of work which also exposes us to continuous criticism. Because our country—and this is where I agree with Prof from the beginning, because you have been consistent on this—our country is too obsessed with economic growth figures and the projects that are announced. The more mega projects that are announced, certain companies' shares will rise and that will also increase GDP.

It works first, but we have reached a level where if we continue on that model, we can focus on that as a government—meaning we enter big projects to artificially try to push the stock market—but over the past 2 to 3 years, the gap has been getting bigger because the family below still feels, "My salary remains just like that." But the focus now—and I think we made that hard decision—the focus is on household income, salary, and the form of work that we produce.

In the short run, people will be angry because they don't feel the excitement of "Which project is big? Which one is RM30 billion?" But unless we do that, there's no point in becoming a high-income nation in 2 to 3 years. That, I hope, puts a context to whether or not we are moving towards a libertarian economy, because we are certainly not. There are certain sectors, especially those involving wages, where we still have a *direct intervention* approach.

Though, lately... after this, the moderator really won't have a function anymore because Prof already has question after question!

**Moderator:** Let him ask! I also have certain matters. If you need to stop, stop. I think the moderator will add more. Okay, thank you.

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** If GDP increases but the *share of income by industry* declines from 37% to 32%, when we look at heavy figures like this, we feel as if we are being exploited by industry. But actually, if we look at specific industries, some are increasing and some are declining, like construction. So this is the issue. Now, the question for households is that they don't understand this aspect. Why aren't their wages going up?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, that's right. I once managed a company with you, Prof. Managing a company, although the concept is the same, which is we want to *push the value*, is much easier because we focus on what is right in front of us. In this country and economy, as Prof said, *there are many sectors and many different and unique groups with their own problems.*

So let's take productivity. It is true that if we look at the overall productivity of the country, it is increasing. But if we translate it to the makeup composition of our economy, then we will be quite alarmed. For example, 97% of our enterprises are small, medium, and micro enterprises (MSMEs). Only 2% of companies are big. But these big companies basically drive about 50% plus of the GDP, and the number of employees they employ is slightly higher than all SMEs or non-formal companies.

So, they are the ones who actually have the ability to make automation investments and engage in global trade. Productivity is driven by them. Because of that structural economy, there is a problem with the wider workforce. We grew very quickly in the 80s and 90s through industrialization, and at that level, yes, we needed international investors. But there was a point where, because we did not focus much on *the bulk of the economy* that involves more of our people, when the sectors dominated by these big companies have *productivity largely contributed by them*, their salaries increase. That's also what caused a lot of young people to feel left out. A 25-year-old sees his friend getting a salary of RM5,000 while he works elsewhere as a graduate earning RM2,000. But their sectors are different.

**Moderator:** Right now, YB Rafizi, the Minister of Economy, is making an *intervention*. He says he wants to fix this through *direct intervention*, which is the *Progressive Wage*. Does Prof agree that this can address this problem?

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** Progressive Wage is a good effort. In Singapore, it has been implemented since 2014, but until today it is not finished. There are still certain sectors that have just started, for example, janitors, washers, and cleaners. Before this, they looked at choosing sectors where productivity is high. So if productivity is hard to measure, we couldn't do it.

Actually, we are somewhat left behind. If we look around the world today, they have already started thinking about a *universal wage*—a basic wage specific to everyone. This has been introduced in Canada, and across Europe it will be a standard for the European Union. So we may need to look at that too, alongside what YB mentioned earlier about social protection. We can do it together because we want to make sure—there are several countries where, when *universal wage* was introduced, poverty dropped by up to 30%. What is your view on *universal wage*?

**Rafizi Ramli:** *Universal wage* is Universal Basic Income. Yes, that's right. If we had the means, Prof, I think we would go for it straight away. The problem is, we also have to accept that our fiscal position is quite severe in terms of deficit.

We have to make a choice. For example, *is it sustainable* for us to continue going into debt to pay bulk subsidies and at the same time give a *basic wage*? We could become popular for a short while, *but it's not sustainable*. In fact, my concern is that if we continue straight on that basis, we may even destroy the economy because we do not push the right sectors to reach a certain productivity level for them to be competitive in the future.

So I think what we have to do now is to get things in order. Because of that, targeting these subsidy resources is the best step for us to start introducing elements of *social protection*, *which is comparable with Universal Basic Income*. Only, *while we continue to improve our fiscal position, it will open up more space for us to start strengthening social protection.* And at some point, whether we call it Universal Basic Income or *social protection*, the amount of *cash transfer* or assistance to families will become higher and help more of those in need.

That's from *social protection*—this is what we call *raising the floor*. But at the same time, *we can only do that if the economy continues to grow and becomes more competitive because we need income* from companies and workers who can generate more value.

That's why the focus on MSMEs, which make up 97%, is extremely important. I know there are analysts or commentators who say the Minister of Economy is doing nothing because there are no big projects. Of course, if we focus on big projects, in the end, the profit only goes to these 40 or 50 companies. Our focus is on these 97% of companies that need to be pushed upward.

If you ask any small or medium enterprise, they all want to become bigger. But they have constraints. We always hear about capital constraints, but an even bigger constraint is the talent constraint (*talent*), especially in this day and age where they must be ahead in terms of technology and creativity. Without *talent* and R&D, they cannot create something new to take their products to a higher level or new markets. For that, they need the right talent. If they pay low wages, they will never get that talent.

Ultimately, our workers who can generate higher value end up crowding and competing for jobs in only 50 to 60 big companies, and that does not help. That is why, besides *Universal Basic Income* which, God willing, will start to be introduced through *social protection*, this wage issue is not for popularity, but to enable small companies to start getting good talent to build better value.

*That leads to a bigger contribution from our MSMEs, meaning a more sustainable income to the government in the future. It's that virtuous cycle that allows us to move towards Universal Basic Income as and when we can afford it.*

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** I see the government is playing a *balancing act*. Like what YB said, there is a fiscal problem. As a citizen, I want the government to implement many populist principles, but we know the government has fiscal constraints.

I agree that we have started to establish the practice of financial discipline—*fiscal discipline*—where we want to bring down the deficit to 3%. That means we need to reduce the budget. What actually happens in Malaysia, if we really outline the government's administrative budget, the amount is much lower.

In Budget 2024, for example, if we look at the Ministry of Finance, it could reduce its budget by RM16 billion. The Ministry of Economy by more than RM1 billion. Other ministries increased by RM16 billion to RM20 billion. This shows that we need to go back and look at the budget. *Government budget should be government administration budget.* Now we include elements of subsidies, incentives, and various things that make it very bloated and out of control. If we could separate subsidies out of the budget, our budget would become lean and beautiful.

The only problem is, how to handle that budget? We have in the Islamic system, for example, the waqf economy, where waqf is an institution created for the *social protection* we mentioned earlier, funded by the public. But it needs to be started by the government.

I want to give an example of what happened in Norway with their *oil fund*. When they found oil, they opened an *endowment fund*—which we call waqf. Today, its value reaches RM10 trillion, so it can fund 30% of government expenditure.

We are not too late to start. We still have Petronas which is worth nearly RM800 billion, we have Khazanah, PNB, and many more. 40% of listed companies in Malaysia are government-owned. Why don't we move it to an endowment fund like a Waqf Fund? Then it is managed professionally like EPF. EPF *is doing very well*. The returns will then be used to fund *social protection* programs. So we do not touch its principal, *and because of that, it will become sustainable for the long term.* YB, what about the waqf economy?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, if we look at the example of the large listed companies, they are already owned by the people. PNB's capital is funded by unit trusts owned by ordinary citizens. Likewise, CIMB, Tenaga, and EPF are owned by the people through their contributions.

I think there are two groups of large companies that are not directly owned by the people in that manner. One is Petronas, which is state-owned, and the other is Khazanah. Petronas actually already contributes to an *endowment fund*, which is the National Trust Fund (KWAN). KWAN was never touched until the COVID-19 period.

The difference with KWAN—it has the National Trust Fund Act—is that it is not actively invested like the *sovereign fund* in Norway. I think it is extremely conservative because they really want to protect it. The contribution from oil and gas revenues into KWAN is not as large as what Norway does, but it once reached around RM20 billion plus up to 2017. The mechanism is already there.

But back to our fiscal problem. *To be fair to Petronas*, Petronas has certain profits and they follow government instructions. KWAN has its act and cannot be touched just like that except during the COVID-19 *emergency*. Petronas could contribute more to KWAN, but if the government's fiscal position requires RM40 to RM50 billion a year to cover bulk subsidies and incentives, where do we dig it from? We dig it from Petronas.

The same goes for Khazanah. The government can make a decision to put funds into KWAN, but its act has to be changed. We have that flexibility, *but we have to fix the government's fiscal position first, and it requires tough decisions.*

*Sovereign funds* like Norway's function more or less like PNB because they are highly *equity-driven* and invest globally. Meanwhile, KWAN is a conservative fund for us to *set aside*. If we want to do it like Norway, it means the government has to change the KWAN Act to create another active fund in the global financial market. But that petroleum trust money will be exposed to profit and loss. *If we can manage it as well as Norway, then it will go up, but it is also exposed to losses.*

The original principle of establishing KWAN was to protect the fund. *All these decisions are academic unless we fix the fiscal situation first.* Once the fiscal position is strong, economic growth is better, and the gap is controlled, then we can think about managing this extra fund.

**Moderator:** Prof, I read your criticisms of the Madani Economy. You said that because there are no elements like waqf, our country's economy follows the IMF framework. After YB Rafizi explained the situation of our waqf and funds earlier, do you still hold that view or has it changed?

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** Thank you. For more than 60 years, Malaysia's economy has actually developed depending on FDI (*foreign direct investment*). If we measure FDI against the growth rate, they run in parallel. At times when FDI goes on strike or does not come, we slow down.

Now we are at a *crossroad*. As long as we continue to depend on FDI, that's when our economy becomes volatile and vulnerable. Like what YB said earlier, we depend on certain companies to advance our economy. Even the foreign worker aspect is under their control.

We need to change direction. If we compare Singapore with Hong Kong, Singapore progressed based on FDI and its growth rate is lower than ours. Hong Kong, on the other hand, progressed by demonstrating entrepreneurship and worker *skills*, so it is more stable. We need to study this.

Currently, 70% of our workforce has no qualifications, so they are considered low-skilled or *semi-skilled*. Their skills need to be upgraded. We can use the *Progressive Wage* as a *carrot*. We say, "Okay, if you want an annual wage increase, you must undergo this training and upgrade your skills." It's conditional on employee upskilling.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Alhamdulillah, *that's good*. If I can add a little bit to what Prof mentioned earlier, *the split between FDI and domestic direct investment (DDI) was actually higher 10 years ago or in the 90s*. The percentage of DDI was much higher in the past than it is now.

When there is FDI, DDI will come along. Our challenge now is to *pivot* our economy. I agree we cannot rely entirely on FDI. The survival of our economic competitiveness depends heavily on our ability to *unlock* this DDI so that it generates higher-income jobs.

Over the past 10 to 15 years, the share of new investment in DDI has decreased, even though we do not lack capital. So, in these *high-value, high-growth* fields, while there are large investments from abroad, we must align the *value chain* involving DDI so that the ecosystem is built.

In the past, especially in the E&E and semiconductor sectors from the 70s to the 90s, we were more successful in building *value chains*. But recently, the *spillover* has been quite limited. That is why one of the main sectors we have included in the economic restructuring is modern agriculture.

FDI might come in at RM3 billion to RM4 billion in a high-tech sector like a *data center*, but the number of jobs created might only be 50 people, of which 10 are foreign experts and the other 40 are low-level workers. This is different from modern agriculture—under the People's Income Initiative (IPR), we use government land and build modern agriculture as a *production line*. For a cost of RM4 million over a 5-year period, it can benefit 200 workers who get RM2,000 to RM3,000 a month.

**Moderator:** This means that now we want to leverage local labor through the modern agriculture sector. We *utilize more labor*, but those who are a bit more *skilled-up*?

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is the *balance* that we have to make. We are talking about *high-growth, high-value sectors*, and it is true we need technology to *push the economy*. But we cannot forget that *job creation* for such sectors is smaller than before. That is why we cannot run away from sectors that have a *spillover effect* and more sustainable *job creation* for the people.

That is why when people criticize me saying there is no big plan, I don't mind taking a slightly longer route as long as we focus on things that make a real difference. If a RM50 billion project ends up using all foreign workers, how much *job creation* actually reaches the people? That is *artificial*. Although this modern agriculture initiative is not very *sexy* and has no giant announcements, it builds *job creation* at the right wage level.

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** With this approach of yours, YB, how long will it take before the people can feel the effect of this real *job creation*?

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is where perhaps Prof thinks I am a *libertarian*, right? Hahaha.

I don't think the government's job is to provide everything—it's not sustainable. But the government's job is to give focus, policy signaling, and create the impetus for something good to happen. For the poor, we provide *social protection*. For *job creation* and industry, it is important to provide infrastructure and initial investment to reduce feasibility risks (*feasibility*).

For modern agriculture under the Ministry of Economy, our target by the year 2026 is to create 20,000 job opportunities. Once the ecosystem, service providers, and *value chain* are formed, it will *lower down the barrier to entry cost* for the private sector.

Right now, if you ask the private sector to do modern agriculture, they say it's not profitable. But when the government starts with these initial 20,000 jobs, it becomes a catalyst (*catalyst*) for the private sector to enter. Once we reach an *inflection point* showing that it is *commercially doable*, the industry will grow on its own.

It is just like the palm oil industry in the past through Felda in the 60s and 70s. Now, the majority of it is led by the private sector. The government's job is to lower costs and initial risks, not to manage the entire economy along the way. Once it *scales up*, the entry cost barrier comes down and it is easy for people to get in.

**Prof. Emeritus Dr. Barjoyai Bardai:** I like the idea of making agriculture a viable industry, especially when linked to food security (*food security*). It's just that from the outside, we see this as being too slow. We expect something more aggressive.

For example, recently we heard that Boustead Plantations is up for sale. To me, it is quite awkward that they want to sell almost 200,000 acres of land at a price of RM2.3 billion—which is about RM17,000 per acre for *prime* land. The government doesn't need to shell out money; instead, it just needs to launch an acquisition through *crowdfunding*.

Say there are 200,000 acres, we invite 2 million young people to contribute RM2,000 each, and we already have RM4 billion. This public-listed company will be owned by this group. We build 2 million houses on a portion of that land, and the remainder is used for modern agriculture projects—vegetables, goats, cattle, and fish.

In Selangor itself, there are still 120,000 hectares of plantations. A developed state like Selangor should not have plantations of that size anymore; it should be converted into these modern agriculture projects. I am also looking forward to the *rendering machine* that will be installed at UiTM as an interesting model. All of this can be done through *crowdfunding*.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I have some *reservations*, Prof. In Boustead's case, the situation is quite unique because they have to pay off their debts. If we take the *crowdfunding* model for the public to participate, I'm not sure if we can achieve it.

But I understand the concept—which is to involve more people so that they have *skin in the game* and commitment, rather than getting everything for free.

I really agree with that concept of commitment. Modern agriculture is indeed *my pet project*, even though people get angry saying I am not the Minister of Agriculture. But I see modern agriculture as an economic activity that is highly strategic for the long term.

After a year of working on it, I am on a *learning curve*. I now understand why modern agriculture is difficult—it is easier to build a factory than to do modern agriculture. First, the land issue. Finding land is extremely difficult unless it is government land or has to be bought at the price of tens of millions. Ordinary people do not have that capital. Wealthy people, if given the choice, are afraid of the perceived high risks of agriculture. We cannot blame the public because the attempts at modern agriculture 10-15 years ago mostly failed terribly and lost hundreds of millions of ringgit.

That's why the fastest way is to use government land. Even this year, we only got around 1,000-plus acres; it's not easy. So building pilot projects to achieve a certain scale is very important. When the project economics are proven, only then will investment interest rise. If Prof had asked me to invest in agriculture before this, I wouldn't have wanted to either because I didn't see a proven *business case*.

So far, the IPR pilot projects prove that it is *viable*. We set a monthly income *floor* of at least RM2,000. From several harvests at the current sites, the yields can reach up to RM28,000 for 3 months for a single participant—which is around RM9,000 a month.

It is *doable* because we control the business risk—from land clearing, modern infrastructure investment, up to having a guaranteed buyer (*off-taker*). When we can prove it on a certain scale, only then will the public believe it can be done. We will certainly continue...

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, the criticism that it is too slow, *I'm trying my best to get it*. That's why I was the one who applied to participate in this IPR [Inisiatif Pendapatan Rakyat].

**Host:** Now, give us a quick *update* YB, how is this IPR doing? Is it well-received?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The response is good. There are tens of thousands of people waiting.

**Host:** Does everyone get it?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Not everyone gets it because it depends on the land. So for each site, the *size* is between—if in the Peninsula—between 10 to 20 hectares for about 20 to 40 recipients. Those waiting number in the tens of thousands. But even to get 10 to 20 hectares, we break a sweat trying to find 20 hectares of government land. If in Sabah and Sarawak, it is easier. Sabah, for example, one of its sites can reach up to 200 hectares and so on, right. *But, you know, we will continue to* go look for land *because* to open an IPR site, we have to go meet with the department that owns the land. So we look—like near Hospital Bahagia, Tanjung Rambutan, a big hospital, they have land that isn't doing anything. So we really have to go knock on doors and say we want to take it, we want to borrow that land.

*So, I previously thought that one direction would be done. It doesn't work like that. Even though we have said all this, you have to go down and ask. And the team at the Ministry of Economy that is working on this internally, there are at least 5 to 6 people, so we have to be fair to them as well. The problem is in terms of expectations of how it can go. But at least the business case* has been proven. It can happen.

**Host:** Anything more about PADU, don't you want to criticize? I want to ask one thing about the tourism industry. Recently, we have been hearing a lot...

**Professor:** *With the vacationers, they didn't want to go to Langkawi, they went to Thailand, to Hat Yai. And I went to Hat Yai too. I saw what was in Hat Yai. I saw that there was actually nothing much. They said to go there to eat, but to me, eating there is not even delicious.* So I compared it to Langkawi. Langkawi has much more, all kinds of things. But why don't they come to Langkawi? To me, this is my analysis. *Because they don't like the sellers in Langkawi and the hotel workers in Langkawi. They don't respect the tourists. They are arrogant—well, not arrogant, but I think they are not trained. YB has to give training, have to open a training center. Why is this happening? Actually, the average salary of these tourism workers is RM1,200.*

**Host:** So that is lower than the minimum wage, does that mean? So where do they work?

**Professor:** *As long as they work, they feel they will get enough salary. So, if we compare with what is in Thailand, we can see that the people of Thailand are soft and respectful. That's interesting.* I went to Indonesia, to Singapore, and to Batam. Batam actually has been developed well. Previously, the area was quite neglected, but now *it's full of beautiful houses and beautiful restaurants. Now people are shopping there, and the shopping experience is different from other places.* So why is it interesting? *Because they are the ones who are welcoming and engaging with the tourists. We in Langkawi, our tourism workers are not engaging enough. We have to be angry with the people of Langkawi.* That's all. If you agree with this comment of mine, I say it is indeed justified that we get scolded.

**Rafizi Ramli:** *It's hard to answer. How about training? I think the customer service needs to be better, so maybe we can tackle it as training. We don't have the exact data to explain why, but I think the view that the Prof gave is repeated by many of us.*

One issue is that it is expensive. *Because if I ask the kids who go to Hat Yai, they actually think that if they have RM400 to RM500, they can cover two nights, travel, and everything else. Then they go to eat, the price is lower, the sellers smile, and so on. The situation in Langkawi is more expensive, and the price is definitely higher than RM400 for two nights.* So the price factor and other factors make it less attractive.

I think it has various issues that need to be addressed by the government, by industry players, and so on. One is *positioning*. All this while—I might sound a bit harsh, I've only been to Langkawi once or twice, right—I think our *positioning* from the start for Langkawi was towards the *high-end*. *And our target in the past was Arab tourists and so on. 20 to 30 years ago, Langkawi was modern and luxurious for Arab tourists.
