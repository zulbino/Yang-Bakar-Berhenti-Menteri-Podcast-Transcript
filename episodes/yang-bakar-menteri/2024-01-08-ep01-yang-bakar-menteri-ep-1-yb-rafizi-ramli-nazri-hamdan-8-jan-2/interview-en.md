It's not like expenses or payments are consistent every year. There are debts taken out 7 years ago. I'm not sure, maybe many people—Brother Nazri can do a podcast on this later—government debt usually isn't like a mortgage. So I have to admit, actually, if you ask me how much it is this month or how much in bonds, I would actually have to ask MOF to get the details. **Nazri Hamdan:** And after that, it will be audited as well?

**Rafizi Ramli:** After that it will be audited, because that is audited before being released. That's why if asked right now what the overall debt is or looking at all the bonds, I think it does help, it does give an indication, true. But at the same time, it also needs to be viewed over a longer timeframe. That's why the trajectory is important—what we call the deficit glide must happen. Then, year after year, we can see whether the budget, for example the one to be presented for 2025, will have a more definitive number for 2023 and projections for 2024. Then, if it stays within the band of the budget, at least we can take comfort that everything is proceeding as we plan.

**Nazri Hamdan:** So can we hope that at least with what the government is doing today—reducing the deficit, which we understand—will there come a point or a year in the near future where we will succeed in at least achieving a surplus again?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It is actually doable within... on that question, Nazri, that's why I'm not committing to a surplus. For the first time, we will start to feel government debt—which has been going up—plateau and begin to decline. But if our sole target is to cut the deficit until it becomes a surplus, that could easily be done by 2026. However, it means we would have to slash development expenditure. So on that basis, achieving a surplus budget beyond 2028 is actually possible, barring any major economic crisis—like another COVID—the glide will allow for it.

**Nazri Hamdan:** Later we will develop RMK-13 (13th Malaysia Plan), covering spending from 2026 to 2030. Under RMK-13, may I ask—now that we have reached, I think, RM90 billion in development expenditure, will it be increased further in the 13th Malaysia Plan?

So from that perspective, if you ask me, of course our tendency is to continue increasing development expenditure. And if you look at the Fiscal Responsibility Act passed in Parliament, the government's commitment is at least 3% of GDP for development expenditure every year.

At least 3%. So we have to look at our GDP at that time, and in terms of choosing whether to reduce the deficit or show a budget surplus, we cannot cut development spending below that commitment. So there are many parameters here that we have to navigate.

**Nazri Hamdan:** As I see it, our development expenditure is almost equal to the subsidy allocation. So it's not good for the country.

**Najib Bakar:** So that's how it is, it needs to be balanced. Are you satisfied with YB's answer just now?

**Nazri Hamdan:** So those are the kinds of things that we on the outside don't understand. So at least we get a picture of how it works. To me, it's shocking. I didn't expect that out of RM90 billion, only 50-plus percent is channelled to basic DE.

...because we inherited the problem, we don't want to do anything or we don't answer directly because "it's not my problem"—that's not allowed. I think we inherit the problem, it's normal. We inherit the problem, but the target and the things we have to do—that's why we have to target all the subsidies, all the tiring things.

**Najib Bakar:** On subsidies, we'll get to that in a moment. But I think there are a few questions Brother Nazri wants to ask.

**Nazri Hamdan:** Let's step away slightly from this debt topic. I want to ask about GST, YB. Back when GST was introduced, when YB was in opposition, I remember there was a campaign rejecting GST. Correct me if I'm wrong, YB was among the vocal ones, and I read your articles. I followed YB back then, reading carefully why we should reject GST. So we reverted in 2018, returning to SST. Correct me again if I'm wrong, but we are the only country in the world that did that.

Now, today, we hear the Unity Government saying we are open to implementing GST. So, it means that GST is not wrong. It means that GST is correct if we implement it. So, what is the difference between implementing GST in the past and now? The government, the coalition said, "Eh, it's possible." What is the difference between implementing it then and implementing it now? Is it just a political weapon?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Right, so that's the question. I will answer this question with a long response. So, I'll take a breath.

First, I think we need to look academically at why generally, in theory, many economists feel that GST is effective. GST effectiveness depends on the standing and level of development of a country. I mean, let me get to the philosophy of GST first before we get to that—what types of countries are suited for GST? This is what people answer: more than 100 countries in the world use GST or VAT. One category is countries whose economies are not yet mature and most of their people cannot pay taxes because they live hand to mouth. So there is no concept of assessed income, because there are not many people in the formal sector and their businesses are not mature. But the government has to collect taxes. So what is the best way? We do GST, so that everyone, because the socio-economic level is more or less the same, when you implement GST, almost everyone bears roughly the same tax burden. Because there isn't much of a tax base. So you have the poorest countries, many of which use GST. Okay.

On the other extreme of GST implementation is a country that is already advanced. So in terms of taxation system, the majority is already in the formal sector. They have payroll, they have already paid income tax, or their business is already in the tax system. So how can the government ensure the tax rate is more orderly and efficient without overburdening a huge bureaucracy? The problem with income tax is that because it's based on assessment, there's a lot of risk of tax evasion, tax avoidance. So for those countries—and that's why many advanced countries have GST—they use GST because the assessment and the bureaucracy to collect the tax will lower income tax or corporate tax and raise GST, but the actual tax burden is equitable. Malaysia's problem is that we are right in the middle. Meaning we have 40% who are still in the informal sector. They haven't been paying income taxes before, right? So the first philosophical question we need to answer: by implementing GST, are we being fair, or are we able to ensure that the tax burden remains the same before and after GST for every family? Now, in 2013, 2014, when this GST issue arose...

...our fuel subsidy was in between RM5 to RM8 billion, it wasn't RM80 billion like now. Furthermore, the backdrop was that we were facing many governance issues prior to that. And we also know that in practice, when a government implements GST starting at a certain rate, no one reduces the rate—it always goes up. It starts at 6%, then it's easy for the government if they want to cover the budget slightly to raise it to 8%, then 10%. That's how it works all over the world in reality. That's why with GST... people always say in their perception, "Okay, if there's an economic recession, the government can lower GST, it's adjustable," but in practice, who ever lowers GST? That's why for countries that started below 10% in the 60s, by now it has become 25%, 30%. That is also a dicey matter when it comes to Malaysia, because our wage levels are actually low. And we know that by now. That's beyond argument, we know our wage levels are low, right? We don't have a mechanism to impose GST and lower the income tax rate like other countries, which would benefit most families, because they aren't paying income tax anyway as their income is already low. So when we look at 2014, 2015 for example...

...we were not burdened with subsidies like now. You were talking about 5 to 8 billion subsidies, in a situation where we were facing the problem of 1MDB—the biggest hole we wanted to close—and also in a situation that I think conclusively we can say...

**Nazri Hamdan:** Wait a minute, this year we have to pay 1MDB debt?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, this year. We still have to pay 1MDB debt this year, right? So in 2015, with that backdrop, we can conclusively say that due to the national wage levels and household incomes, when we implement GST, in terms of equitable taxation, we shift the tax burden onto families who shouldn't be paying tax. Because there were other things that could be done to strengthen our revenue. And actually, from 2015 to 2022 now, there are foundational facts we must consider regarding whether to do GST or not. What is our issue today that brings up the GST conversation again?

One is that in terms of tax collection, tax revenue as a percentage of GDP, we are among the third or fourth lowest in Southeast Asia. Our tax revenue as a percentage of GDP is between 11.3 to 11.7%.

**Nazri Hamdan:** On a macro level, yes.

**Rafizi Ramli:** So that, and two, economists will say, "Look, your tax revenue as a percentage of your GDP is actually very low. You should push it up to 12%, 13%." And obviously, the easiest way is to collect taxes from everyone, which is consumption tax. Because of that, this thing is repeated over and over again and becomes a talking point, especially to analysts, the market, and also to economists. We need to examine whether that is the only way for us to strengthen our fiscal position.

When I say we are open, it means I think we'd rather be agnostic about whatever ways to strengthen our tax revenue. But it doesn't happen in isolation. GST is not a silver bullet. You have to fix all the other things, and we will be guided by several main principles. First, we really have to be careful about equity: will we move that burden to the poorer people? That is, I think, our first guide. Secondly, we also have to look at dollars and cents. For example, GST before 2018, the estimate was RM41 billion, but that didn't include the tax refunds of roughly RM19 billion. So if you take the net GST collection after refunds, it was around RM30 billion on average every year.

**Nazri Hamdan:** Roughly the same as SST.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Which is roughly the same.

**Nazri Hamdan:** Oh, SST is slightly higher now.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Slightly higher now, right? Collected from all citizens. That's why so far, if you look at the government's stance, we will try our best to avoid placing this tax burden on every single citizen. If we can target the rich first—whether through high-value goods tax, capital gains tax, savings, subsidy retargeting—we'll do that for our fiscal strength. But at the same time, we don't want to politicize to the point that we are closed, for example saying "this is completely off the table". That's why when asked, I say we are agnostic. But we will be guided by all these principles, and so far the reason GST has not been implemented up to now is because we feel there are other ways to strengthen the country's fiscal position without overburdening the people.

However, at some point, for example if public wages rise, more people enter the formal sector, and more people pay income taxes—if at that point we have crossed the threshold where implementing GST does not violate the principle of equity, meaning the net effect is the same whether a family pays through income tax or through GST, the net is identical—we are open at that time. That's why I don't rule it out, because we aren't anti-GST. We want to implement GST according to these guidelines so that it aligns with the country's economic standing without being unfair to the people. Without retargeting subsidies, without controlling our debts, without tightening our governance, without collecting capital gains tax from private limited companies that make RM20-30 million profit in a single flip without paying tax, why would we tax ordinary families? So I think that's the background that illustrates what our true stance on GST is.

**Nazri Hamdan:** So basically, YB, you are saying that right now the direction is you are going to tax the rich more, and transfer the burden and take the money to the lower class. When there is a structural adjustment, then the government is willing to do GST?

**Rafizi Ramli:** What it means is that the overarching principle is that it has to maintain equity. Meaning it cannot be regressive; it must be progressive. Any tax cannot be regressive, taxing the poor more than the rich. And we must first focus on ensuring whatever we explore in national taxation is progressive. Once we feel that is settled, then we can consider any form of taxation, including GST which affects everyone. But the principle that taxation has to be as progressive as possible, that has to be maintained. And comparing that, when GST was first introduced in 2013, 2014, 2015, it certainly was regressive, and it shifted that burden onto the poor. What we are doing so far is trying our best to close existing loopholes to ensure that wherever has not yet been taxed, especially high-income groups or corporations, we tax those first for our tax revenue. This GST matter, I think, can only be considered when we are confident that more of our families have higher wages, higher incomes, that when we implement GST, if we implement GST, they will get the benefit from tax reduction. So the difference is tax is collected; it's not an additional burden of tax to them.

**Najib Bakar:** Okay, that's on GST. But we know people will resurrect old news of YB talking about GST and such. People always say... about lowering car prices. So what is YB's stance on that?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It's the same thing; we have to look at the position and context at that time, right? We have opportunities based on our financial situation from time to time to implement certain things. In 2010-2011, we didn't have a lot of expenses, deficits, and debts like now. So our fiscal consolidation was not a big issue then. Oil subsidies were not as big as what we have now. And at that time—I can't recall off the top of my head—our budget then wasn't even RM200 billion. Our total budget was not up to RM200 billion in 2010-2011. And we knew that car loans were a major monthly household expense. First is mortgage or rent, then car loans, right? And we also know that our excise tax is high: 85%, 100%, and so on. And I think in 2010, 2011, if I can recall, we collected around RM11 billion every year from our excise tax. At that time, if we started reducing excise tax and focused on other revenue sources when our income sources and financial position weren't as acute as now, it could be done—provided we manage our expenses and find new revenue streams. We also weren't spending on 1MDB and incurring all sorts of debts.

Now it's 2024. With all the additional debt, with the situation from the point of view of tax revenue as a percentage of GDP, in the situation of our subsidy expenses which have increased by 10 times—if we want to reform our excise duty on cars, which is 85%, 100%, it will take away easily RM10 billion from our national income. In a situation where all these expenditures are locked in. If we take away RM10 billion, sure, we can reduce the price of cars, but we have to cut other things. Subsidies used to be RM5-6 billion, now RM80 billion, so we have to cut it. That's why I feel the question of the price of the car and so on, now it has become water under the bridge. Because matters we could have restructured earlier, because we didn't do it then, now carry additional burdens. So that decision is difficult to make under current financial constraints. That's one.

Second, regarding cars, going forward we will face an economic landscape where carbon emission compliance becomes more critical. We will also face economic conditions more suited to encouraging a transition away from internal combustion engines, for example to electric vehicles. People ask, "Why are you so busy with EVs? This is for the rich," and so on. It's not just about carbon emissions or green compliance or green economy; it's about the time when we will start importing petrol. So if we do it now, encouraging the purchase of as many cheap cars as possible that surges petrol consumption, we will face another problem. That's why when comparing something from 10-12 years ago—whose assessment was based on projections at that time—it's difficult to compare directly. Hence the reason why when something for the benefit of the people can be done immediately, don't delay it. Because the context, parameters, and environment change from one era to another. If we aren't careful, obviously we miss the opportunity.

So if people ask me now, suppose we have plenty of money, we already have a budget surplus, we can reduce the cost of excise duty—even if we already have the financial ability, we still have to look at the basis that will cause the fast purchase of ICE cars that use petrol. Is it sustainable for our economy? Because while the people can get cheap cars, we will quickly start importing petrol, which will create new issues in terms of petrol consumption and so forth. So, in the current situation, we have to assess based on the current needs and the future trajectory, which is of course very different from 2010-2011. Is that okay? Are you disappointed?

**Nazri Hamdan:** Disappointed all the time. Actually, I'm disappointed not because of anything else, but because I didn't get a clear answer. Expecting that... okay, going back to GST earlier. Many people expect GST to return, but when YB speaks like this, I don't know when GST will actually come back.

So, at the end of the day, of course, I agree with what YB said—GST is a regressive tax. But at the end of the day, we are still there, and we are not committing. I mean, like YB committed on PADU—okay, it's supposed to be done on January 1st, and I respect YB because on January 1st, it started. Why can't we do the same thing with GST? Okay, we want to correct all these structures, and then commit to a specific year for GST. Why not like that? Isn't that to give a good signal to investors abroad, to show that we really want to improve our fiscal position? Isn't that good? Why can't the Unity Government commit, "I want to do GST in this year"? Why?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Because it goes back to what we discussed earlier. Other than the principle issue of regressive vs. equitable, the dollars and cents must also be computed.

So, for example, SST has exceeded the net income from GST when it was implemented up to 2018. When we implement GST, it will have an effect on inflation and many other things. So compared to PADU, for instance—we'll get into PADU later—the case for PADU, digitalization, and consolidated data for the government is overwhelming. Meaning, the difference between pros and cons is vast. So we know that whatever our challenges, we can bite the bullet. But with GST versus SST, the dollars and cents—where we can update existing systems, whether direct tax or indirect tax, to yield the same revenue—because GST is not a silver bullet. Unless we take the view that whatever the cost to the economy in terms of regressive impact, inflation, price increases, GST is overwhelmingly the silver bullet because it will surge collection without a big impact. You and I know that's not the case.

So, for example, if we look at the SST chart and it's far behind GST, then I would have been more affirmative about GST. But now, the dollars and cents, we have to weigh it. Logically, because everyone has to pay GST, tax collection should go up. But we've had three years of GST, and GST also involves refunds. So we can't just look at gross collection; we have to look at net income after refunds. As far as the net income after refund is concerned, it is not much different from SST. So that is the debate. We are trying our best to make sure that any taxation regime in Malaysia is not regressive. At this time, GST will still be regressive until the wages of the people start to reach a certain level.

**Nazri Hamdan:** But in the current government, the government said they want to do cash transfers and rationalize subsidies. Isn't that one step where the government moves the money to the lower classes? Of course it is regressive, but at the same time you're also transferring and assisting the B40. Why do you have to wait for this? Once you collect this, you just transfer—make a cash transfer. Why not?

**Rafizi Ramli:** One is that we have to balance with the inflation impact, and that is a dicey point. **Interviewer:** Before we move on to PADU, I want to ask YB one last question. Last year, was hardcore poverty eradicated?

**Rafizi Ramli:** When the Right Honourable Prime Minister announced that we want to target zero hardcore poor, eradicating hardcore poverty means zero hardcore poverty. That concept never existed in our country's vocabulary before. The issue of eradicating hardcore poverty was never there, so we referred to international practices and established a policy. The policy is as follows: we take the country's official hardcore poverty rate. In 2023, it was 0.2%, which is equivalent to around 18,400 hardcore poor households.

Before PADU, the data was still not as good as we wanted it to be. The government policy regarding eradicating hardcore poverty or zero hardcore poverty defines zero hardcore poverty as meaning that every year, a number of families equivalent to the total number of hardcore poor households according to national statistics have been provided assistance and lifted out of the hardcore poverty line. This is to ensure the government continues to focus on a specific number of families so that every year we lift that many families out of hardcore poverty, because we know hardcore poverty will continue to recur. Without this zero hardcore poverty policy, there would be no truly integrated focus on a specific target.

On that basis, the number of families assisted to exit hardcore poverty certainly exceeded 18,400 households when we look at data covering all government system aid from Amanah Ikhtiar, STR, zakat, and so on.
