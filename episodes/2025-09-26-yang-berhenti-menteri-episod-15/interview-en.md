**Host:** Now we enter the next segment. A slightly heavier segment, YB. Regarding the implementation of the RON95 subsidy adjustment through the BUDI95 program, where a 300-liter monthly quota is given to everyone regardless of income limit. What is your view if global crude oil prices spike and the Ringgit exchange rate weakens in the future?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Previously, discussions at the government level and the consensus among economists were for us to shift from a bulk subsidy to a targeted subsidy based on income thresholds. A bulk subsidy given to everyone regardless of income exposes the government to substantial financial risk.

The Finance Minister II stated that savings are estimated at between RM2.5 and RM4 billion annually based on current oil prices of around USD 67–68 per barrel and an exchange rate of RM4.21–4.22 to the USD. Under the Automatic Pricing Mechanism (APM) formula, for every USD 1 increase in global crude oil prices, the price of RON95 should rise by about 3 to 4 sen.

For comparison, in 2023, the average global crude oil price was above USD 80 per barrel, and the Budget 2023/2024 range projected USD 85 per barrel—a USD 20 difference compared to now. If oil prices rise by USD 20, the cost difference is around 60 sen per liter. Right now, the actual market price of RON95 without subsidy is around RM2.60 to RM2.70 per liter, meaning the government absorbs a subsidy of about 60 to 65 sen per liter to set the price at RM1.99 per liter for that 300-liter quota.

Imagine if oil prices rise to USD 80 per barrel or the Ringgit weakens to RM4.40. The subsidy the government has to absorb would jump from 65 sen per liter to RM1.30–RM1.40 per liter. When this happens, the annual subsidy bill will surge past RM20 billion again—returning to original levels before targeting was introduced. The targeted savings of RM2.5 to RM4 billion would be wiped out instantly.

The two main goals of retargeting the RON95 subsidy are:
1. Strengthening the country's fiscal position to secure RM8 to RM10 billion in savings annually.
2. Reducing the government's exposure to global crude oil price volatility.

If this conditional 300-liter bulk method remains, the goal of insulating the government from global oil price volatility will not be achieved. Even worse, if the subsidy bill expands again and the government is forced to raise fuel prices from RM1.99 to RM2.30 in the future, the public will question why they had to go through the trouble of using their IC if prices still end up going up.

There were also statements from the Ministry of Finance mentioning that this is only phase one, and subsequent phases might withdraw eligibility for the T15 group. If done in such phased stages—given first and then taken back—it will generate continuous polemics and controversy with the public.

That is why my view from the start was to "bite the bullet." Do it properly according to income eligibility. The government's duty is to make tough but correct decisions. As long as we explain it well, plug leakages, and provide direct assistance to those eligible, the public can judge. My responsibility as a Member of Parliament is to remind the government to be cautious so that the original problem does not recur when global oil prices spike.

**Host:** A follow-up question, YB. You strongly advocate for the cash transfer method. Why was the Cabinet hesitant to implement cash transfers—was it out of fear of price hikes and inflation?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The majority voice in the Cabinet worried that implementing cash transfers would cause price hikes and make the public angry. Second, there was a political concern that cash handouts do not guarantee public political support, as seen with BR1M in the past.

However, from an economic perspective and international experience, research shows that cash transfers are the best and most efficient method to deliver subsidies. Regarding the inflationary impact, simulations were conducted. A phased price increase does have an initial impact on inflation, but according to projections we prepared earlier, by 2026–2027, inflation would be brought back under control.

Compare that to the current method: the RM1.99 price is capped at around RM700 of fuel expenditure per month. Small traders who travel frequently and use more than RM700 worth of fuel monthly will still feel the increase in transportation costs. This could also serve as an excuse for traders to raise goods prices. So, whichever way you turn, inflationary impact will exist. That is why we must be guided by the primary policy objective—reducing the nation's exposure to crude oil price volatility and ensuring subsidies are targeted specifically to low-income groups who truly qualify.

**Host:** Given that this policy has been announced and will be implemented using identity cards at petrol stations, how do you view the enforcement challenges, risk of leakages, and ground-level service execution?

**Rafizi Ramli:** There are two main issues: operational smoothness at petrol stations and the risk of leakages.

In the Klang Valley, testing might run smoothly because many users regularly use cards or digital transactions; furthermore, internet and fiber optic coverage here is excellent. But in rural and remote areas, internet connectivity can be spotty. Moreover, many rural residents have never used their MyKad IC chips for such transactions. If an IC chip is damaged when trying to buy fuel, they will be forced to pay full market price. That is a frustrating experience for citizens. If hundreds of users face damaged chip issues or station system outages daily, it will generate continuous public outcry.

Regarding leakages, the main challenge involves small traders operating vehicles like pickup trucks. Experience during the diesel subsidy targeting showed night market traders were impacted because the quota provided was insufficient, or vehicles were registered under other individuals belonging to different income brackets. The risk of leakages and the implications for small traders must be closely monitored over the next 6 to 12 months.

**Host:** Turning to social issues. Regarding the statement by the Kelantan Police Chief on statutory rape / underage sexual offense cases, controversy arose regarding the proposal to take action against both parties if the act was committed on a mutual consent basis. What is your view?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Many responses issued refer to statutory rape generally—that is, sexual relations with an underage individual. Universally, statutory rape applies even if there is consent from the minor because they lack sufficient maturity and are vulnerable to manipulation. If an adult engages in relations with a minor, that adult is charged with rape. I don't think the Kelantan Police Chief or any sane person suggested punishing underage children who are victims of adult exploitation.

However, there is another category that is less honestly discussed: cases where both partners are underage teenagers, for example, 16-year-olds who are dating and engage in consensual relations. When the relationship is discovered by families and a police report is filed, the 16-year-old boy is charged with statutory rape.

My own experience dealing with IPD Ampang Jaya in the Pandan constituency in 2023 showed a high rate of statutory rape cases. Upon checking with the police, it turned out the majority of cases in that area were not adult victimization, but consensual relationships between underage teenage couples. Yet legally, the police had no choice but to prosecute the teenage boy.

The statement by the Minister of Women, Family and Community Development, Datuk Seri Nancy Shukri, provided clarity that charged minors remain subject to the Child Act, where sentencing and rehabilitation differ (such as placement in a Henry Gurney School). For the families of the teenage boys involved, they feel the situation is unfair because both were minors and engaged in the act together.

Several NGOs have suggested guidelines or a special age clause ("Romeo and Juliet clause") to handle consensual teenage couple cases through counseling rather than criminal prosecution. However, in our societal context, extramarital relations are a moral and religious offense, making absolute leniency hard to accept.

PDRM should present detailed statistics: how many handled statutory rape cases actually fall into the consensual teenage category. We cannot remain in denial about youth behavior today being different from past generations due to internet exposure. Society needs to be more open to discussing "consent" and reviewing the legal framework to align with current realities.

**Host:** Regarding the Sabah mining scandal and the release of alleged corruption videos, MACC stated the videos were edited and the complainant refused to cooperate without guaranteed whistleblower protection. What is your view on public perception and legal procedure in this case?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Regarding the Sabah mining scandal, my view matches that of ordinary citizens. First, I am not surprised. Second, that is the reality of money politics deeply rooted in Malaysia.

However, to be fair from a legal perspective, MACC's statement has merit. Primary evidence like video footage must be authentic and unedited to establish legal validity. We do not want anyone to use video editing technology or AI to slander and frame political opponents.

Second, regarding the Whistleblower Protection Act: legal protection is granted only to whistleblowers who are clean of the crime, not individuals who actively participated in giving bribes. The current issue is a crisis of confidence and public perception. This trust crisis cannot be resolved merely through media statements; it requires transparent, impartial enforcement regardless of the political standing of the parties involved.

**Host:** All right YB. Moving into our "FUYYOH!" segment, which highlights new innovations, science, and technology. This week we have the commercialization of Sodium-ion batteries on a large scale. How do you view this technology?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Most batteries for electric vehicles (EVs) and gadgets today use Lithium-ion. The main drawback of Lithium is that it is a costly, geographically limited rare material. Due to the high cost of Lithium, the transition to green energy and clean technology becomes quite expensive.

Sodium-ion is the primary alternative because Sodium is derived from salt—an abundant resource globally. The primary challenge with Sodium-ion previously was its lower energy density compared to Lithium-ion (about half the energy storage capacity of Lithium per kilogram). So if used in cars, the battery size becomes excessively large and heavy.

However, recent breakthroughs by companies like EVE Energy in China—successfully developing a commercial, large-scale Sodium-ion based Battery Energy Storage System (BESS)—represent a complete game changer.

For Malaysia, this is crucial for the National Energy Transition Roadmap (NETR). Why was solar energy implementation slow in Malaysia previously? It wasn't a lack of sunlight potential or expensive solar panels—PV solar panels keep getting cheaper. The main challenge was power grid stabilization and energy storage. Solar only generates peak power for about 4–5 hours a day. If that energy is fed into the grid simultaneously without a massive battery storage system, the grid can be disrupted.

To upgrade the national grid, we require extremely large BESS capacity. If Sodium-ion battery technology matures and becomes cheaper over the next 5 to 10 years, the cost of upgrading Malaysia's green energy grid will become significantly lower. At the household consumer level, solar storage battery costs currently reaching RM40,000–RM50,000 per unit will drop dramatically, allowing citizens to rely independently on their own solar power.

**Host:** Now we move to reader and viewer questions. First question from Ahmad Elias: Is the 39% decline in domestic tourists to Langkawi caused by a consumer protest against high service, accommodation, and food prices? What is the best step to revive tourism there?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Without official survey data, from a qualitative standpoint, I agree that cost is the dominant factor. Malaysians today consider traveling a lifestyle choice. When making comparisons, with a budget of RM1,000, domestic tourists can enjoy a comfortable holiday in Krabi or Southern Thailand complete with accessible, cheap halal food. In Langkawi, the Ringgit cannot stretch as far due to a high cost structure.

If accommodation, food, and service costs in Langkawi continue to be perceived as expensive compared to neighboring destinations, this decline will persist. The primary challenge for Langkawi operators and tourism authorities is designing tourism products that offer good value for money so citizens feel domestic holidays are worth their spend.

**Host:** Next question from Syazli: Given concerns over declining student motivation, is it appropriate to reinstate public examinations like UPSR and PT3/PMR?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The original premise of abolishing UPSR and PT3 was sound—to reduce reliance on an exam-driven education system (*rote learning*) that forces children to memorize drills and measures human capability solely through test scores.

Before making a hasty decision to bring back exams, we must identify the root cause. Is student motivation declining solely due to the absence of exams, or is it due to syllabus design, teaching methodology (pedagogy), and the learning environment?

Advanced foreign education systems emphasize context and real-world application first. For example, in Mathematics, students are taught why a formula exists and how it applies in daily life, rather than immediately being forced to solve algebraic equations mechanically. Similarly with History—it should be taught through engaging storytelling to understand geopolitical lessons, rather than memorizing dates and facts.

My stance is neutral. Many gaps remain in teaching methods, curriculum quality, and educational bureaucracy that must be fixed. If we rush to reinstate public exams without fixing these root causes, we will simply return to the old problem of producing uncreative memorizers.

**Host:** Question from Qurban: Is inflation in Malaysia structural because the majority of bank credit flows into real estate and consumption rather than productive capacity? Should credit creation controls (*M3 money supply*) be made the core anti-inflation strategy?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The view that banking credit flows more into speculative real estate distribution and consumption credit rather than productive manufacturing capacity holds truth. It is appropriate that national capital be directed toward manufacturing and productive investment to strengthen economic structure.

However, linking inflation in Malaysia directly to the M3 money supply is inaccurate. Although data shows the money supply has increased over recent years, correlation does not imply causation.

Inflation increases in Malaysia are primarily driven by the food basket. Our food inflation rose due to global factors—such as post-COVID supply chain shocks, climate change, and the war in Ukraine—which drove up imported commodity prices.

The core problem in Malaysia lies in local supply chain structures. For example, farm-gate (*ex-farm*) agricultural prices might be RM5, but by the time products reach consumers, the price becomes RM15 due to layers of transport costs, wholesaler margins, rent, and labor. Rather than raising interest rates or limiting credit creation—which could hurt consumer borrowing—it is far more effective for the government to focus efforts on streamlining food supply chains to narrow the farm-to-market price gap.

**Host:** Final question: What is the 5-year direction for the Malaysian film industry? How can we replicate the success of South Korea, India (Kollywood/Bollywood), or the UK in producing quality works, and how can we ensure agencies like FINAS are managed based on merit and transparent KPIs?

**Rafizi Ramli:** During my time at the Ministry of Economy, the creative industry (or orange economy) was among the sectors studied in the National Economic Action Council. Regarding KPIs for agencies like FINAS and MDEC, that information is actually presented annually in Parliament Budget Documents, though rarely scrutinized by the public.

However, we must address the core problem of the local film industry. When comparing local films to works from South Korea, Kollywood, Poland, or Hollywood on streaming platforms, the primary difference isn't camera equipment or cinematography, but the quality of storytelling and scriptwriting.

Kollywood films like *Mandela* (starring Yogi Babu), for example, take everyday life themes or local politics and translate them into deeply compelling, moving narratives without requiring dance sequences or massive fantasy budgets.

In Malaysia, our main weakness is ideas and script quality. Lacking deep story concepts, local filmmakers repeatedly fall back on the same safe formulas—such as ghost or gangster stories. Much emphasis in our industry is placed on superficial aspects like actor popularity or filming gear, whereas in developed nations, screenwriters and drama schools are accorded high priority.

The government cannot resolve all these issues bureaucratically. The real challenge is how we can discover and nurture creative writing talent among young people, and provide a supporting ecosystem so great narrative ideas can be translated into high-quality artistic works capable of competing internationally.
