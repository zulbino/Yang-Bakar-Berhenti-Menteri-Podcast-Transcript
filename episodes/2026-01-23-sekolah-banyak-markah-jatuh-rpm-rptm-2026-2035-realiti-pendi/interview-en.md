What hasn't been properly explained is that this Malaysia Education Plan (RPM) is a continuation of the 2010 Education Blueprint. One good thing introduced during the 2010–2025 Education Blueprint was benchmarking. Our country uses two primary methods to measure the quality and outcomes of the education system: the first is PISA (Programme for International Student Assessment). PISA is organized by the OECD and involves OECD member countries as well as partner countries, including Malaysia, Singapore, Japan, China, and Vietnam. PISA measures three key student skills: mathematics, reading, and science.

PISA doesn't assess all students; it uses a representative sample covering a spectrum of abilities—from high-achieving to average students. From that sample, we get benchmarking figures to determine whether the skills of a 10-year-old student in Malaysia are comparable to a 10-year-old student in Singapore.

The latest 2022 PISA results highlight an important reality. The 2010–2025 Education Blueprint targeted Malaysian students' PISA achievement to reach the top 30% by 2025. However, in 2022, our performance fell below the OECD average in all three subjects. For reading skills, our score was very poor—a low 388. We lag far behind neighbors like Singapore (575 for Maths), Japan, Korea, and Taiwan. Even compared to Vietnam (Maths 469), we fall behind. Our students' reading literacy is not much different from Indonesia's and far lower than Vietnam's.

If we look at the 15-year trend since the Education Blueprint was introduced, our results haven't budged. When Malaysia first joined in 2009/2010, the reading score was 414. In 2012, it dropped to 398. In 2015, there was a jump to 431, but it was questioned by the OECD due to sampling issues—the sample wasn't representative. In the official OECD PISA report, they put that note: it's not comparable because of sampling difficulty. Before COVID-19, our scores remained roughly the same, around 414–415. After COVID-19, severe learning loss occurred. The impact equates to losing 4 years of learning time compared to Singaporean students of the same age. That shows how dire our situation is. So for Radzi Jidin, for instance, just because I often criticize Fadhlina doesn't mean Radzi Jidin was any better. Similarly with Maszlee Malik's era, there wasn't much difference.

Besides PISA, we have another indicator: TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study) for Grade 8 (14-year-olds), managed by the IEA. The 2019 TIMSS results were also severe, with the score gap against Singapore reaching 150 points.

This data reveals the true state of our benchmarking and quality. The 2010–2025 National Education Blueprint targeted a range of 500 to 520 (top 30%). The global average PISA score ranges between 470 and 490. Not only did we miss our target range, we are below average. We have to accept this fact. When I was Economy Minister, major discussions on boosting the economy and productivity all hinged on our human capital. And human capital starts in schools. With these numbers, we clearly have a major problem.

This is a management and planning issue, not a problem of having an insufficient total number of teachers.
