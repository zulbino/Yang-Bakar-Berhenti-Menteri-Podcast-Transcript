# Interview (English)

**Host:** Assalamualaikum and welcome to the *Yang Berhenti Menteri Podcast* episode 12. How is everyone doing? To those still tuned in with us, keep typing in the comment section. It's been a long time since I last met YB Rafizi. Since it's been so long, I want to ask an off-script question—how has your diet and weight loss journey been progressing since I left you?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That's a personal question, end of story! That wasn't in the script and wasn't discussed. But for someone who had a heart attack, *actually it's not bad*. Discipline is good; I managed to go for a jog before this podcast.

**Host:** Our format this time is quite new and more concise. We go straight to the first segment called "Beria Mat". The rules are simple: I show a picture, and YB comments on whether the picture is "beria" (over-the-top/doing too much) or not, along with a score from 1 to 5. First picture, what do you see, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** At this time I wasn't in Parliament, I only read media reports. This picture of Wan Fayhsal went viral briefly because of his proposal to change the name of the Rafflesia flower. If you ask if it's "beria", it definitely is. But I have a better suggestion. Instead of asking Parliament to change the name of the Rafflesia flower, isn't his party the Flower Party? He should bring a proposal in a Bersatu meeting to replace the Bersatu Bunga Raya logo with the Rafflesia flower. No need to bring it to Parliament.

**Host:** So that counts as quite over-the-top then?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Definitely over-the-top.

**Host:** Next, the second picture. What do you see, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I see eggs, the Menteri Besar of Selangor, and the Deputy President of PKR doing a gimmick going grocery shopping at a store. This is about YB Nurul Izzah asking PKR elected representatives to give RM100 for the SARA program. Her intention to ask them to give this RM100 to people in need is good, but if I were an ordinary citizen, I would say this matter could have been resolved from the beginning.

If from the start when planning this gift to the people the data had been neatly organized, rich people wouldn't need to get it. For the poor, B40 and M40, give RM200 or RM300 per person. Take it from the allocations of MPs and rich people. So Izzah wouldn't need to go over-the-top asking all MPs to go to stores to show paying using their ICs. People will just look at this as a gimmick.

**Host:** Third picture, did you notice it, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I noticed it. Once you're caught red-handed, you're caught. No need to twist things around. For a long time now, Facebook has shown edit history. It's so over-the-top trying to spin it as if it were manipulated, when the one in the wrong is the person who showed themselves editing their original comment.

This is the kind of attitude present in our society—in their head, everything wrong in this world is the fault of the Chinese. If his beard turns white, it's probably the Chinese's fault too. He went over-the-top trying to cover it up after getting caught. But I hope this serves as a lesson; don't do it again because netizens nowadays are diligent about checking edit history. This is double over-the-top.

**Host:** Next picture, did YB notice it?

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is the influencer who made a flotilla—a house on top, a car underneath. Then he moved on the sea and was suddenly arrested by the Terengganu Maritime Enforcement Agency. This was just to make content. It's worth it to attract attention and get YouTube payouts, but we have to be careful because now there is climate change.

Whether it's the Manjoi ADUN earlier or this influencer, we shouldn't become a society willing to do anything just for content. Two weeks ago, an English teacher was brought to court for creating content making up a story that the late Zara was put into a washing machine. When asked in Parliament, her answer was because she wanted to make content. This culture of going over-the-top to make content needs common sense and laws. Don't cause trouble for others, spread false stories, or add to the workload of enforcement officers.

**Host:** What about the scores, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I'm a bit stingy when it comes to scores. On a scale of 1 to 5, the highest score of 4 goes to this flotilla case. Second place is a tie at score 3 between Hafiz Sabri and Wan Fayhsal. Izzah and Amir get a score of 2 because their content invited all kinds of comments.

**Host:** We continue to the next segment, "Standing Order". What is YB's view on the use of Standing Orders in the Dewan Rakyat currently?

**Rafizi Ramli:** During my first term as opposition, I rarely stood up to invoke Standing Orders. In my view, if Standing Orders are used repeatedly, it wastes Parliament's time, whereas Parliament is a place to debate substantive matters. Nowadays, at every little thing people stand up claiming "misleading the house", especially from the opposition. The public watching will think our elected representatives get confused very easily.

**Host:** First Parliament issue: Previously while in government, YB supported and explained the benefits of the Urban Redevelopment Act (PSB/URA) Bill. Now many PKR MPs like YB Nik Nazmi, YB Rodziah, YB Wong Chen, and YB Lee Chean Chung are opposing it and asking for it to be postponed. Have you changed your stance?

**Rafizi Ramli:** No, this needs to be corrected. The views of PKR MPs and my views, as well as Nik Nazmi's, have not changed since we were in cabinet. The legislative process has two stages: policy decision and bill details.

On policy, do we need improvements to facilitate urban redevelopment? Yes, I support that. Our cities in Kuala Lumpur, Penang, Johor, and even opposition states are aging. Many low-cost flats built in the 70s through 90s do not meet current comfort specifications.

But *the devil is in the details*. Supporting the policy does not mean legitimate concerns should be brushed aside:
1. **The Question of Threshold:** The first condition is that the building's age must be 30 years. Does this mean a developer can enter any 30–40-year-old housing estate in Pandan, persuade 80% of residents, and use that to redevelop the area even if the remaining 20% disagree and the housing is not dilapidated? This is a legitimate question.
2. **Rights of Tenants vs. Owners:** In urban low-cost flats, at least 40% to 50% of residents are low-income tenants, not owners. Owners might live elsewhere and want a larger new unit to sell or rent out at a high price. When development begins, tenants have no rights and are forced out. Where will these poor families go? This is the gentrification dilemma.
3. **Ministerial Powers:** Are the minister's powers too vast in determining developers? Imagine if the government changes and a minister has absolute power to decide on billion-ringgit projects even if 20% of residents object—this exposes risks of corruption.

The opposition by PKR MPs is very different from the opposition side, which plays up racial issues claiming "the Chinese want to chase away the Malays". Many poor Chinese and Indians renting in Pandan flats face the same dilemma. PKR fights to defend the poor regardless of race. Kudos to the PKR backbenchers who dared to speak up constructively. Don't rush to pass it; take time to improve this Bill.

**Host:** Next, the Government Procurement Bill. Perikatan Nasional says this centralizes power under the Finance Minister, while the Second Finance Minister says it decentralizes power. Which one is correct?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The origin of this Government Procurement Bill is to address the issue of leakages. Every year the Auditor-General's Report reveals various problems, but nobody is brought to court or subjected to strict disciplinary action. The reason is that we do not have specific legislation on procurement accountability—we only have circulars and SOPs. Prosecuting requires proving elements of corruption under the MACC Act or the Penal Code, which is very difficult. So, this Procurement Bill is important policy-wise.

But I sympathize with the opposition and understand why they walked out that day. The legislative process has four stages: First Reading, Second Reading, Committee Stage, and Third Reading. The First Reading is the first time MPs see the full draft of the Bill. Ideally, before the Second Reading begins, the Bill should first be referred to the Parliamentary Special Select Committee (JKP/JJKP) on Finance to get feedback.

What happened was, this Bill was bulldozed—placed for First Reading just two days before Second Reading. There was no time to delve deep into this thick and important Bill. When the Speaker gave technical excuses that the JKP must write a letter first before being referred, it made it seem like we are not serious about institutional reform.

Pressuring backbencher MPs to vote immediately in a rush is unfair. We support the Procurement Bill, but the execution method made MPs feel like mere rubber stamps. Planning at the Ministry of Finance level should take 4 to 5 months, allow the JJKP time to review it, and only then debate it properly.

**Host:** Moving on to the "Life is Tough, Kid" segment. First issue: When citizens receive MySara aid, the application system goes down. Does this inconvenience the public, or what?

**Rafizi Ramli:** My view differs from the Ministry of Finance. I have long disagreed with the grand cash handout approach ("cash is king"). Assistance should be structural economic reform and targeted through social protection systems like PADU—directly entering the recipient's bank account every month based on eligibility.

When it is announced like a "gift" starting on a specific date (August 31), of course people will flock to stores. Furthermore, this MySara system uses a third party, the MyKasih Foundation, rather than a government-developed system. This raises questions about data privacy and system bandwidth capacity.

When the system goes down, the government's goal to gain political capital turns into public complaints. More worryingly, the Ministry of Finance plans to use MyKad/IC for the implementation of targeted RON95 subsidies later on. Imagine if the registration system or IC reader system goes down at petrol stations nationwide—it will cause severe congestion and ruin the customer experience. The lesson from this MySara issue should open the government's eyes to prioritize targeted cash transfers over physical execution using ICs at petrol stations.

**Host:** Next issue: The inquest proceedings into the death of the late Zara Eleena. Although expert pathologist testimony explained that the probability of the deceased falling or being pushed was low, the public still finds it hard to accept the facts and trusts TikTok narratives more. How do you view this, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is a very unfortunate thing for our society in the digital media era. When scientific facts and medical expert testimony in court are no longer believed, and instead society trusts TikTok rumors more, that is a dangerous trend.

Early on when this issue arose, I asked my friends in Papar. That school is an ordinary school far from the city, not an elite school for VIP children. So allegations that there was a VIP child being protected make no sense at all.

However, the main reason the public did not trust these proceedings is because the government lost control of the narrative due to delayed action. This issue was allowed to fester for two weeks without official clarification from KDN, PDRM, or KPM. False narratives became firmly entrenched in people's heads. In crisis management, the government should take preemptive and proactive steps to establish the true narrative within the first 24 hours, rather than blaming social media platforms once the fires of slander have already spread.

**Host:** Next issue: YB Azmin Ali predicts that PRU16 will happen in the near future. What is your comment, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don't know, maybe he made up with Anwar Ibrahim? [Laughs]. Just joking. Any opposition will build a narrative that Parliament will be dissolved early to show the government is weak and unstable, while ensuring their party machinery remains ready.

However, according to historical records and Prime Ministerial practice in Malaysia, a sitting Prime Minister in power will serve until the final day of the term. Official reports from government departments, Special Branch, and Military Intelligence usually state that the Prime Minister is popular. No agency will advise a Prime Minister to dissolve Parliament early. So, practically speaking, the assumption of an early PRU simply will not happen.

**Host:** Moving on to netizen questions. First question from Fikri Amiruddin: *"When YB raised the Tabung Haji issue before, some said YB was an instigator, but others saw it as check and balance. Which did more damage to Muslim confidence—YB's critical voice or PAS's silence at the time?"*

**Rafizi Ramli:** When I exposed the Tabung Haji case in 2015, I wasn't thinking about an image of check and balance or being an instigator. The financial facts showed that Tabung Haji's reserves at the time were at a negative level, there were problematic investments, and high hibah payouts violated regulations because finances were insufficient. As an MP with a financial background, it was my duty to tell the truth.

To close this issue transparently, the government should declassify and table the Royal Commission of Inquiry (RCI) Report on Tabung Haji. Today Tabung Haji faces a new crisis—savings are declining because the cost of performing Hajj is rising and the waitlist time upon registration reaches 100 years, causing young people to save less. Structural matters like these are what should be given attention.

**Host:** Second question from Aiman: *"Sarawak has only received RM49 billion since 1975, while 95% of oil and gas revenue goes to Petronas. Is this fair?"*

**Rafizi Ramli:** Saying 95% of oil revenue goes to Petronas is an inaccurate statement. Under the Production Sharing Contract (PSC), royalties are set at 5% to the Sarawak state government and 5% to the Federal Government.

The remaining 90% does not go into Petronas's pockets for free. Hundreds of billions of ringgit are required as capital and operational expenditure (CAPEX/OPEX) to develop platforms and oil infrastructure on the seabed off Sarawak. After deducting development costs, sharing with international partners, and tax/dividend payments to the Federal Government (which are ultimately channeled back into Sarawak's development allocation), Petronas's net profit margin is actually only around 10% to 15%. So, the narrative that Petronas "pockets" 95% does not reflect the reality of the oil business.

**Host:** Third question from Amir: *"Youth age limit of 30: genuine reform or a subtle way to eliminate the mature generation?"*

**Rafizi Ramli:** Here is an academic view: setting the age limit must look at life expectancy. Previously, average life expectancy was around 60 years, so age 40 was considered middle-aged and appropriate to relinquish youth leadership roles to younger people.

However, with current health quality, average life expectancy for Malaysians has reached 72–75 years, and Gen X is projected to live up to 80–90 years. If life expectancy is getting longer, age 30 is actually still very young. Personally, lowering the youth age limit to 30 is good for empowering young leadership, but the sociological definition of youth will likely keep shifting in line with national life expectancy statistics.

**Host:** Last question from Suffi Fauzi: *"Why does YB rarely mention health financing issues? Is the health issue in Malaysia a structural problem or an implementation one?"*

**Rafizi Ramli:** Health financing is a very complex issue with no silver bullet. Generally speaking, Malaysia's healthcare system (combining public and private sectors) is among the best and most equitable.

In the United Kingdom through the NHS, 100% is funded by taxpayers but waiting lists to see a doctor take weeks. In the United States, healthcare is fully privatized—without private insurance, a patient won't be treated at a hospital. Malaysia stands in the middle.

However, our main challenge is high medical inflation. Private hospitals are expanding rapidly as commercial entities demanding a 10%–12% return. In fact, GLCs like KWSP and PNB hold shares in these private hospital chains. As private costs skyrocket, the burden on the public health sector mounts further.

This issue encompasses both structural and implementation problems. The government cannot focus solely on building physical infrastructure (CAPEX); instead, it must invest in "software"—namely the welfare, training, and salaries of doctors and nurses. Just as in the education sector under RMK-13, where we recommended transforming 30%–40% of IPG's role into continuous training centers for teachers, public doctors similarly need continuous training and reduced administrative burden. This is a national crisis requiring coordination among the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Finance, and Ministry of Economy under the leadership of the Prime Minister himself.
