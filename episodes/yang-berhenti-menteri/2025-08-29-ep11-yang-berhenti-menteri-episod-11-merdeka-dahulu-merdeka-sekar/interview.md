# Interview

**Iqbal:** Assalamualaikum and welcome back to the latest episode of *Yang Berhenti Menteri*. I am your host, Iqbal, and here is our permanent fixture, YB Rafizi Ramli. Also with us tonight is a special guest, YB Nik Nazmi. This is only the second episode of the show—before this, Rafizi used to appear as a guest, and now he is returning the favor.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I’ll be frank: why is it so hard to get people to appear on this show? Everyone is afraid.

**Iqbal:** Afraid of you or afraid of the format?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I want to say I’m afraid of Farhan, but generally, it’s like our society—people who are a little "anti-mainstream" make others nervous. A few panelists who agreed to come on last week cancelled at the last minute.

**Iqbal:** Don't be so arrogant, Rafizi. Last week, we received feedback that my performance as a host was very disappointing to you because you were looking for something deeper. Your audience loves your lectures, and you’ve admitted to having the soul of a preacher. So, let’s give the people what they want tonight. The audience of this podcast wants to go deep into issues, not just surface-level remarks. They don’t mind if we ramble a bit, as long as the content is substantive.

**Rafizi Ramli:** *Berisi* (substantive), like me. Though, I have lost some weight.

**Iqbal:** Last week, you mentioned that sports weren't your forte. You said that back in college, you were everywhere *except* for sports. When we asked what ministry you’d be interested in leading, the Ministry of Youth and Sports was low on your list.

**Rafizi Ramli:** It’s actually opportunistic. You have to match a ministry with your skill set. Back in Form 1, I saw all the sportsmen; I knew I could play rugby, but I’d have to work double or triple as hard as them to make a difference. So, I chose the path of least resistance and focused on something else. When I came back from studying abroad, I noticed that while Western culture idolizes the "jocks," the popular kids here were usually the debaters.

**Nik Nazmi:** It depends on the culture. At MCKK, the debating team had a long tradition. They were the most *kiasu* (competitive)—they’d hold screen tests and once they had you, they made sure your whole life revolved around it. My experience was a bit different; I tried my best to get into the basketball team, but it wasn't there anymore, so I eventually got involved in debating in Form 3. I managed to win a trophy, so it worked out.

**Iqbal:** We’re recording this near Merdeka Day, so I want to start with a question about the "soul" of Merdeka. Nik, you were born in the 80s; Rafizi, you're from the 70s. When I hear my elders talk about Merdeka, they say the spirit was stronger back then—flags everywhere, people were more patriotic. Do your childhood memories align with that?

**Nik Nazmi:** I don't know, my father was a civil servant, but he wasn't the type to put up flags everywhere. That’s not to say he wasn't patriotic; it’s just that everyone has their own way of showing it. We focus on values. By the 90s, there were certain political connotations attached to putting up a flag. I grew up in that environment. Even when I was studying in London, I had a flag in my room.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don't think the "heat" of Merdeka was physically stronger back then. Maybe people forget that in the 80s and 90s, Malaysia was poorer; not many people had cars to put flags on. I grew up in Kemaman, in a wooden house—it would look funny to have a flag on a wooden shack. I don’t think that era was more "festive" than now. The only thing I remember is people watching the live telecast, which was boring to me as a kid. But there was a calmness; the issue of "who is more patriotic" didn't arise. There was no judgment.

**Nik Nazmi:** I agree. My perception is that the era of 1988 through the 90s had a "Malaysia Boleh" spirit. We won the Thomas Cup; there was confidence. I was too young to feel the political tension of the 80s, but the 90s felt calm. There was a sense that Malaysia could do anything—even the Malaysia Airlines commercials showed spaceships. We felt we were on our way to becoming a developed nation.

**Iqbal:** That slogan, "Malaysia Boleh," and the classic songs were widely accepted. Now, when there’s an attempt to release new marketing for Merdeka, the reception isn't as positive. What do you think is going on?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don't think it's about the marketing. Back then, there was no social media, so people couldn't easily criticize. I didn't pay much attention to Merdeka then, either—the best part was just that it was a holiday. If anything, the tension we see today is symptomatic of social media.

**Nik Nazmi:** Back then, we had a monopoly on information. Everyone watched the same news at 8:00 PM. Now, if you don’t see it on your "For You" page (FYP), you don’t hear it. The government has lost that monopoly.

**Iqbal:** At what point did you really feel a sense of patriotism?

**Nik Nazmi:** For me, maybe every time I watch the Olympics and see Dato' Lee Chong Wei in a final.

**Rafizi Ramli:** It depends on how you define patriotism. Growing up as a villager, we never dreamed of "big things." Most of our perspective on the country came from our interactions with people. As I read more history, I felt things could have been done better. I was very anti-racism from a young age because I realized this country was built together. I don't have that feeling of "Wow, I'm so proud of the country," but rather a feeling that Malaysia has done both bad and good, and there are ingredients we need to defend.

**Nik Nazmi:** It’s similar for me. My father’s generation had a romanticism for Tun Razak. My father shared stories not just about the government, but about figures like Ahmad Boestaman and Dr. Burhanuddin. I was curious about the history that wasn't celebrated. We appreciate those who fought for independence, even those on the left. We both have that curiosity—we don't look at things superficially.

**Rafizi Ramli:** We benefit from facts and having a higher curiosity. Because we were debaters, it was a requirement to read widely. For example, we were taught that communists were "evil" and would kill everyone. But when you read about the MPAJA (Malayan Peoples' Anti-Japanese Army) and the power vacuum after the Japanese surrendered, you understand the complexity. You learn about British policy and the socioeconomic realities of why migrant communities formed in tin mines. It's very different from the political stereotypes most Malay children grew up with.

**Iqbal:** Do you consider yourselves anomalies among Malaysians?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes. We don't represent the majority. We were raised at MCKK, which has a long tradition of leadership. But even then, I know people who went through the same schooling, live comfortably, and still harbor a sense of "Malay insecurity."

**Nik Nazmi:** It's that nature-versus-nurture debate. Even if they had scholarships and an easy life, that insecurity remains in the group chats.

**Rafizi Ramli:** For me, a big influence was coming from a working-class family. We believed 100% that social mobility came through education. I had to compete to be the top student, often against Chinese students—that competition had a positive connotation back then. It made me confident. I don't have that insecurity because I know what the worst-case scenario is, and I’ve already done better than my father’s generation. I think the challenge for Malays today is this "middle-class syndrome." We’ve become comfortable, and social mobility has slowed down because we're no longer hungry for that competition.

**Iqbal:** When did you realize you were different?

**Rafizi Ramli:** When I was in my third year of university, a classmate was reading a newspaper statement about UMNO and struggled to spell it out. I realized that political discussion at home wasn't something "ordinary" for everyone else.

**Nik Nazmi:** I had a similar experience. My dad was 52 when I was born, so our dinner table conversations were serious—religion, politics, economics. I thought all kids were like that until I saw how my friends reacted. My first realization of politics was in 1990; I bullied a batchmate because he supported a different party, but that was just childhood stuff. The *Reformasi* movement provided the platform for me to read, see alternatives, and seek a multiracial perspective.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Back then, the biggest fight was breaking the information blockade. I remember running a website and even a shortwave radio station from London for *Pemuda Keadilan*. Technology is what breaks and molds society—look at how Facebook helped the 2018 election. Technology allows people to access information outside the government’s monopoly. That is the lesson for any government: you cannot control information anymore. You have to accept that society changes with technology.

**Interviewer:** There are stories circulating about his family, his son-in-law, the "fourth floor," and so on. It eclipses everything else. It easily provides, as they say, the recipe for an explosion. And then there’s the view that Tun Abdullah Badawi’s tenure was seen as unfulfilled, leading to disappointment because…

**Rafizi Ramli:** There are also issues related to cronyism, corruption, and so on. Even though, actually, if you remove those issues, he did implement some monumental reforms. That will also happen to this government. On the one hand, that’s why I watched Dato’ Sri Amir Hamzah closely when he was handling the Government Procurement Act.

What is being asked—whether by backbenchers or the opposition—the questions are valid. And there is only one answer: "This is an improvement; this is better compared to the status quo." That is dangerous, you know, if we tell the people—especially PH voters themselves—that if the government does something, "Oh, why are you complaining? This is already better." That is what happened during Tun Abdullah Badawi’s time, and it is happening now because the public's expectations and hopes are different. We cannot just say that just because we are marginally better, we are already the best. Add to that the fact that with the current government, it repeats those stories, and it clouds everything.

If we talk about Dato' Sri Anwar—hopefully the best-case scenario for him—one day people will remember him like Tun Abdullah Badawi. Meaning, "Oh, actually, maybe we didn't give him enough credit." It’s interesting, but at the time he was Prime Minister, there were all sorts of things happening. That’s why I think our mentality—and to be fair, I felt it too when I first became a Minister—involves a lot of heartache.

**Interviewer:** Because…

**Rafizi Ramli:** It’s long hours, and whatever you do, it feels like it’s not right. You have to balance that with the noise from the outside, with people belittling everything. At the same time, you have to fight the system and find something. You have to wonder, "Why bother?" It’s natural to feel that we are trying our best and wonder, "Why are you like this?"

After about a year, I realized that this is indeed what it means to be the government. To be honest with those who are demanding things outside or criticizing us, the question is: is it wrong to aim for higher success? We promised not to be just better than UMNO, BN, or PN before; we promised to be radically different. This issue will continue to haunt any government that claims it will implement reforms.

**Interviewer:** On one hand, you have people who are…

**Rafizi Ramli:** Conservative. They will never be satisfied. They feel we are too liberal, too open, and moving too fast. But that was part of the landscape. 

**Interviewer:** At the same time, people who want change—our stubborn supporters—feel dissatisfied. But what's more, of course, we are in the reality of the government today, and there are challenges. But when we go back and use answers that are sometimes templates…

**Rafizi Ramli:** …which might have been prepared by senior PTD (Administrative and Diplomatic Officers) or political seniors before, we copy and paste, and we give the same answers. While the answer should be what we have been talking about when we were fighting to become leaders. I think it’s okay; we may not be able to do 100%, but the way we answer should be consistent. Once you are in the system, if we are less engaged and stop looking out, we will feel comfortable too quickly.

**Interviewer:** So, YB Rafizi, regarding that understanding of being in government versus being in the opposition: did you already know that before becoming a Minister, or was this a realization that only came after you were appointed?

**Rafizi Ramli:** A little bit, because I had experience in the state government from 2008 to 2018. But it’s not the same; the federal level is not as intense. In Selangor, I might handle education, but we were mostly distributing funds. At the federal level, the buck stops with you. Even if there are many things like land or minerals that aren't my direct responsibility, people will look to us. It’s a whole different level. I realized it while I was a Minister. I had never been an Exco member or in the public service before; this was my first time.

There was a sense of unfairness. Sometimes the comments—most of them from netizens early on—were frustrating. I’m a nerd, so before you make your argument, make sure you read it properly. Don’t comment on things you don’t know and then make a fuss. Initially, it did raise my blood pressure, literally. After a while, I realized you have two options: either you take it to heart and pick a fight with these people—the netizens or the *walaun* (loyalist supporters)—or you focus on the job. You have to choose the second option. You try your best, you prepare your best. It’s understandable to be misunderstood. In fact, being misunderstood is part and parcel of being a Minister or a public figure. It’s like watching a movie; people make a judgment after just 10 minutes. "Understandable to be misunderstood"—that could be a t-shirt for a Minister. Eventually, if we leave something behind, one day people will understand.

The netizens who missed the bus will eventually catch up. It’s quite normal. After almost a year, I realized you have to take a step back. It’s not a popularity contest; winning an argument with these people is useless. The important thing is to be clear where you need to be clear. You can’t stop people from being angry or criticizing, but you can advise them to check the facts. That’s the best you can do. The rest, hopefully, is that your work will be appreciated many years down the line. Once I took that position, it made many things easier. It provided clarity.

**Interviewer:** To what extent do you believe intention is important? If we take Tun Abdullah Badawi, for example, maybe his intention for a hands-off policy toward the internet was just because he was lazy. If his intention was laziness, is that still worth praise?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don’t know; I never had the time to ask him. It’s hard to be a good person, right? But I think, yes, in hindsight, there were many positive things. However, one thing I learned when I became a Minister—which is very different in scale from my previous duties—is that we must have an end goal in mind. If not, we will just be firefighting. 24 hours a day, you get a hundred letters and invitations, all big and small; everyone gets upset if you don’t show up. At the end of the day, you have to know your end goal. You have to do it in the best way possible, and you have to maintain discipline regarding your life, your health, and your family. If you don't, you burn out, your time is over, and you’re not happy.

I judge a politician first and foremost by intention.

**Interviewer:** But as you said, how can you ascertain someone’s intention?

**Rafizi Ramli:** You can tell from their behavior. When I say "intention," I don’t mean regarding policy. People always complain that I’m not a team player or that I’m elite, but that’s because I judge someone in politics first and foremost by their intention. You can look at a leader—every picture they take, you can see their intent. You can tell if someone is truly subscribed to reform or public service, or if they’re just in it to live comfortably. That initial screening is very important.

But intention alone is not enough. You need the right attitude—being diligent, willing to learn, not arrogant, being honest, and not taking money. Then, the third level is competence. Only then, in my view, does that person have the potential to be a leader. You can tell which colleagues are just interested in attending functions and officiating ceremonies—their intention isn’t to bring change, but to enjoy the comfort of being a Minister. It’s easy to be on "autopilot" as a Minister because everything is provided: speech texts, draft answers.

**Interviewer:** You want to chair a meeting, right? The "Assalamualaikum" and everything are already written for you. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, there are people like that in the Cabinet. They have a speech note, they read it one by one. But my point is, what’s the use of that? If that’s the case, it’s better for the KSU (Secretary General) to just enter the Cabinet. You are the one with a political mandate, accountable to the public; you have to give direction. I have great respect for civil servants, but they have a different job scope. They provide the advice, but the final decision on political direction comes from us.

In the Cabinet, we are responsible collectively. We have some Ministers who won’t speak unless it’s their specific portfolio. I speak a lot because that is our last intervention—if the government wants to do something we aren't comfortable with, you have to speak beyond your brief. I’m not just the Minister for NRES; I am a member of the Cabinet. Many play it safe, but that’s where I have a problem with PAS.

**Interviewer:** In terms of?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don’t think their intention is necessarily malicious. Their intention is to be an elected representative and to be comfortable in the government. But regarding attitude and competence, I don’t think they pass the mark. That’s why many ask, "Why don’t you support PAS?" It’s because if someone has the intention but the wrong attitude, it doesn’t work. That is our contest in PKR—the culture and the attitude must be correct. If we see that a group is only interested in living luxuriously, being a "big shot," and is not careful about money, their attitude is off. That will destroy the trust people have in us.

People don’t understand why political leaders like me or YB Nik disagree with others in our own party. "Why are you fighting?" It’s because of that layer. If your intention is right but your behavior is wrong, or if you’re a good person with a good attitude but you can’t do the work—you just become a "flower pot" (ornamental figure) in the office—that’s not useful. A very expensive flower pot. Our political culture still has a long way to go. People think, "He thinks he’s the only one who’s right." But is it wrong to set a higher standard? Without that, what’s the point of changing the government and entering politics? We might as well have just stayed in UMNO. We entered because we believe in values.

**Interviewer:** When I discuss political parties in Malaysia with my friends, they say we are far from independence. When we think about what a political party is, in my opinion, a political party should be putting forward a vision. "This is our vision; if you believe in it, vote for us." Versus many parties in Malaysia where we don’t know what they believe. They just ask, "What do you like? If you like this, we will do that." They don't offer a sense of what Malaysia should be based on core principles. Do you think that’s fair?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That’s a fair comment. But just because a party like PAS or PSM has clarity doesn't make them better than others. You have to look at *where* the clarity comes from. For PAS, it’s easy because they ride on religion. For PSM, it’s easy because their socialism won't work at all; it won’t solve Malaysia's problems, which is why they are un-electable. Malaysia is very pluralistic. We need to be as inclusive as possible.

I can tell you straight away why I’m in PKR. It’s not about Malay, Chinese, or Indian—it’s about the worldview I hold. I don’t want to be a Malaysian who is righteous by suppressing others or taking their rights. I want to be a confident person who can put forth an argument with confidence, whether I'm right or wrong, and be respected for that. I want that pride. PKR is the path that translates that worldview because of the premium we place on intellect and because we don’t take the easy path of exploiting emotion or race. 

**Interviewer:** Many people who supported the Reformation weren't just supporting Anwar Ibrahim as an individual; they supported him because they felt he was oppressed—an issue of justice that needed to be fought. But the problem now is we don’t have that shared understanding. The voters now don't know that story. Reformation has been a long road. We won; now we are trying to sustain it. That’s our problem: how to make people understand the struggle for justice. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** The challenge is that vision is not just about form, but substance. For a pluralistic society like Malaysia, clarity can't be just a "form." It has to be the essence. Everyone—Chinese, Indian, Malay, Sabahans, Sarawakians—must feel they have a stake. If we continue with identity politics—where the Malay party tries to empower Malays and the Chinese party tries to empower Chinese—that is the fundamental problem of Malaysia.

**Interviewer:** Speaking as someone who was there in 1998, I remember my mother picking me up at the rally. I was 5. I remember screaming "Reformasi." For me, the most recent thing that gave me awareness was the incident with the Prime Minister at the time—the idea that he could do that and get away with it was utterly unacceptable on a visceral level. With Dato' Sri Najib possibly experiencing a return to being a "hero," does that undermine the idea of what Malaysia should be?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Whatever our feelings, that is a cycle that has happened in other countries. Societies forget. Tun Mahathir is right; our people easily forget. Look at the Philippines—Marcos, 1986. I remember the shoes, the clothes, the joy of liberation. I never thought he would come back, but he did. It’s a continuous cycle. The test isn't on Najib, PKR, or Anwar; the test is on our society. The younger generation didn't go through the emotive experience of 1MDB. They didn't feel our feelings. It is our duty to continuously ensure there is enough sanity in our society so we don't forget. If we are lazy, it will happen again.

**Interviewer:** If we are lazy as a society, is that a reason to give up, or do we just have to keep going?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Disappointment is an easy feeling. It’s instinctive. But this is like the fight for independence. We used to think that once we were independent, everything would be easy. We have to educate the community that it is a process. Without being vigilant and applying those values to future generations, that thing will return to haunt us.

**Interviewer:** It’s a test. You might be overwhelmed by the times. It’s a reminder to me and others: even if our ideas are progressive and we don't win right away, someone has to do it. It’s a cycle. We can’t let the chain break. I’m prepared for any scenario—even if Dato’ Sri Najib or Datin Seri Rosmah were to become PM one day. I’ll do what I can before my expiry date. But as long as the society has an alternative, it’s okay. My worry is that the people will feel that "never again" will they risk change because they’ve been hurt. That is the danger. Once that happens, you open the gate, and it takes so long for the next generation to dare to take the risk again.

**Interviewer:** Now that we have a government that can change, what is the main challenge?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The challenge is to maintain our identity. If we don’t, we’re just a cheap imitation. Why take the cheap imitation? For me, we have a unique selling proposition as PKR. We are a united government, but where is our red line? If people see that our excuse is always, "Oh, we don't have the numbers," then people will ask why we are holding power. We have to be clear; we cannot be grey. If we are grey, we will lose because we don't have the grassroots organizational strength that UMNO, PAS, or DAP have. PKR is a guerrilla party. We don't have the drones; we just have guerrilla tactics.

**Interviewer:** From your perspective, what are the main threats to Malaysia in the next 15 years?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The lack of social mobility. We shouldn’t take the view of the city people or those who are influential; our lives are okay. But the majority of the people are struggling. They want a better house and a middle-class life. In the 70s, 80s, and 90s, the "Malaysia Boleh" spirit existed because social mobility was good—if you studied and worked hard, you could uplift yourself. Now, mobility is slow, partly because we are starting from a higher point and partly because of the structural competitiveness of our economy.

If people feel that no matter how hard they work, their life stays the same, they become dissatisfied. They start pointing fingers. That’s why we see the Trump phenomenon in the US, or the lurch to the far right in Europe. It’s because of the lack of social mobility. People get frustrated and become anti-elite. They start blaming the "rich" or "immigrants." 

In Malaysia, it’s unique because race and class are often interchangeable. When people talk about "the rich," they often mean non-Malays. It’s dangerous. My economic insecurity is low because I have achieved mobility. That’s why I don’t believe in cosmetic solutions. The "time bomb" is the lack of mobility. We have to swallow the bitter pills of structural economic reform. If the government is only interested in the short term and keeps kicking the can down the road, if Malaysia isn't careful, we will spiral just like other countries. It becomes "tax and spend, tax and spend." Who is unlucky enough to be the government when we default? It’s going to be us.

**Interviewer:** What was the most "merdeka" (liberated) point you’ve reached in your career? 

**Rafizi Ramli:** 2018 was a feeling of liberation, but the moment Dato' Sri Najib was convicted—that was significant. Not many countries can manage a peaceful change of government and then bring their former Prime Minister to prison. That was a sense of achieving something.

**Interviewer:** For me, the most liberating moment was 2008. I was outside politics, working in corporate. When the results came in, I realized that everything wasn't futile after all. It couldn't be turned back. That provided the hope that led me to, foolishly, resign my job and get involved. 2013, 2018, and 2022 were intense, but 2008 was the shock that started it all.

**Interviewer:** You’ve described that emotional resonance and that sense that the last 10 years were not in vain, which really ignited the spirit. If you had to sum up Merdeka as an emotional vibe for the youth who perhaps haven't experienced what we have over the last 10 to 20 years, what does Merdeka mean to you both?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Merdeka, I think ultimately, being free is one part of it. But it also means we have the capability to determine our own direction. 

There is pressure there—and I think that’s the thing. When I look at the younger generation, we have already implemented Undi18 for better or for worse, but I think that was inevitable. We can question it, but it was inevitable. The issue isn't so much about them choosing to vote for PAS, but rather when the kids don't care at all or don't take an interest. We fought so hard to get Undi18; the whole point was for us to determine our own direction. So for me, freedom is one thing, but if you are free yet unable to determine your own direction, it doesn’t have any meaning.

**Interviewer:** YB Rafizi?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yeah, I think Merdeka is precisely what it is. Buildings and all these other forms—to me, they aren’t that important. What I wish for is that everyone has a "jiwa merdeka" [independent spirit]. It is difficult to explain exactly what that spirit is, but I hope that whatever we have been doing over the last three to four months—that is actually the meaning of it. I am glad. Personally, Nick, by pushing back and holding our ground, I feel we have proven to ourselves, more than anything else, that we still have it.

We still have that "fire in the belly." It means that if we need to walk a long distance to decide our own success and to do what we think is right—with confidence and without apology—we can. And I think that is what I hope for everyone in Malaysia. Don’t apologize for whatever we are. If you are Malay, don't feel that we are weak, soft, oppressed, or stupid. Whether you are Malay, Chinese, or Indian, have that confidence, have that conviction, and do what we think is right. We won't necessarily be right all the time, and if we are wrong, we will learn from it. But have the pride to chart your own course in life. If everyone is like that, I think Malaysia will prosper.

**Interviewer:** Great!

**Interviewer (Co-host):** I think that is a good point to end on. Selamat Hari Merdeka. Ikhbal is being very polite today. 

**Ikhbal:** I have to be. After being criticized so much, I am traumatized. I’ve learned from my mistakes. I am liberating myself from negative comments. Thank you to all the viewers of *Berhenti Menteri* for being with us for the last hour and 40 minutes, and Selamat Hari Merdeka. Today, I think viewership went down.

**Rafizi Ramli:** [Laughs] Don’t call me. It's because people are bored if there's no "bakar-bakar" [stirring up controversy].

**Ikhbal:** Oh, they just want to see things catch fire. If they want to see that, I have to invite someone else. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** It was weird because you had people providing feedback asking, "Can you ask more vision questions? Long-term vision-building questions."

**Ikhbal:** Oh, really?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yeah, there were some. 

**Ikhbal:** There were a few, but not many. Those are just the "nerds" asking. Okay, so there are some nerds out there. To the nerds, Selamat Hari Merdeka.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I can see that.
