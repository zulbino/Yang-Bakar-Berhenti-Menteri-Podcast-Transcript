# Interview

**Host:** Assalamualaikum and welcome to *Podcast Yang Berhenti Menteri*, episode 5 with Saudara Rafizi Ramli. Our guest speaker tonight is Dato' Syed Azwan (DSA). How are you, Dato'?

**Dato' Syed Azwan (DSA):** I'm good, I'm good. I'm healthy.

**Host:** Dato', I want to ask you something. When was the first time you met Saudara Rafizi?

**DSA:** The first time I met him was in Alor Setar, regarding the 13th Malaysia Plan (RMK 13).

**Rafizi Ramli:** Oh, ya, ya. Okay. That RMK 13 topic is quite sensitive. I actually touched on it in my YouTube live as well—regarding the last-minute changes. That was when? Was it last year?

**DSA:** This year, in Alor Setar. I think it was in the second or third month.

**Rafizi:** Oh, yes, because the last time we did it was during the Kedah floods. It was postponed to the second month, then the ninth month. So it was last month.

**Host:** Dato', since you are our sponsor for *Pintu Topi* tonight, please introduce yourself to our audience. Who is DSA, beyond what we see on your live streams and YouTube?

**DSA:** Hi, DSA here. Assalamualaikum, Salam Nusyamadani. My content signature is "Hi DSA." Initially, I didn't know how to start. I did a lot of work, but after COVID, I didn't know what to do. I was very skeptical about social media; when I heard "TikTok," I thought, "Why TikTok? It's just for kids." But I would always go out of the country for business conferences. When I went to Thailand, I was the first one, *tergedik-gedik* [eager/excited], wanting to see the places where I’d held my past conferences. 

In Chiang Mai, it opened my mind. I went to a coffee shop called "Graph." When I entered, I felt, "This is my life." I had been through the ups and downs. After COVID, I hit rock bottom. Before COVID, I would just travel and have fun, but after the crash, I felt I needed to do something. I said, "Why not make a TikTok account?" My account was born in Chiang Mai at that store. 

**Host:** Why did you go to that coffee shop? 

**DSA:** Everyone was playing on TikTok, but it was specifically the coffee shop that changed my perception. They said life is like a graph—it moves up and down. That shifted my perspective. I started my first video around July 2022. It's been about three years. I didn't know what my "niche" was, so I just hit the ground running. At first, I talked about personal stuff, but people weren't interested. So, I started touching on politics. 

One turning point was a video in Bangkok about "The Roti Lady"—a person who makes beautiful bread. That one got one million views. After that, I touched on politics and young people, and the first video there also hit one million. I had to force myself to do TikTok. After 2018, I wasn't involved in politics because of my situation. I was late to the game, and now I'm chasing after the ministers. 

**Host:** YB's TikTok followers are already over 200,000. 

**DSA:** For me, TikTok—my children asked, "Walid, why do you want to play TikTok? Your life is relaxing, people don't know you, people don't hate you." I said I want to do something new. Something that can't be bought with money. I first came across your content about young people who are sometimes motivated by money. They think money is everything, but I believe we need knowledge to be rich. 

**Host:** Okay, Dato'. Tonight, we'll open the floor. The empty table is yours. Dato' will ask questions to YB Rafizi, and if you have an answer, you answer back. If not, I'll ask again and "burn" it again. Can you do that, Dato'?

**DSA:** Yes, I can. 

**Host:** Let's start. 

**DSA:** Actually, on Saturday, I want to go to Kuala Terengganu for the *haul* [commemoration] of Tokku Paloh. I was in the "Saif Sharifah" gang, and they were pushing me to go back. I said it’s okay; I want to go to get a little blessing. Tonight, I was surprised to see YB’s podcast title: "YB dah kembali" [YB has returned]. When I saw that on the media, I held onto that phrase: "Welcome back, YB." Why did the journalist say that?

**Rafizi:** When was that? During the press conference in Subang Jaya? Look, before I became a minister, I had a close relationship with the media because I was the one doing the most press conferences. It was normal for them to see me two or three times a week. But when I became part of the government, we couldn't do as much media. It's not our job. If a reporter asks, there are things we can't comment on. For two and a half years, I felt that distance. So, when we met again recently, especially with the senior journalists who started their careers around the same time I started in politics, it was like a reunion.

**DSA:** I actually made content, too, "beating" you. I said, "What happened to YB Rafizi? Since becoming a minister, he’s gone quiet." I said, in the words of people from Perlis, you were "tak ada telor" [spineless]. 

**Rafizi:** Is that so? See, we have two roles: as ministers and as politicians. The Ministry of Economy is the most complicated because it touches everything—transportation, education, etc. The Cabinet's decision is that ministers shouldn't comment on other ministries' portfolios. If I talk, it’s about the economy, inflation, or prices—the stuff others don't want to touch. I’ve been reprimanded by the PM a few times: "Don't touch this, don't touch that." So, it’s hard to speak out. I chose to focus on the Ministry’s tasks. If I comment on politics too much, it disrupts our focus and upsets people—UMNO isn't happy, DAP isn't happy. So, I decided to focus on the work, even if it’s "boring."

**DSA:** I supported the economy work in my content. I did a piece on PADU. I don't know why I told people to sign up for PADU, but I thought it was interesting because it helps identify who truly deserves aid. But, YB, is PADU still being used?

**Rafizi:** The backend data is useful for all ministries. It saves over 50% in costs because agencies don't have to build new backend databases. That will continue to be used. As for the cash assistance aspect, there are different views. The Ministry of Finance feels it's too complicated to integrate income and household data. They view STR (Sumbangan Tunai Rahmah) as their product, and PADU interferes with their existing system. They prefer the status quo. 

**DSA:** Let’s talk about RMK 13. Before, nobody cared about it. Suddenly, it’s in Parliament, and now YB is involved in the management. I went to the convention, and I saw a difference in how you answer questions. But I want to tell you, the UMNO representatives I met... they don't like direct aid to the people. They want it through their channels. 

**Rafizi:** That refers to the IKR (Inisiatif Kemudahan Rakyat). Within the government, views differ. Some think IKR dilutes the influence of politicians because people can request aid directly, and we bypass the old system. Previously, it went to the State Development Office or ICU. Now, we have a new channel. If people request something, we investigate it to ensure it’s actually needed—not just a contractor pushing a project. If the local council is "lazy" and doesn't want to execute a project for the people, I won't approve their permit. In places like Kedah, where the government is 100% opposition, I still approve projects if they truly benefit the people. That’s why I get criticized by my own allies, but I just swallow it. 

**DSA:** I agree. But what about the RMK 13 last-minute changes? It feels like the government is scrambling.

**Rafizi:** I understand the process is complex. It takes a year and a half to draft. I don't think they are changing everything in two weeks. Before me, people treated RMK as a "wish list" for projects. My instruction was that a five-year plan isn't a project list; it’s a policy framework. The large projects require a long-term planning cycle, and they are submitted every two years through a "rolling plan." So, in this two-week period, I am reviewing the final draft to see what needs to be added or cut.

**DSA:** I saw the Minister of Defense suddenly wanting to buy assets. I was surprised. 

**Rafizi:** The government has a planning cycle. Requests for assets are submitted annually. From the second to the sixth month, the Ministry of Economy grills every request. We check everything—where to cut, why, the price. We have engineers, architects, and experts on our team to verify these things. We set a ceiling—for example, RM400 billion for five years. That’s RM90 billion a year. From that, we have to pay debts and maintain ongoing projects. Everyone fights for that extra RM500 million.

**DSA:** There are many things to improve in the budget process. I actually disagree with ministers making grand announcements for projects that were already approved two years ago.

**Rafizi:** I agree. I wanted to publish the list of approved projects for every parliamentary constituency so that the people know what is coming. But it was not agreed upon because it’s considered "dangerous." They’re afraid of political interference in tenders. But transparency is important. If you know the projects, you can hold your representatives accountable.

**DSA:** Moving on to the coalition government. YB, we are in a coalition. Sometimes I want to criticize our counterparts in UMNO or Amanah, but I worry about breaking the unity. The coalition is fragile. Do you think, after the 16th General Election, this entity will stay the same?

**Rafizi:** The scenario is unclear. The unity government was formed because of a hung parliament. Within PH, there are varying views—some want to move forward with BN, others are hesitant. The same goes for BN; the top leadership is inclined toward PH-BN, but the grassroots are different. We have two and a half years to see if the leadership can harmonize these views. As it gets closer to the election, the voices will get louder. 

**DSA:** What about the practice of appointing those who lost in the election? It creates a culture of "bootlicking" because they don't need to serve the people to get a position.

**Rafizi:** I understand the need for experienced, senior leaders when forming a government. However, we must heed the people's view. One of the successes of the Reformasi movement was our stand against such practices. If we appoint losers, it encourages sycophancy. In the coming election, Anwar has already planned this. We must make sure these people contest in winnable seats. Don't let them lose and then appoint them as Senators or Ministers. The people won't accept it.

**DSA:** I agree. One more thing: the head of J-KOM. It’s better to have someone professional. Some people in PKR don't like me because I’m "brutal." But when we hold public positions paid by the people's money, we have a responsibility to them and the party. If someone is "sleeping" on the job or doing lives with only 10 viewers, we have to be honest. 

**Rafizi:** When I became a minister, many of my officers were not from the party. For example, my press secretary used to be Said Saddiq’s media officer. I chose the best person for the job. If we give positions just to reward "service," but they can't do the work, it’s the Prime Minister who gets blamed in the end. 

**DSA:** I like Johari Ghani. I’ve mentioned in my content that he could be a future UMNO president and Prime Minister. He said a candidate must be someone the people like, regardless of the party. Do you agree?

**Rafizi:** We have to balance it. When we field 100 candidates, some need to be national figures, but others need to be local favorites. The problem arises when branch leaders think they are entitled to a parliamentary seat just because they hold a position. That’s wrong. The party leadership must put its foot down. If the candidate is a "crook," don't put them up, even if they are a branch chief. 

**DSA:** Why not scrap the branch system entirely, like parties abroad?

**Rafizi:** That’s a fair point. The old system was necessary 40 years ago when there was no social media. You needed a physical grassroots machine to knock on doors. Now, social media has shifted that. We can reach the people through screens. DAP and PKR can win without 100% reliance on the traditional branch machinery. The people now look at national issues and digital reach. The future of political parties lies in finding a model that isn't dependent solely on local branch strength.

**DSA:** I agree. Recently in Thailand, someone told me, "We don't want a broadcast leader; we want a 'light' leader." I was angry. Without broadcasting, how do you lead the narrative? 

**Rafizi:** Perspectives differ. But I think I see it clearly. We have to adapt.

**Interviewer:** There has been some chatter recently regarding a new initiative. Someone mentioned that PKR needs to form a media campaign team to rebuild the Prime Minister’s reputation. What is your take on this?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I want to comment on the “broadcasting” aspect. I don't want to mention names, but my experience with Syamsul Iskandar since day one in this party—well, he calls himself a “broadcast leader.” I don’t want that. He isn’t a broadcast leader; he’s a “hidden leader.” He likes to operate this way: he tells people like DSA (Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim) or myself to go out and campaign with the people, to be the face of the party. He stays in the background, and when we win, he’s the one who steps in to reap the rewards.

**Interviewer:** So, that’s why, in the end, when the election results don’t align with the will of the people on the ground, it becomes a problem within the party. People ask, “Who are you? I’ve never seen you, I’ve never even known you.” That leads back to my earlier question.

**Rafizi Ramli:** The reality is that the current political campaign is a digital campaign. If it weren’t, I wouldn’t have a podcast every week. If there are still people who think that traditional news or TV3 has the biggest influence, they are mistaken. The people now value the quality of leaders and the party in a continuous digital environment. People may not follow the developments in other countries, but look at Trump. One of the things he did differently was utilizing podcasts. He went on all the podcasts that people labeled "right-wing" or "anti-immigration." The Democrats asked, “Why are you going there? That guy is a racist.” But Trump knew there were 100 million people listening.

So, if Sam or anyone else says, "We don't need to broadcast," fine. Don't broadcast, but if you contest, you will surely lose. Like it or not, our image is formed through continuous engagement. It’s not just about talking; the public assesses our character, our knowledge, and our skills. It’s different from the pre-digital era. Back then, if a party placed a candidate, people had to vote for them whether they liked it or not. Now, the public's expectations are higher. You can't act. There are leaders who try to use gimmicks—like washing things during a flood—but in this digital age, you can’t get away with it. People follow every move you make daily. They know when you’re acting because they’ve been following you from the start. That is why authenticity is critical. If you aspire to be a national leader but choose to be a "hidden leader," you don’t have much time left in our country. That’s my view.

**Interviewer:** Regarding Nick Adams, what do you think? I think it’s positive because Trump chose him for his influence. But it’s unusual—not a diplomat, but an influencer from Australia being sent as an ambassador.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don’t follow his background too closely, but we have to understand that in the US, ambassadorial appointments are mostly political. It’s not like us, where the vast majority of ambassadors are career diplomats. It’s standard practice there. Even JFK’s father, Joseph Kennedy, was appointed ambassador to the UK. It’s a reward for supporters. It’s not something foreign. As for Nick Adams’ character, if the government wants to protest, that’s their right. But eventually, he will arrive. For a country like Malaysia, we need to balance this. There’s nothing wrong with stating openly that we disagree with his views. Once he is the ambassador here, he must represent the US government’s stance, not his own personal views. If he crosses the line, there are diplomatic mechanisms to protest. The most important thing is ensuring that in the current tariff wars, we maintain strong influence with the White House and Trump. If Malaysia is smart, we should use Nick Adams’ proximity to Trump as a strategic advantage. 

**Interviewer:** It’s a very rational take. But for a government like Anwar Ibrahim’s, it’s difficult to answer to narratives about being "pro-Zionist."

**Rafizi Ramli:** If he makes pro-Zionist statements as an ambassador, we and various NGOs have the right to protest. We can send diplomatic notes because it conflicts with our sovereignty. We cannot just act to win the social media war or because we are afraid of being attacked. You think Trump cares if we object to Nick Adams? He’ll send him anyway. In international relations, there are rules. We agree on some things and disagree on others, but we must respect sovereignty. We should be professional. If we can secure benefits in tariff negotiations, the government’s duty is to explain that to the people. 

**Interviewer:** I’ve noticed people praising you lately, like Abdul Karim Rahman Hamzah from Sarawak. He said your removal was a loss for PKR and called you a smart strategist. Yet, there is a recurring theme of conflict between two groups in any party—the idealists and the controllers.

**Rafizi Ramli:** If we look at history—Lenin and Stalin, for example—after the Bolshevik revolution, the true ideologue was Trotsky. Stalin was the head of propaganda. He used his position to control the narrative and eventually destroy Trotsky. It happened in China too, with Mao Zedong and the "Gang of Four" surrounding him to maintain control against people like Deng Xiaoping. In any party, there will always be a conflict between those who are the "face" for the people and the group that acts like a cat trying to catch a mouse—hiding in the back. They want to control the party. Let the "broadcast leaders" do the hard work, and when power is won, they take it. 

The struggle in PKR from the Azmin era until now is exactly that. My difference is that I am seen as someone who does not compromise. The message to me has been clear: "If you want to stay, stay as a minister, sit in the president’s office, but be a good broadcast leader and don't interfere. Let us handle the behind-the-scenes work." I chose not to. I have too much other work to do. But if the faces that the people recognize are eliminated, and the new leaders cannot win the hearts of the people like the old ones did, then PKR will lose its relevance. The danger is that they hide behind Anwar Ibrahim. Anwar takes all the heat, and eventually, they will drag him down because he is the only interface with the people. If I were the type to just say, "Okay, I'll do my job and you take everything," I would be fine, but I can't. I entered this party to fix these cultural issues.

**Interviewer:** If you ever become President of PKR and want to form a government, but you face these elements, would you still try to form a government at all costs?

**Rafizi Ramli:** In 2022, there was a lesson in how we formed the unity government. In Germany, coalitions are formed after the election with detailed agreements. When we formed the government in 2022, it was a start. But in the future, if a party doesn’t have a majority, the people will accept a coalition if the terms are clear and not just a "blank check." If the conditions set by the partners are too contradictory to our ideals, then it’s better not to be in government. It’s not worth destroying the party’s soul just to hold office. 

**Interviewer:** People are confused by your recent comments about the "Bangkit Malaysia" initiative. Are you involved?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don’t know what Syamsul Iskandar is organizing or what he wants to announce. But I have to accept the reality that for the past few months, PKR’s image hasn’t been great. If people blame me, saying it’s because of the internal elections, I’ve already moved on. The problem is when the party culture treats loyalists—people who have put up flags for decades without ever asking for a position—like they don’t matter. When those people feel like this isn't their house anymore, that is dangerous. 

The current leadership hasn’t handled this well. Don't lecture people on "loyalty" or "sacrifice." When you have a person like "Abang Liah" in Padang Engah—a military veteran who worked for the party for years without ever wanting to be a candidate—and you treat him like he’s invisible, he will eventually walk away. When people who have given their lives to the party feel they no longer belong, they stop supporting us. That is the reality. We are not "Kiam Bang" (water lettuce) that just drifts; we are humans with hearts. The leadership needs to understand that.

**Interviewer:** You’ve become quite popular; people are even looking for you in Padang Rengas. I have one final question. As you know, you weren't present recently, and there was quite a bit of backlash. I won't comment on it myself—I know the reason why you weren't there. Now that Parliament is reconvening, there will be the Prime Minister’s Question Time (PMQT). Are you planning to be vocal and pose questions to the Prime Minister during these sessions, even as a government backbencher?

**Rafizi Ramli:** For me, if you look at the origin of the PMQT culture, it comes from Westminster, right? PMQT is a platform for the protesters, actually, every week to ask the PM. So, I am not a protestant. And I don’t intend to condemn Anwar Ibrahim for his work, except unless there are big issues that need to be addressed. I really don’t intend to make the Parliament a place to fight. When I was a member of the parliamentary committee, I only used the Parliament to challenge Najib and 1MDB. 

I’m not confused; I’m focused. All my questions are related to facts and scandals. When I ask them one by one, the ministers provide answers—perhaps the government officials just want to sabotage, but they do answer. First of all, I have no intention of being a hero in Parliament. 

Now that I am a senior backbencher within the government, I see that in any other country's parliament, a senior backbencher is someone respected by both sides of the aisle. They serve as a role model for junior backbenchers, and even the opposition should respect them. I need to find that balance. I intend to focus on issues—Fadlina [Sidek, Minister of Education] shouldn’t take this personally—but I will focus on education, because I feel our country doesn't discuss education enough. I will also speak on the economy if given the opportunity.

My questions have already been submitted. There is no intention to embarrass Anwar. My view is this: I know people outside are saying this is a government that wants to fall, that there’s a movement to make Anwar fall. But in PKR, what will you do to ensure the government stays?

**Interviewer:** You believe this government needs to be maintained?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It’s not because I’m trying to be sycophantic [“tak sop”] toward Anwar or power, but because the country cannot afford another change in government right now while facing severe economic challenges. If our growth drops to 2-3%, or even lower, we need a stable government. Anwar Ibrahim needs to remain as the Prime Minister until the end of the term. I may not be able to influence what happens during the election—that strategy and campaign is up to Nurul Izzah and other leaders—but as a member of parliament, my job is to ensure this government does its best to navigate these severe challenges over the next year or two. If others don’t want to do that work, I can do it.

It’s not because I want to overthrow the government or dislike Anwar Ibrahim. It’s the work of a parliamentarian. But I hope that, as a backbencher, I won’t be viewed as overly aggressive. Everything I say, if it’s true, should be accepted well. Our intention is the same: to ensure the people can value the quality of the existing government. Even if I don’t fully agree with everything, this government is better than the alternative, and it seems to listen to criticism and is capable of improving.

If we just keep blindly praising the government while the people feel a certain way, I don’t agree with that. Everyone uses the same tricks, but I don’t care. Win or lose, I am who I am. I have enough to survive for the next 10–15 years; it doesn’t affect me personally. But if we go through this and the government gets worse, or if they refuse to listen and fail to improve the situation, the ones who lose are the people. That is why Parliament is important. 

I have never looked at Parliament as a venue for "peacocking" or chest-thumping. I see Parliament as having two tasks. One is to obtain information that isn't in the public domain—that is a privilege only MPs have. I will dig for that. If something isn't right, I will uncover it and I will speak up. But as for speeches, I have no intention of attacking a minister for the sake of it. However, if I get a chance to speak, it might not be harsh, but it will be a bit "hot" or "stinging." It’s just a little bit, just listen to it! [Laughs]

**Interviewer:** What you said just now, people can judge for themselves. There is no intent to bring down the government.

**Rafizi Ramli:** We have to help. Like me, every day, if I see anything regarding the PM, I make content, even though I don’t get anything for it. That is a responsibility. Look at my studio—there is a picture of the Prime Minister behind me. I believe I was the only one who dared to put his picture behind me. 

Think of it like our mother. If our mother never got angry, we would have been ruined. When we were young, she would scold us; as we grew older, she scolded us in a different way. But imagine if she scolded us 100 times—she wouldn't be human. In our culture, there are people who need to be scolded. And that scolding can be a little rough, a little painful. Don’t shoot the messenger; get the message. That is the important thing. Our political culture hasn't reached that level yet, so it is our job to get the people used to it. In the end, as PKR has said since the beginning, the people must be the ones to judge. 

We need a process where the government follows the voice of the people. This is a process that has wisdom. If I were a minister again, maybe this would get stuck in the middle of the road. Because there is no one in PKR or PH who is a little bit "deaf." Maybe for now, for us to be firm, we need a voice like this. I joined the KKR [Kementerian Ekonomi] because I want to be a good person. In my live content, I always say that the opposition’s narrative is only about three things: baiting the "stupid" Malay, the "poor" Malay, and then DAP. 

We have to educate the people. It’s the same with the media. I am not surprised by who is appointed as a judge, but the way you bring the story—now I am an expert! [Laughs] The young people who will move forward must be educated, they must be trained to judge our leaders based on who is actually doing the work. That is all. 

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Dato'. That was a very productive hour and 50 minutes. Thank you for coming all the way here. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** I have plenty of time. Next time, I might even go to your studio at DSA to play around. 

**Interviewer:** We’ll see you next week. Thank you to everyone for listening. Good night!
