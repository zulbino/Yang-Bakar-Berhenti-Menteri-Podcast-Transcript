On top of that, when we give directly to district councils, it reduces political control somewhat — if it went through the old system, through the State Development Office, through ICU, it could be controlled. That process still runs as usual — every year ICU submits applications for allocations to the Ministry of Economy for projects managed by ICU in their respective states. The Prime Minister might prefer that approach. But actually, under the Ministry of Economy, going back to right after independence, every year district councils throughout Malaysia have applied directly to the federal government for small projects. The reason is, I think there are only 3-4 municipal or district councils in Malaysia that actually have money to build infrastructure — their door-tax collections and other revenue are barely enough to pay salaries. As for state governments, since almost all of them don't have sufficient revenue for development spending, they can't give money to municipal councils either. That's why, in rural areas — sometimes even in urban areas people don't realize this — asking for a small RM200,000 bridge to be built can take ten years without approval. Sometimes I've seen ministers come to me and say, "can you help look into a RM200,000 approval in my constituency?" That shouldn't happen, because at the same time, this RM100 million allocation is actually paid by the federal government through the Ministry of Economy to all district councils. The difference is, they write letters to the minister. So when they write letters — when I first entered my office, in the very first month, letters would come flying in every single day, everyone requesting allocations, wanting roads built, and so on. I was once a constituency representative myself, so I understand. Actually, if we let 100% of applications come solely from district councils, solely from government officers, they wouldn't necessarily do what the people truly want — they'd just follow what an officer says, "I want it here." So we introduced IKR. IKR means there's a new channel — the usual allocation, where applications from municipal councils are split into two. One where the municipal council applies directly for projects under RM500,000 — we process it as usual, there's a process they need to apply through. But for the public, they can apply directly. When they apply directly, some people also ask, "I applied a long time ago, it's already approved, why hasn't it been built?" When they apply directly, we investigate and check — because among the public there are all sorts, including contractors applying for projects they don't actually need. So officers will verify that the project is genuine, that the location is correct. Once we've reviewed and approved it, we offer it to the district or municipal council, and ask them to carry it out. If they do, we give them the allocation. There are also places where we've approved something but the district council doesn't want to build it — my approach was, if a district council is being lazy like that — we've approved three, four projects for the public but they don't want to build them — fine, then their other applications, I won't approve. But because of that, the public applies directly. When the public applies — in Kedah's case, it's true, Kedah is almost 100% opposition, there are only two or three state assemblymen, one parliamentary seat. So people might not like it, because they see Kedah as 100% Perikatan Nasional, yet the Ministry of Economy is still approving allocations there. This is something I found unfair to myself — even within government, many people cursed me for it, PAS also cursed me. Yet we were just doing what's normal — if a project is genuinely needed and will benefit the people. That's why many people complained about me, went to complain to the PM and so on. I just swallowed it, pretended not to notice any of it.
