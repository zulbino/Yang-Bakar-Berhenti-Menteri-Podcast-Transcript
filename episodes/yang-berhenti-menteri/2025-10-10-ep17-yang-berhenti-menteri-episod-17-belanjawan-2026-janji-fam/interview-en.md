For the viewers' information, the budget documents presented usually consist of five main documents, while the sixth document is merely the text of the Finance Minister's speech, which is what people commonly read. For the next macro aspect, my attention turns to Development Expenditure (DE). For the viewers' information, government spending is divided into two main categories, similar to the concepts of OPEX and CAPEX in a private organization. Operating Expenditure (OE) is equivalent to OPEX, involving operating costs such as staff salaries, utility bills, and non-capital expenses that do not produce direct returns. Meanwhile, Development Expenditure (DE) is equivalent to CAPEX (capital expenditure), which involves investments to build assets or projects that can add value and generate new income for the country.

In the public sector, Operating Expenditure (OE) consists of several main components such as emoluments (civil servants' salaries), pensions, transfer payments to state governments and public agencies, and supplies (costs of office management, events, and utilities). Two other main components that are always the focus of the people are the *debt service charge*, which is the interest payment on government loans, and subsidies and social assistance (such as the Sumbangan Tunai Rahmah [STR] program, Sumbangan Asas Rahmah [SARA], JKM assistance, as well as fuel and electricity subsidies). Meanwhile, Development Expenditure (DE) is used to build schools, roads, hospitals, and to channel business assistance grants.

My main concern when looking at the budget documents presented earlier is that there are indications that, for the sake of reducing the national deficit rate, Development Expenditure (DE) is being sacrificed. Based on the performance in 2025, we face challenges in implementing development projects as planned. The DE allocation approved for 2025 was originally RM84.7 billion, but the revised projection presented earlier shows only RM78.7 billion is expected to be spent. This means there is RM6 billion in development allocation that was unspent. This situation could be due to the government's decision to restrict such spending to meet deficit targets, or the failure of implementing agencies to spend the allocation because projects did not pass the *milestones* set for payment claims. Both of these situations are actually a problem.

Under the provisions of the Federal Constitution, the government is not allowed to borrow or go into debt to finance Operating Expenditure (OE). Loans can only be made to finance Development Expenditure (DE). Therefore, the national deficit rate (measured as a percentage of Gross Domestic Product [GDP]) is closely linked to the amount of new debt issued for DE. For example, if the deficit rate is 5% of our economy's size, which is worth around RM1.7 trillion, the amount is roughly over RM80 billion. This increase in new debt will subsequently burden Operating Expenditure (OE) in the coming years through increased interest payments (*debt service charge*). Consequently, the government's room to borrow in the future will become narrower, thus shrinking the allocation for the country's physical development.

For 2026, the DE allocation is estimated at RM79.5 billion. Although there is a slight increase compared to the revised 2025 estimate (RM78.7 billion), this amount actually registers a 6% reduction when compared to the original planning of Budget 2025. A 6% reduction in development allocation within a single year is a very large amount. Yet, this DE allocation is crucial as a *catalyst* to stimulate industrial investment and the country's economic growth.

Another critical aspect that worries me deeply is the increase in the *debt service charge*. For 2025, the amount that needs to be paid for debt interest alone is RM54.3 billion. To understand whether this amount is large or small, we can compare it to the total Operating Expenditure (OE) for 2025, which is valued at RM332 billion, representing about 17%. As a simple analogy, if our monthly salary is RM2,000, 17% of that income (which is RM340) is used solely to pay credit card interest, excluding the principal payment. Such a financial situation is very dire. According to international financial standards, a country's debt service charge should not exceed 15% of total revenue. However, with interest payments of RM54.3 billion out of national revenue worth RM334 billion, we have clearly exceeded that 15% limit over the last year or two.

For 2026, these debt interest payments are expected to continue rising by 7% to RM58.3 billion, which is an increase of RM4 billion within a year. This means that despite all the difficulties faced in implementing subsidy targeting programs like Budi Madani to save around RM2.5 billion to RM4 billion annually, that savings amount is still insufficient to cover the increase in the country's annual debt interest payments, which surged by RM4 billion.

Finally, let's look at the subsidies and social assistance component. From one perspective, the government has indeed successfully reduced the country's subsidy commitments. From the records I recall, the country's subsidies and social assistance burden in 2023 was around over RM60 billion. This amount has decreased to around RM57 billion in 2025, and is projected to continue dropping by RM8 billion to RM49 billion for 2026.
