There are decisions that involve basic policy decisions. So when the policy decision is made, it must be brought to the cabinet and it must get approval from other ministries. In this government, the political dynamics are still there. If someone wants to change the status quo—plus if I am the Minister of Economy, which involves the policies of all ministries and complicated things—then the political dynamics are quite important.

If we look at the example that happened after the election in Keadilan, when Nurul Izzah contested, everyone knows that it was with the blessing of the President of the party, who is also the Prime Minister. When the Prime Minister gives his blessing for his candidate to compete in the presidential election, the indication is quite clear that the President of the party, who is also the Prime Minister, has lost confidence in me as a candidate, as the Deputy President, and also as the Minister of Economy.

So when the message is clear, it will be translated into the government that the Prime Minister has lost confidence in my position in the party and also as the Minister of Economy. And when we look at the election decision, especially the decision where those closely aligned with me were cut off, it gave an image to the government, civil servants, and other ministers that actually I and my team have been rejected by the party.

So when you have lost the confidence of the Prime Minister, and then you lose your mandate from the party, if suddenly you want to continue making major changes that require backing and support from other ministries and civil servants, other ministers will say, "Why should I rock the boat and go through all the trouble for someone who has lost confidence?" So, what for? Thus ultimately, if I remain, I would become a *lame duck* minister.

And as a minister who is seen to have lost the confidence of the Prime Minister himself and of his own party, if I continue wanting to do work that I believe impacts the country, it will drag down my ministry. Civil servants in my ministry would have to do all sorts of heavy lifting, only for it to be rejected in the end. Unless I wanted to be an "ornamental minister" who just clocks in and clocks out, taking a free salary without making any impact. *And that's not me.* I also have to consider the function and role of the Ministry of Economy so that it doesn't get dragged into this.

It's quite different between private companies and the government, because the government needs legitimacy to make changes. So if the message is clear, through this election, that the President and the Prime Minister have lost confidence in me, then for the interests of the nation, it is better to step down honorably.

**Rafizi:** Yes, I get a lot of messages and emails saying, "Oh, you have to stay to get the job done." First, at the risk of sounding repetitive, the dynamics of government are very important to understand. To carry out the work of reforming the economy, that's why the mandate is so important.

Having said that, what is the function of the Ministry of Economy? The Ministry of Economy is the macro planner—it plans for the *mid-term* and *long-term*. From the beginning, in December 2022, I informed the ministry that if you want to do reform, you must hit the ground running. For example, look at multi-tier levy management to reduce foreign workers—that decision was postponed for years. If you look at the major initiatives, I would say almost 80% of the initiative policy planning I set for the Ministry of Economy has received policy approval and been passed. The crucial matter of energy transition; *labor market reform* policy (progressive wage); strengthening the fiscal position such as targeted subsidies; transition towards government efficiency (Iltizam Act); the establishment of the Digital Ministry and *GovTech*; new growth engines such as the chip ecosystem, JS-SEZ, IPR to help public income, and IKR to facilitate public infrastructure allocations. **Interviewer:** YB, *having said that*, we understand the Ministry of Economy does a lot of macro planning. But there is a segment of society that feels YB's presence there didn't make any difference. How does YB respond to this criticism?

*Let the facts speak for themselves.* I hope the *reforms* we have made—involving the labor market, new growth engines, and targeted subsidies to strengthen fiscal positioning—will be continued to bring greater impact to the people.

*Managing the economy is a function of a few variables.* First, the economy must grow. We have achieved that in 2024.*

For me, the goal of being a politician isn't power or becoming a minister. *What exactly is politics?* There are various definitions, but one effective definition for me is: politics is the authoritative allocation of resources and values in society.

Meaning, how we ensure the distribution of resources—property, land, jobs, education, and health—occurs fairly, efficiently, *and sustainable for the long run*. And that can only happen *if we establish a government based on values*—noble values from Islam such as fairness, honesty, and diligence, which are also universal values. *So that is politics.*

**Rafizi:** To me, my understanding of what politics is: people have never said that politics is strictly the same as being the government, or being a prime minister, or a minister. Politics is basically the authoritative allocation of resources and values in society. So going forward, our societal model—where everyone looks at ministers, the Prime Minister, and politicians as a *magic wand* to solve all problems—is not *sustainable*. Because the resources to be allocated are getting fewer over time, and things are getting harder.

There are more and more constraints. People will say, "We want healthcare like this—one, two, three, four—just go and get this and that." The population is growing. Expectations for healthcare quality are rising, but nobody is willing to pay taxes.

So going forward, for example, not only regarding financial resources, we will have a problem regarding the size of the workforce. Now people are angry, saying that we can't get a job and so on. Actually, we at the Ministry of Economy—one of our biggest concerns is that in 20 years, we will have a shortage of workers because the birth rate is getting lower.

And these younger people either marry later or, if they marry, have fewer children. So this is what's called the *sandwich generation*. This is the context of the *sandwich generation*. We will reach a time when the workforce coming from Gen Z, a bit of Millennials, Gen Z 20 years from now, Gen Alpha, and Gen Beta—their numbers will be small. But they will have to go through life bearing increasingly high costs. At the same time, there will be older groups from Gen X and Boomers who are still alive, who are no longer in the workforce, and who need to be funded by the social system—their healthcare, their welfare, and so on. So it's a double burden on them.

So, the current model that relies 100% on the government to do everything and take care of people's welfare—I don't think it's sustainable in the next 20 to 30 years. That's why, if you look at models in other countries, people have started talking about the *third sector*.

We have the first sector, the public sector, the government sector. We have the second sector, the private sector. For decades, we have only talked about the public sector and the private sector. In countries that are more advanced than us, they started talking about the third sector.

The *third sector* actually refers to ordinary citizens—ordinary people who have skills and economic sustainability in providing services and provisions to the public that were previously borne by the government, including creating job opportunities, healthcare, and so on. The difference is, the third sector is a quasi-public sector alongside the private sector. You must run it financially sustainably. You can't just collect donations and give things out for free; services must be paid for, but it is not driven by profit and wealth.

So, if people ask about politics, about whether jobs are increasing or not, I think let the facts speak for themselves. I also think that the only reason I wanted to be in politics before is so that I can influence matters that can bring good to society. I never believed that being a politician meant becoming Prime Minister or a Minister.

There are many more spaces that I think we can add value to. And I don't think we should pay attention to detractors. Detractors are easy to speak—that's their view. What's more important is: the more you have a good track record, the more you touch people's lives, the more people get benefit from your work. For me, I think that's my ultimate goal as a politician. Actually, I derive satisfaction from having an impact on common people's lives, and I'm a lot more comfortable doing that than being stuck on top where I feel my hands are tied and my mouth is locked.
