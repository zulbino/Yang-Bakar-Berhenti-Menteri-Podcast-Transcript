**Rafizi:** 10 to 15 years ago, when I began exposing scandals and corruption, my primary aim wasn't just demonstrating personal integrity. My concern was that society, particularly the Malay community, viewed corruption passively—thinking as long as they received some benefit, it was acceptable.

That was why I focused exposure on Malay-majority institutions—1MDB, MARA, Tabung Haji, Felda—to demonstrate that corruption is a destructive sin that oppresses ordinary people. Targeting lower-level officers wouldn't change public awareness, so we focused on high-profile politicians.

**Zaim:** Why does that mindset and culture exist? Isn't it shaped by the underlying system and its leaders?

**Rafizi:** You can design the most perfect system, but if the people operating within it do not fundamentally believe in its principles, they will always find loopholes. In many Western societies, there is a deep-seated cultural consensus that taking what isn't yours is inherently wrong. They don't rely solely on exhaustive institutional checklists because dishonesty is culturally unacceptable.

If a Prime Minister installs systems without changing societal values, corruption persists. If society rewards shortcuts to quick wealth—whether through politics or speculative schemes—over decades of hard work, people will pursue shortcuts. That is a cultural issue.

However, given that Malaysian society is patriarchal, leadership remains vital. A leader must set the standard and be "whiter than white." If the leadership lacks moral authority, you cannot transform the culture. The broader challenge is reforming public mindset so that society genuinely rejects corruption, reducing reliance on enforcement bodies like SPRM.

**Zaim:** So the education system plays a key role in shaping that mindset, right?

**Rafizi:** The system refers to the culture and also the setting of our society. It involves everything from our businesses, our success models, our schools, and so on.
