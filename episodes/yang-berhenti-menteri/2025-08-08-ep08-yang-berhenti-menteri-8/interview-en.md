# Interview (English)

**Moderator:** *Assalamualaikum* and welcome to the eighth episode of the *Yang Berhenti Menteri Sudah* podcast. Today, we are joined by our usual panelist, Dr. Irwan from the International Islamic University Malaysia (IIUM). How are you, Doctor?

**Dr. Irwan:** *Alhamdulillah*, I am doing well.

**Moderator:** It’s quite interesting; when I checked the comments on our social media, I saw so many IIUM students saying, "That's my lecturer!" and "The best!" It seems your strategy of attracting students to watch the podcast is working! Please, go ahead.

**Dr. Irwan:** Okay, a little introduction about myself. My name is Muhammad Irwan Arifin. I have been with IIUM since 2004. I graduated from IIUM with my first degree in Economics, a second degree in Islamic Studies, and I completed my Master’s and PhD in Economics. I returned to serve IIUM in 2015, and *Alhamdulillah*, I am still there.

**Moderator:** My younger sibling was at IIUM around the same time, but they studied computing or ICT. Doctor, how does one specialize in Economics?

**Dr. Irwan:** To be honest, I still couldn't say I am an expert in any one specific area. I am still exploring different fields, including Islamic economics. I tend to look at myself as a versatile teacher. Whatever the head of the department assigns, I just say "yes" and give it a try because I really love to learn.

**Moderator:** That sounds very Malaysian. I had an ex-staff member, a specialist doctor, whom I met at a hospital after many years. I asked him, "Aren't you a specialist?" He replied, "Yes, but I'm a specialist, a GP, and a campaign aid." That is very Malaysian. 

Anyway, let's move on to the 13th Malaysia Plan (RMK13). YB Rafizi, in your post after the 13th Malaysia Plan was read, you mentioned that 95% was kept and 5% was...

**Rafizi Ramli:** Actually, it was just a slogan change.

**Moderator:** Just the slogan?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, I think so. If you look at it, most of the changes were editorial or cosmetic. 

**Moderator:** Depends on what you mean by "cosmetic."

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don't pay that much attention to design; I focus on substance. If there is a change, it’s substantive, which is a good thing because it takes a long time to prepare and requires a lot of input. Initially, I was concerned about radical changes, but the process has involved a lot of back-and-forth. Pushing the needle is often met with resistance because people want to maintain the status quo. 

**Moderator:** Doctor, if you want to interject or ask questions, please do. Don't let me be the only moderator! 

**Dr. Irwan:** Good, I'll do my work. RMK1 through RMK13—how has it evolved? It moved from agriculture and poverty eradication to industrialization. To summarize, what is the main message of the RMK13 compared to the previous ones?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The answer should come from me, as I am one of the architects. But as an outsider looking at it, Dr. Irwan, this is the first five-year plan by the new government. It seems to follow global economic growth trends. We started with agriculture to escape the poverty line and become a developing country. At the same time, we saw some brave social engineering—like the New Economic Policy—which carried risks, but the previous leadership managed to navigate it, though it wasn't perfect. 

The difference with this RMK13 is that we are pushing to reach developed nation status. We have been trapped in the middle-income status for a long time, and we are still looking for that catalyst. I see a few steps in this plan that show we are serious about leaping forward.

**Dr. Irwan:** It’s not perfect, but it’s like medicine for long-term health. Behavioral economics tells us that human agents are short-sighted; we think of immediate pleasure and forget that we have to make sacrifices today. I salute the government for the risks taken in this plan.

**Rafizi Ramli:** If you read the previous RMK documents, the diagnosis is the same. We have been trapped in the middle-income bracket for over 20 years. Everyone wants to cross that line to become a high-income nation, but the approach in RMK13 is different. Politicians usually view the RMK as a list of projects, which is why debates in Parliament often devolve into, "Why isn't this project included?" 

However, we have fundamental structural problems. The reason we can't leap forward is the talent pool, which links back to education and future investment. RMK13 wants to reach the deepest root causes. I hope it’s seen not as a list of projects, but as a roadmap for structural reform. 

**Dr. Irwan:** It’s a new approach and very challenging to change the mindset. I see an emphasis on "intergenerational equity"—looking at the well-being of the next generation. Subsidy rationalization is very risky, but brave and necessary. We shouldn't sacrifice the future for our current appetite (tumbolok). Even in Islamic economics, we are reminded to think about long-term benefits. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** That is the spirit we are trying to convey. We cannot "kick the can down the road" forever. We (Gen X and early Millennials) are starting to feel the burden of debt. One thing I've been criticized for recently—mostly by the youth—is the proposed retirement age moving toward 65 and the move to mandatory preschool at age 5. 

**Dr. Irwan:** You're mentioning early schooling and retirement at 65. Perhaps it’s not just about the numbers, but explaining the "why." Digital-native generations have shorter attention spans and look for instant gratification.

**Rafizi Ramli:** In the RMK13, we tried to make it clear. Education investment—specifically early childcare—is the most important factor. Our brains are most flexible at that age. If we start school early, students can join the workforce by 16. I know the Gen Z and Gen Alpha groups are upset, thinking we're trying to take their jobs or overwork them. But if we don't do this, they will bear an even heavier burden 20 to 30 years from now.

**Dr. Irwan:** Perhaps they see the direct impact—difficulty finding a job or underemployment—so they feel it immediately.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Exactly. One radical change in RMK13 is the university target. Previously, the target was "marketability," which is why it was always 96-97%. It was disconnected from reality. We have discarded "marketability" as a KPI for higher education and replaced it with "matching employment." We don't want a student to just have *a* job; we want them to get the *right* job within the right salary band.

**Dr. Irwan:** That will be a big challenge for education providers, but it’s the right challenge. We must ensure our graduates are worthy of their positions. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** That’s also why I insisted the RMK document be concise—no more than 200 pages. People don't read 500-page reports. The main diagnosis in RMK13 is the disillusionment caused by the failure of social mobility. When I was young, if you excelled in school, you could support your family and improve your life. That is no longer the case. That is the fundamental problem we are trying to fix.

**Dr. Irwan:** Education is the key to breaking the poverty cycle, but there is an asymmetry between economic growth and salary growth. Wages are stubborn, even if the country is rich.

**Rafizi Ramli:** There is no "silver bullet." We have to look at our economic structure. Our largest employers are micro and small enterprises (PMKS). We cannot mandate a RM3,000 minimum wage for graduates without the underlying value creation, or we will destroy the economy. We must invest in sectors that can offer higher wages—energy transition, advanced electronics, digital economy, and critical minerals.

**Dr. Irwan:** That's true, but we should also leverage our culture. Our "third sector"—philanthropy, *wakaf*, and *zakat*—is unique. We are raised to help one another. If we can codify this, it could complement government efforts.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I agree. That is why we are building the "Ayu Malaysia" concept to codify these social enterprise models. But there is one thing I haven't seen anyone notice in the RMK13: the aging society. By 2050, the government won't be able to bear the full cost of caring for the elderly. However, we have a cultural advantage: children giving money to their parents. We need to formalize this in our tax structure. If we can provide tax breaks to those who support their parents, we incentivize this positive cultural practice and reduce the burden on the state. 

**Dr. Irwan:** It’s a great idea to quantify and acknowledge those contributions. YB, regarding your thinking process—you’ve seen countries like Japan and Korea struggle with aging, despite being rich. We are much poorer than they were when they started aging. How much time do we have?

**Rafizi Ramli:** We only have about 17 years. It’s scary. That is why I am a macro planner; I look at the numbers. We are already late. We should have started this 10 years ago. I even suggested child benefits, but it wasn't approved. However, by providing universal preschool and other services in the RMK, we help reduce the cost of living for young families, making it easier for them to start a family. That is the goal.

**Haziq:** Psychologically, when a young family wants to get married and have children, they don't care about what is given in kind. They think that their salary is RM3,000–RM4,000 per month, and they can't eat their children's food. So actually, cash incentive, in my opinion, Prof, is important for childcare.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Because some of the burden can be removed through the provision of universal services—especially childcare and preschool now. I hope they will do it. Things are already in motion; don't try to take shortcuts. But even then, to be fair to families, it’s about the necessities like pampers and milk. And actually, if there were a child benefit—RM100 or RM300 per month—even if it doesn't help 100%, at least we provide a small cushion; it prevents that psychological barrier seen in other countries. It's the cost-benefit analysis that leads people to think, "having a child isn't viable," and then you are done. But maybe if you are wearing the government hat, that one will be off because of the child. So, whatever happens, they are likely calculating the life cycle. When they are young, they might be in a deficit and need assistance, but when they grow up, they will help to contribute back. They pay it back, right? Because I return to your question earlier, Haziq—the experience of other countries—I haven't found a country that has transitioned into an "old country" successfully because of the fertility problem. That's a global issue, right? I haven't found a country that has succeeded in improving the fertility issue with married people. Do whatever you want, raise the money however you want; the moment the younger generation feels that it's not worth it, it doesn't matter. There are things like economic growth and wages that you can play around with via macro policy—fiscal and monetary policy. But when it comes to marriage and having children, you cannot do that. It’s all internal; it’s all emotional. You cannot manage generational emotion and behavioral economics with these fiscal policies.

**Haziq:** Before it reaches that point?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I think it is in our best interest as a society to provide incentives early. So that we don't reach the stage of Gen Beta—let's say we are at Gen Alpha now—where the marriage rate drops drastically, then fertility drops drastically, and the replacement rate is finished. Once you reach that, no matter what study you do, the concern of an aging nation is there. I would also like to give credit that in the 13th Malaysia Plan (RMK13), there seems to be a firm promise from the government regarding the deficit. I think that’s a very good move; it means we are binding ourselves. If we want to be indebted, we have to ensure it is for development expenditure and things like that. So I think that’s perhaps one of the ways to somehow minimize or cushion the economy while the country is growing. Is it really my idea? No, I think it was there from the beginning, but when we came in at the end of 2022... I'm not an economist, though. But of course, I went through it; I’m a chartered accountant. So if you do ICAW, economics is part of the module. You don't go into the details, but you roughly understand. People like me understand income statements and balance sheets more than anything else—that’s our life. 

Look at the government's income statement. The tax revenue is around RM330 billion. Even that is already hit; it really is said to be RM330 billion. When that RM330 billion is collected, RM46 to RM48 billion is used just to pay interest. So, even if you ask me as an individual, even if our deficit isn't that bad—say 3% or 4%, which is okay—my argument is that I would prefer to save RM5–6 billion from debt repayments and spend it on the people. Instead of paying RM46 billion, let's just pay RM40 billion so that RM6 billion can be given to us. You can give us allowances and all that. Even before we go into the nitty-gritty of deficits, if we manage our income like a family—paying credit cards until RM4,500 a month in interest—it's crazy, even if your salary is RM20,000–RM30,000. It’s a waste. So, from the very beginning, to me, before we talk about rationalization or subsidies, everything must be done with the view of really pushing down that service charge. That RM46 billion a year is about 16% of income. But actually, the fiscal glide isn't just in RMK13; in July 2023 and the Mid-Term Review of the 12th Malaysia Plan in early 2024, the fiscal glide was already mandated. So, from 5% to 4%, by 2025 it should be 3.5%. Because if we continue with that fiscal glide, only in 2028 will our debt-to-GDP ratio be around 60%. When that happens, by that time the GDP will be larger, and at least our debt service charge—the RM46 billion which is now 16%—we can bring it down a bit. It has been codified as an act. That’s why the credit should go even earlier than the 13th plan. We have to do that because, as the Minister of Economy, every month there are fund managers from around the world—from New York, Tokyo—who manage trillions of dollars.

The message to every government, every minister is simple. I am sure about before me and after me. There’s this one fund manager, I think he looked after trillions of dollars; he said, "We like what we hear and what we are seeing from a fund because we are simple people. We ask, although we are analysts, we go through all these numbers, but we are very simple people. We like governments that think long-term." Deficit is just kicking the can down the road so that the younger generation carries this burden. So, if anything, I’m still worried. That’s what I said in Parliament; this isn't politics. It’s okay to protest or to fight, but my hope is that anyone who takes the government does not just get into debt and spend money. Which is not popular, but you need to do it. You have to crush it; it’s part and parcel of our job. The problem is, we are worried that if we change the government, they will be in trouble and we will go through that cycle again. But if we stay like this, by 2028, I think we will achieve the fiscal glide we want.

**Haziq:** YB, a quick one. Maybe this is out of nowhere, but regarding your speech in the debate—which might have been a bit rushed—about the allocation for education. You mentioned we spend a lot on infrastructure, building things. If we look back at the "Ayo Malaysia 2022" campaign, you brought up five pillars—I’ve forgotten them, what were they? One of them was the democratization of education. Actually, your diagnosis was quite similar; you said we spend too much on infrastructure. You suggested the solution was to shift toward digital learning. Why doesn't that seem so bright in this RMK? Has the diagnosis changed?

**Rafizi Ramli:** When we have limited resources—I mean, economics is the art and science of resource allocation—we have to ensure that the focus for those five years is given to the biggest impact. Digital is a decisive basis. Education is there, too. Anyone who has children at school knows there are many shortcomings, but those things are in place. If we look at the ratio of education allocation compared to the size of the economy, Malaysia is actually on par with many upper-middle-income or even high-income nations. We do spend on education. I’m not saying building schools is wrong, because part of it is due to under-investment from years ago. Outside the Klang Valley, you might not feel it because class sizes aren't that bad—maybe 20-something, that's normal. But in the Klang Valley, 50 or even 60 students in one school is common. Schools are definitely not enough. So I understand when the PM says we have to build vertical schools. When I say we have to balance physical investment, it doesn’t mean I’m anti-school. That’s why in 2023, 2024, and 2025, the Ministry of Economy approved dozens of new schools every year. But there is a "but." Even if the school is beautiful and the syllabus is as good as any in the world, you need the teachers more than anything else. The real resource of education is the teachers. We cannot tell the teachers, "Sorry, we want to build schools first, you figure it out yourselves." Because that is inefficient. If we want to change the syllabus, we’ve done that so many times—we’ve asked them to learn English, and so on. The real thing I don’t think we’ve tried properly is giving the budget to train teachers so that teacher training reaches the same level as other professionals like engineers. I give my example: even though I don’t practice as a chartered accountant, I still have to submit my training form every year—CPD, continuous professional development. It has to be done.

For teachers in Malaysia, we need to visualize that they are trained in such a way, with modules and so on. We don't have to do it at expensive hotels or build a new college. We have many teacher training institutes (IPG). So, in the 13th plan, we finally believe we got the agreement. Part of the IPG must be changed completely to become a professional institute, training teachers in a modular and flexible way. If before this, it might have been done more ad hoc, if they have to spend six months, they need to be trained properly. When we have a dedicated part of the IPG—meaning there is a budget and resources—over time, our teachers will be trained so they aren't constantly chased. Otherwise, every time there is a new minister or a new government, the new teachers are told, "Oh, this is all right." That is what is different with RMK13. If we manage to put that process in place, starting now, a good education system depends on the resource and development you put into the "software"—and that software is the teaching power, the teacher. I think if we manage to improve the quality of our teachers, other things like digital and syllabus quality will improve immediately as a direct result.

**Haziq:** You mentioned the minister's role earlier—that we shouldn't be experts, but rather managers of resources and people.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, our job as politicians is to defend the experts. I mean, no minister is an expert in everything. We have advantages: resources, officers, experts, and the mandate to make decisions. So you listen to everything. When you have agreed and you think the direction is right, you make a decision, and the minister’s responsibility is to defend the people who have done this job. If a minister is afraid of being fired, they will have a problem. "Everything good is here, but I will be criticized." Well, that's a minister's job—to be criticized! You have to defend all the staff and stakeholders in the back who are doing the real work. Your real work is getting the "buy-in" and managing the differences in opinion so that those in the back can work. You can change the government, people come and go, these politicians. But if you know your core job is to defend the good people doing a good job, for me, whoever becomes a minister should be okay.

**Haziq:** Maybe if I may ask, regarding RMK13, there are external factors that determine the success of everything. Among them is the new announcement of 100% tariffs on semiconductors, even though we don't know the details from the US government. How do we actually navigate that?

**Rafizi Ramli:** [Donald Trump] is unpredictable, and he is willing to gamble everything. So I think it has led to what we can see as "economic bullying." But I think perhaps we have to be aware that when you bully, once you start to give in, the bully will continue because they know you will fold. If we are very firm but don't have the resources to defend what will happen to us, that carries risk. Since we are a seller, it seems like a biased market with the way negotiation happens and the threats given; there is an imbalance of power. Honestly, I don't have the answer to it. We continue to pray, because we don't have the upper hand. We want to sell our goods, and the buyer seems to have the power. If they say, "It's too difficult, I don't want to buy from you," we are the ones who lose. I think we should think—we have already made our goods, and we want to sell them. So it's important that those leading the negotiations know how to balance how much we should compromise. 

If you look at RMK13, I’ve been observing, and not one opposition member has brought up a substantive issue about the "fault lines" of RMK13. Everything is based on simulations and projections of economic growth between 3.5% to 4.5%. From that growth, you get the revenue projections, debt, and everything else. Even before the 100% chip tariff story—when it was the first round of 25% tariffs—the ASEAN economy was expected to grow by only around 3%. So I’m waiting, I’m usually waiting—who wants to ask? Nobody asked. Actually, if anyone asked what the fault line of RMK13 is, it’s whether, if the tariff war continues to impair global trade, will we be able to reach at least 3.5%? And honestly, I wouldn't be surprised if the first-year impact, in 2026 or 2027, drags, because it has a knock-on effect on the world economy. If our growth is 3%, all these beautiful plans are out the window. That’s without even mentioning oil prices.

**Haziq:** And regarding the Tax collection? You emphasize transparency and integrity, yet the current system seems to favor the SST regime. GST is theoretically more transparent. Why the disconnect? Is it political?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I don’t know what the view in the cabinet is now, but throughout my time in the cabinet, most discussions on GST were between the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Economy, and the PM. My view is that it’s actually a matter of fairness and equity. At the end of the day, GST is regressive. The one who pays is the people. In the UK, the first interest we are taught is VAT before income tax. To me, the simple thing is: for equity, you have to look at the total burden of a family's interest before and after. If a household in Malaysia has an income of RM3,000, it doesn't pay income tax. But the moment you implement GST, you impose a burden on things they buy every day. So there is that moral debate. Secondly, the issue with GST—and this is the experience worldwide, and we saw it in 2015—is that any country that implements it sees a jump in inflation. If GST is 6%, you see 6%–7% inflation. In Malaysia, once prices go up, they rarely go down. If you trace the current price spikes, a lot of it is what we inherited from the 2015–2016 period, when there was a spike of up to 5%–6% inflation. In the current situation, we want to maintain inflation below 2% so that as we push for labor market reform and wage growth, we can control the cost of living. If you do GST now, it tears everything apart. I hope that 4–5 years down the line, when we have already bridged that gap, the government will have more policy options.

**Haziq:** YB, lastly, because you want to return home. Regarding the 2031 nuclear energy target in RMK13—some say it is very ambitious.

**Rafizi Ramli:** It is ambitious. When people say 10 years for nuclear SMR (Small Modular Reactor), that includes, for example, the ratification of international treaties. Usually, our process to ratify international treaty acts takes 2–3 years. If you can cut some of the processes, 2–3 years here and there, and the technology for SMR gets better, it’s possible. But it's about capability building. In the end, it has to go through business viability. We don't want to be in a position where we need it but we aren't ready because there is no law, no compliance, no infrastructure. So, I think the next few years are for preparing all those frameworks. It’s reasonable. In fact, my advice is, even if we say we must have nuclear, you still have to watch the dollar. If introducing nuclear SMR in our energy mix causes tariffs to rise by 20%–30%, the government of the day must make the decision: do you want to pass this cost to the consumer just to have a nuclear mix? RMK13 is meant to allow all the framework and infrastructure to be in place so that when we decide we need it, we can "plug and play."
