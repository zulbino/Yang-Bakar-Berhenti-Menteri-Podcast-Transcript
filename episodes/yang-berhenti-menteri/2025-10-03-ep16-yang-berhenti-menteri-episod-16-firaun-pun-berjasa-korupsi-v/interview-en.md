**Rafizi:** This isn't in Malaysia, but in India. When I was small, there was a running joke that doctors have the worst handwriting. **Interviewer:** If YB had to give a star rating for these four issues from 1 to 5, what would you give?

**Interviewer:** Alright YB, let's move on to the next segment: "Hidup Keras" [Hard Life]. We begin with the first topic—combating corruption: a real struggle or merely political theater?

**Rafizi:** Promising to catch big sharks and clean up corruption within 2 to 3 years. Now we're in the third year. This topic will definitely get me slammed by cybertroopers later.

**Interviewer:** I put forward two ironic premises. First, compared to previous administrations, there are indeed big sharks or small sharks being investigated and arrested by today's government. However, based on the data YB shared previously, corruption perception among the public remains worrying and the country's Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) hasn't improved much. Why does this gap exist?

**Rafizi:** We start with the CPI (Corruption Perceptions Index) developed by Transparency International. It is widely used across the world to measure stakeholders' views—including ordinary citizens and local or international business communities—on corruption practices.

It is true that our CPI hasn't changed much. YAB Prime Minister, Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim, often feels disappointed when people bring up corruption because in his view, the situation is actually much better. That is the challenge facing any government. We may feel we've done many initiatives, but the main benchmark outside our control is the CPI. Improving the CPI is also one of the primary goals of MADANI Economy.

When the CPI appears barely changed—only up slightly compared to the previous PN government—it means the public does not feel a significant change. This becomes a target for criticism because PKR and Pakatan Harapan campaigned for over 20 years to eradicate corruption.

The public and the business community judge based on their environment. If those participating in government procurement feel the process is the same as before, they won't feel any change. If they see only big sharks from certain groups being targeted, while new sharks or other cronies escape and become even bolder, they will view this as pure rhetoric.

For example, in government procurement—every time there is a change of government, if new elites and cronies associated with the ruling party begin to dominate, the public will see the same thing repeating. Previously Party A got rich fast, now it's Party B because Party B became the government. The characters—brokers and runners—have merely changed shirts.

That's why during the recent PKR election, I voiced a reminder. The public's hope in PKR is that such things will not happen. When figures suddenly become rich or former political secretaries secure contracts, the public will question it.

Throughout my tenure as Minister, I have stopped talking about the "sakau" [plundering] issue. Before becoming government, we could say this needed to change. But after three years, if the Prime Minister is still shouting about "sakau", the public's logic is: "You've been there for three years to change it. Why are you still talking like the opposition?"

The same goes for enforcement action. Datuk Seri Anwar cannot merely say that MACC/SPRM is now brave enough to investigate the family of figure A or Tan Sri B under his administration. If the perception arises that figures or parties within the government itself are not acted against when linked to project issues, the anti-corruption message will lose its impact.

**Interviewer:** On the topic of corruption as well, MACC recently uncovered a tire smuggling case in "Ops Grip". However, the reaction from some netizens on social media was quite shocking—some said "The public loses out because they can't get cheap tires" or "At least he helped people by selling at cheap prices." Does this indicate a deep-rooted perception problem in our society?

**Rafizi:** There are two distinct matters here. First, this cheap tire issue is a matter of supply, demand, and economic competitiveness. We cannot achieve competitiveness by breaking the law. Laws exist to ensure competition takes place on a level playing field.

The correct way to bring down prices is through economies of scale so that unit costs are low, or through technological advancement. Other countries with low corruption levels develop their economies and provide reasonable prices to the public through these methods.

Our society's attitude sometimes stems from a misconception. When I first became active in politics again around 2009/2010 and exposed various corruption scandals, the common answer I received from part of the Malay community was: "It's okay to have corruption, at least the Chinese aren't getting it."

Corruption is theft and haram [forbidden]. If society considers corruption acceptable as long as their own race benefits, or that breaking the law is fine as long as they get low prices, there is a serious value crisis in our society.

This is a shared responsibility. Religious figures and ustaz have a massive role to educate the public from a young age that corruption is taking what belongs to others and earning haram money. We cannot build a competitive ummah on a foundation of illicit wealth.

The second matter is the issue of crime and tax fraud. The bust involved used tires imported as new tires but falsely declared (under-declared) as used or counterfeit tires to evade import duties.

When declarations are made at low values, the duties and taxes paid to the government become extremely low. That's why those traders could sell them cheaply. Meanwhile, honest traders who follow the law are forced to pay full duties, making their selling prices higher.

This is a dishonest act that harms the government, the public, and other law-abiding traders. There are many loopholes in import declarations at our ports and borders that enforcement agencies must stamp out.

**Interviewer:** Recently, revelations surfaced concerning action taken against a news portal and the issue of a network of 263 cyber accounts allegedly promoting the Prime Minister. Although the Communications Minister has denied government involvement, how should the public evaluate this issue?

**Rafizi:** As a party person and someone who observes social media, I was not surprised at all because I have experienced it myself.

About four or five months ago, during a party election campaign speech, I joked about the existence of "PKR Vietnam Branch". When I checked my TikTok account around last March to understand the communication gap with the audience, I was shocked to find that my posts received high likes from accounts in Vietnam.

When my team conducted further checks, the same trend occurred on content by several other ministers as well as the Prime Minister's account. This means there are certain parties employing unethical tactics like this—purchasing troll farm services or fake accounts from abroad.

During the PKR election campaign, even though my content coverage and reach were far higher organically, this same shadow group was detected giving likes to my opponent's account.

Such unethical practices unfortunately occur in almost every era of government. During Datuk Seri Najib Razak's era, exposures showed that his Facebook followers came from accounts in Pakistan. These cybertrooper companies sell bot accounts or troll farms to anyone willing to pay.

My view is that this method has never succeeded in shaping real public opinion. The public isn't stupid. The greatest danger to a top leader like Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim upon taking office is the risk of living in a "bubble".

When you become Prime Minister, wherever you go, people bow and smile. Events are flooded with thousands of people assembled by officers. Add to that fake account reports on social media showing that he is immensely popular, and it creates an illusion or a mirage. You think you see water, but it isn't there.

My advice to PKR supporters who might be responsible for doing this: if this is your way of flattering the Prime Minister, it doesn't help at all. It only expands the bubble of illusion when reality on the ground is vastly different.

**Interviewer:** YB, recently the Prime Minister's statement that "Even Pharaoh contributed" also caught attention. Could YB comment?

**Rafizi:** The context the Prime Minister was trying to convey is that even if someone has contributions, if they break the law, action must still be taken. Principally, no one can dispute that. I'm not sure if the term "Pharaoh" was a cynical reference to Tun Dr. Mahathir Mohamad, given that during the Reformasi era he was labeled "Maha Firaun".

However, if government messaging continues to rely on the narrative that "the super-wealthy must be fought" yet action appears focused only on two or three political enemies, while wealthy cronies constantly close to whichever government remain untouched, the public will grow tired.

I joked with Datuk Seri Anwar around 2013 when Saifuddin Nasution and Tan Sri Johari Abdul were together with us. I said: "Datuk Seri, right now we can be close because we're in opposition. Later if Datuk Seri becomes Prime Minister, those of us who fought will have to take queue numbers 450 and above to see you, because the elites and the rich will be queuing up at the front first." He just laughed back then.

The public evaluates justice based on the rule of law (rule-based). If the law is enforced strictly against political enemies under the pretext of "even if they contributed, if they're wrong they must face consequences", but wealthy tycoons or cronies who are now close friends of the government are forgiven on the grounds of "they also contributed", then that principle is tainted by double standards.

This is Datuk Seri Anwar's greatest challenge. The same goes for retaining Tan Sri Azam Baki at MACC. Before GE15 in 2022, there was the shareholding issue that clearly violated public service regulations. When the government retained him, any anti-corruption message delivered by the Prime Minister automatically lost its bite and legitimacy.

**Interviewer:** Some would argue that YB's view is impractical. In realpolitik, considerations and compromises must be made for the sake of government stability. If we are too rigid with rule-based principles, a government might not form or function. What is YB's view?

**Rafizi:** PKR is not a minor party; we are a core party in government and Datuk Seri Anwar is the Prime Minister. I know the Prime Minister sometimes feels frustrated because he feels public expectations regarding transparency and reform are too high and impractical in political reality.

However, my answer is simple: That's precisely why the public chose us. The public chose us because they believed we were different from other parties after decades of us campaigning.

If, upon gaining power, we offer excuses like "when in Rome, do as the Romans do", the public will conclude that we are no different from previous parties. We can choose to be pragmatic like that, but suffer the consequences when we lose the next election because the public feels betrayed.

**Interviewer:** We move on to the segment "Dunia Punya Hal" [World Affairs]. Regarding the Donald Trump administration's plan for the future of Gaza, is it acceptable for "the greater good"?

**Rafizi:** I think the answer is unacceptable to the majority of the world's population, except Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu themselves.

In early-stage negotiations, it involved the Trump administration and representatives of Muslim nations like Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkiye, and Egypt, where some common ground existed. However, in subsequent negotiations between the US and Israel, Israel unilaterally altered those terms.

Among other things, Israel demanded veto power over any decision and made troop withdrawal from Gaza conditional upon the complete elimination of Hamas. This means withdrawal will not happen as long as Israel claims Hamas still exists.

The proposal to appoint former British Prime Minister Tony Blair as governor or administrator of Gaza also faced fierce backlash. The British public themselves are angry with Blair's track record of colluding with George W. Bush to invade Iraq in 2003 under false claims of Weapons of Mass Destruction (WMDs). In fact, his firm, the Tony Blair Institute, reportedly already has commercial plans to redevelop Gaza as a real estate project.

For the Palestinian people, this feels like déjà vu. After World War I, Palestine was made a British Protectorate. It was during that time that British Foreign Secretary Lord Balfour, through the Balfour Declaration, arbitrarily promised Palestinian land as a homeland for the Jewish people without regard for the rights of the local population. That was the root of today's Zionist regime.

Trump's plan shows zero sincerity. If the US and Trump were truly sincere about resolving the humanitarian crisis in Gaza, the first fundamental step required would be forcing Israel to end the blockade immediately so humanitarian aid and food can enter. Without lifting that blockade, any plan allegedly for peace is futile.

**Interviewer:** Wisma Putra in its statement welcomed the United States' efforts toward ending the war in Gaza. Was that statement appropriate?

**Rafizi:** Diplomatic language is like that. A government must consider various diplomatic factors and trade relations with major powers like the United States. Welcoming "efforts to end the war" is standard diplomatic language. However, if the Malaysian government were to go so far as to support the physical plan for Gaza proposed by Trump, that would be a major mistake.

**Interviewer:** Regarding the Humanitarian Flotilla mission to Gaza, recently 15 Malaysians participating in the mission were arrested by the Israeli regime and deported back via Europe. What is the most appropriate action or stance Malaysia should take?

**Rafizi:** The government's highest priority is ensuring the safety and return of our citizens. These brave activists have already achieved their goal of shaking the world's conscience.

The reaction of local politicians is typical—each trying to hijack the issue to show who is the greatest champion. But the real effort that needs to be mobilized is ensuring this flotilla movement doesn't stop here. We need to nurture more volunteers ready to join the next wave of flotillas.

The short-term goal of the flotilla is delivering humanitarian aid. Its long-term goal is building global human solidarity to pressure the Israeli regime.

The history of the past 70 years shows that most major military conflicts do not end with absolute victory on the battlefield. The Korean War in 1951 ended in a stalemate, resulting in today's Demilitarized Zone (DMZ). The Indo-Pakistani War and the Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988) also ended without a victor.

The US military was eventually forced to withdraw from the Vietnam War, and later from Iraq and Afghanistan, not purely due to military defeat, but because of domestic and international public opposition. The same applied to ending Apartheid in South Africa.

Major conflicts usually end when global public sentiment shifts drastically. If this Global Flotilla mobilized around 40 ships, I hope the next wave will see 100 to 500 ships sail. When more citizens from various countries are arrested and deported, world governments will no longer have a choice but to take a firm stand against Israel.

I honestly feel ashamed of myself if I merely beat my chest making fiery statements about Palestine from afar, while international activist friends—including Westerners and Jews—are far braver facing risks on the high seas. As a minister, I am bound by the official government stand. But as an individual, if I am not as brave as those flotilla activists, I have no right to claim to be the greatest champion of Palestine. Our duty here is to continue praying, supporting, and contributing to the next wave of aid.

**Interviewer:** Alright YB. Let's enter the technology segment, "Fuiyoh". This week we have a video showing a four-legged anti-gravity robot made by China's Unitree company that can automatically stand back up after falling. YB yourself visited Unitree's office in China while serving as Minister of Economy. Could YB share your experience?

**Rafizi:** When I visited China to attract investment to the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone (JS-SEZ), I spent time visiting Unitree's headquarters in Hangzhou.

I asked the Ministry of Economy team which company was at the forefront of robotics technology in China. The answer was Unitree. Its founder is a young man with a PhD who established the company at around age 22. When I met him, he was wearing just a t-shirt, jeans, and slippers in a very modest factory building.

Yet Unitree is one of the top three robotics companies in the world, competing closely with Western firms like Boston Dynamics. In terms of robot sales volume, Unitree actually sells more units.

Hangzhou is China's tech innovation hub—from Hangzhou came giants like Alibaba, Geely (Proton's owner), the robotics company Unitree, as well as the DeepSeek AI model founded by Zhejiang University graduates. The economic size of Zhejiang province alone is equal to Malaysia's entire economy.

Unitree's main advantage over Western competitors is price. Their robotic dog sells for around USD 6,000 (about RM25,000), while their commercial humanoid/waiter robot sells for around RM80,000 per unit.

If a business pays a worker RM3,000 a month, the cost reaches RM36,000 a year. Within two years, that cost is enough to purchase a robotic waiter that can operate without taking leave or having discipline issues.

While at the Ministry of Economy, I posed a challenge to Unitree's founder. In Malaysia, we face a critical reliance on foreign workers for heavy labor like harvesting oil palm fruit in plantations. Even though the pay can reach RM4,000 a month, locals are uninterested.

I challenged Unitree to adapt their robotics technology for oil palm harvesting tasks. If an RM80,000 robot can perform harvesting tasks in plantations, the market in Malaysia and the region is massive because plantation companies face a chronic labor shortage crisis. Unitree actually sent their team to Malaysia last year to hold discussions with local plantation companies.

The sophistication of this robotics isn't in its appearance, but in its reflexes, coordination, and balance stability that mimic human reflexes. Over the next 10 years, the use of robots in the service and industrial sectors will become commonplace, and Malaysia must capture opportunities in this supply chain.

**Interviewer:** We enter the final segment, the Q&A "Banyak Tanya" [Asking a Lot]. First question from Wan Natasha: "How can the proposal to end secondary schooling at age 16 prevent a one-year gap before starting A-Levels or IB at age 17? And will the government review the automatic placement of officers'/diplomats' children in international schools costing around RM100,000 annually, redirecting those funds to national schools instead?"

**Rafizi:** I am offering my personal view. Regarding school age, A-Levels or IB (International Baccalaureate) programs are pre-university studies. There is no age barrier to starting once secondary school entry requirements are met.

In Malaysia currently, SPM exams are taken at age 17 (late in the year). Results come out in March of the following year, and A-Levels/IB intake terms usually start in August/September. This causes students to wait about 9 to 10 months and only begin pre-university at age 18.

If our secondary school system ended at age 16, students would automatically be able to begin A-Levels or IB studies at age 17 without a lengthy gap.

Regarding international school allowances for children of diplomats or government officers: There is indeed a government policy under the Public Service Department (JPA) and Wisma Putra covering international school fees for diplomats' children—where costs can reach RM100,000 per child per year.

In the private sector, international school allowances are typically provided only while officers serve overseas. Upon returning home, that entitlement ends. For our public servants, this facility continues even after they return to serve in Malaysia.

The general public might just be finding out about this expenditure and view it as unfair. But for the civil service, it is considered part of the welfare package for diplomatic officers. The decision to review this policy rests with the government and JPA.

**Interviewer:** Second question from Furhan Rosli: "What are the main obstacles—such as materials technology, core design, capital, sales volume, FAA certification, supply chain, or ITAR regulations—to developing a commercial or military jet engine industry in Malaysia?"

**Rafizi:** The jet engine industry is a highly capital-intensive monopoly and oligopoly industry. World commercial aircraft manufacturing is dominated by Boeing, Airbus, and now China's COMAC. Meanwhile, jet engine manufacturers are dominated by giants like General Electric (GE), Rolls-Royce, and Pratt & Whitney.

The biggest hurdle for a country like Malaysia isn't just technology, FAA certification, or ITAR export controls, but the capacity to sustain a terrifying capital burn rate before that technology reaches market adoption.

You need to burn billions of ringgit over a very long period to convince aircraft manufacturing companies to use your engine. Airbus and Boeing have guaranteed markets in Europe and the US, while COMAC is backed by China's vast domestic market.

Without guaranteed large-scale volume adoption, building a local jet engine from scratch is economically unviable. The best strategy for Malaysia is to strengthen our position as a high-value Tier-1 or Tier-2 supply chain provider—such as manufacturing turbine blades, wing structures, and aircraft avionics/electronic systems. That is far more pragmatic than attempting to build our own jet engine.

**Interviewer:** Third question from Afiq Ikhwan: "Many politicians now act as influencers. What is YB's principle for distinguishing substantive policy communication from content chasing views? And what are YB's views on the ethics of using government resources for social media content?"

**Rafizi:** I don't see it as double-hatting. Politicians are obligated to communicate, and social media platforms like TikTok, Facebook, YouTube, and X are primary communication channels today.

A politician's job is to drive an evolution of ideas, and that requires continuous communication. What makes the public angry is when certain leaders—whether from the Pakatan Harapan, Barisan Nasional, or Perikatan Nasional eras—become "paralyzed" in terms of communication as soon as they hold government office. Policies aren't explained to the public.

My principle is simple: social media content is a medium for conveying facts and policies, not a platform for gimmicks. I won't dance or follow slapstick TikTok trends just to chase views.

Regarding resource usage: I am extremely cautious with public funds. When at the Ministry of Economy, I rejected the practice of media buying—paying media companies or news portals using ministry funds purely to get favorable coverage of me or the ministry.

All my video posts are recorded by myself using a mobile phone without expensive production crew services. If a leader requires a camera crew of 5 to 6 people wherever he goes, the main question is: Who is paying for those costs? If paid using government allocations, that is a leakage of public funds.

Political gimmicks do not make a person authentic. My principle is what you see is what you get. Politicians are not elected to be entertainers or tickle the public with slapstick gimmicks, but to offer solutions to the people's issues. Political effectiveness cannot be measured 100% by likes or views—especially if those views are purchased through fake accounts.

**Interviewer:** Alright YB. That's all the questions for this episode.

**Rafizi:** My streak of holding back from saying "apa nama" slipped a bit right at the last moment just now. Next week we'll aim for zero "apa nama"s.

**Interviewer:** A bold statement! We'll see the results next week. Thank you YB, and thank you everyone. See you again next week, assalamualaikum.
