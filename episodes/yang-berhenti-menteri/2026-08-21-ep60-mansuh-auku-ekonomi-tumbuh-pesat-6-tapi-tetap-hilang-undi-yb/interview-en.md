**Interviewer:** Let’s move to the topic of higher education. Some academics and members of the public argue that university and higher education are public social goods, and therefore, they should be fully funded by the government. It’s the "best of both worlds" for universities—they get autonomy, and they can do whatever they like. But in my view, there is a catch. When there’s no incentive, there’s a risk that the university’s success will lag behind or fail to meet society's needs. I don’t think this is just a Malaysian case. Everywhere, when people go for higher education, they want to secure a good job afterward. Some argue that a university’s job isn't to help students find work, but to provide knowledge. When you were a lecturer, how did you manage that?

**Rafizi Ramli:** When I was a lecturer, I also acted as a consultant for the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade in Australia. In fact, Australian universities encourage staff to seek consultancy work to fund their research. I think it’s a bit different in Malaysia, where everything is often left to the university administration regarding funding. That’s why I always encouraged my students to learn something practical. Of course, you still need theory, but you also need practical skills. My mindset as a lecturer in Australia was to make sure my teaching matched the students’ needs—ultimately, you want them to find a good job with good pay. In Malaysia, however, that incentive structure is lacking. If you talk to lecturers, a lot of the complaints are about bureaucracy. For instance, in technology-based research, the Intellectual Property (IP) is often owned by the university. So, if lecturers want to commercialize their work, they don't have the personal incentive to do so. They have to go through the university’s innovation office, which is a long, arduous process.

**Interviewer:** Why is it so obstructive?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Exactly. And if they want to do consultancy, universities often have their own internal consultancy companies. You basically become a panel member for the university’s company rather than being hired or receiving a grant because of your own renowned academic reputation. We have these two extremes: one is complete government funding with autonomy, and the other is a completely private university that receives no government money at all.

**Interviewer:** It’s controversial, but let’s look at the vernacular schools (SJKC/SMJK) in Malaysia. They sit in the middle. They receive per capita grants, but it's not enough to survive, so they raise their own funds. If you go to an SJKC or SMJK, the infrastructure is often far better than in a standard national school (SMK/SK), even though they aren't fully funded. They are first-world in terms of quality and infrastructure because they supplement the government’s teacher salary allocations with their own fundraising.

**Rafizi Ramli:** To me, to strike a balance, the government should provide a "basic grant." This grant should be benchmarked against the number of students to cover reasonable operational and capital expenditure.

**Interviewer:** Universities are complaining right now, and it’s a bit chaotic. On one hand, they rely on government grants, but the government restricts them from raising tuition fees. Because of that, they can’t grow or become as market-oriented as they’d like. Take UM [University of Malaya], for example—it’s in the top 50 in the world. If they wanted to, they could recruit many more international students, especially for postgraduate studies. But they are restricted by regulations. If they took in too many international students, people might complain that under the Madani government, universities are ignoring Malaysians for foreigners.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Exactly. If I were running UM, I would strike a balance. I would charge full fees for international students to subsidize local students, but that requires the freedom to set costs and manage faculty expansion without being beholden to rigid government regulations. I’ve always envisioned two types of funding: a basic grant to the university to cover core costs, and then funding given directly to the student. That would force universities to develop programs closer to market demand. If a course only has 10 students, the university would be forced to reallocate those resources to a faculty that has three or four times the demand. They could then charge market rates to international students or those who can afford it, while the government grant—which covers 90% of the fee for eligible students—would be paid directly to the student. The student then decides which university to attend.

**Interviewer:** It’s a difficult model to implement.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Well, good things are always difficult.

**Interviewer:** Before moving on, I think we need to touch on the PTPTN [National Higher Education Fund Corporation] issue, as it’s directly related to the country's financial sustainability. PTPTN is losing money. It charges a 1% *ujrah* (administrative fee), which isn't compounded, but the cost for PTPTN to borrow from the public is between 3% to 4%. So, in addition to the allocations the Ministry of Higher Education receives, the government is also subsidizing the losses incurred by PTPTN.

**Rafizi Ramli:** That’s a whole separate discussion. Right now, students taking PTPTN aren't even paying full fees. In public universities, the fee is 90% subsidized by the government, and the loan only covers the remaining 10% plus cost of living. When we look at higher education reform, we must look at PTPTN simultaneously. The current blanket subsidy for universities creates a mismatch with the market. Courses aren't competitive because they are subsidized anyway, so neither students nor lecturers have an incentive to be market-relevant. The moment you allow fees to reflect market rates—while keeping them lower than private universities due to the basic grant—then students can make an informed choice. Right now, the UPU [Central University Admission Unit] system is too restricted. Everyone fights for the same popular courses. If they don't get their first choice, they end up in a course they didn't want simply because that's where the capacity was available. If the university wanted to expand a popular faculty, they have to go through layers of bureaucracy at the Ministry of Higher Education, which can take years. Universities need to be agile, and that requires autonomy. Grants should be block budgets, not project-based, so that universities can plan predictably.

**Interviewer:** We’re two hours and five minutes in. What happens to the students and the government if this autonomy is granted? Do we need a regulator?

**Rafizi Ramli:** We need to move away from the focus on student protests and disciplinary actions. If the university is autonomous and we trust the academic staff, we can cultivate a culture of academic freedom and respect for due process. If the university’s charter is designed to protect rights rather than punish, it will self-regulate. Of course, we need a regulator to ensure quality, but it should be an independent body responsible to Parliament, not the Ministry. If the Minister doesn't have to micromanage university dormitories or staff appointments, they can focus on big-picture policy. I managed to sneak this into the 13th Malaysia Plan (RMK13)—to start the process of giving autonomy to one public university as a pilot project. Now that there’s talk of abolishing the AUKU [Universities and University Colleges Act], I hope we move toward academic and funding freedom.

**Rafizi Ramli:** There’s a disconnect between GDP and wages. GDP measures production volume—it doesn't differentiate between foreigners or locals. But GNI [Gross National Income] measures income accruing specifically to Malaysians. In the second quarter, real GNI growth was only 2.2%.

**Joe:** The gap between GDP and GNI is widening, which coincides with the AI boom. Most of the export-driven chip companies in Malaysia are foreign-owned, like Intel or AMD. A lot of the profits are being repatriated as dividends to parent companies abroad. That’s why GDP is high, but the domestic benefit is diluted.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Our economy is like a pyramid—the growth is driven by a very small number of large companies. Out of over a million enterprises in Malaysia, only about 25,000 are large companies. 95% of our businesses are small or micro-enterprises. If the 6% growth is driven by 10,000 foreign companies, the average person doesn't feel it in their paycheck. When I was Minister of Economy, we introduced wage reporting using granular data from EPF, PERKESO, and LHDN to track payroll. We found that while median wages have risen from RM2,500 in 2022 to over RM3,000 now, we still face wage compression.

**Interviewer:** And when you try to raise the minimum wage, you hit the reality that most businesses are SMEs that are just struggling to get by.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Exactly. If the minimum wage goes up, they struggle to pay, and often the lower-income earners—many of whom are foreign workers—are the ones affected by these structural issues. That’s why we need to focus on reforming SMEs to become part of a higher-value chain.

**Rafizi Ramli:** The arrest and immigration reports have substance. When interviewed, workers told me their salary was effectively more than RM2,000. It is true that, combined with the RM1,700 minimum wage, allowances, and overtime, the total is indeed RM2,000. Employers usually compress wages across three categories. One is the fresh graduate—which is why their starting salary is currently around RM2,000. You can imagine the irony: foreign workers doing the lowest-level jobs like waste collection earn RM1,800–RM1,900, while a university graduate starts at RM2,100. It is a mess. There is hardly any growth compared to the past. Fresh graduates are always victims, as are experienced workers. If you are a technical worker and your salary increases by only RM30 or RM50 a year, after working for five or six years, your salary is only RM2,004. They did not feel the impact of the minimum wage order at all. In fact, part of the salary increase that should have gone to them, the employer had to use to pay according to the law for the lowest-paid workers. So, we have a huge wage compression. Because of that, our median wage is RM3,027. Most people fall into that category—either they have worked for 2–3 years or 5–6 years without a diploma, or they have a diploma but their salary is still around RM3,000 after five years. When you add up all these numbers, arranging them together, it explains why... The economy grew by 6% and the narrative being brought is, "Oh, that's good, Dato' Sri Anwar. If we change the PM, we will die. The economy will not grow after this." I think we have exhausted that runway. That runway can only be used in the first 2-3 years. That is why I always said we need a cogent and attractive narrative to build trust in our economy. Trust, the influence portfolio will happen, FDI will happen, that will cause the ringgit to strengthen, money to come in. And at least, it builds trust to invest in this country, not just abroad, but also domestic capital to be deployed here. But, to ensure people feel it, one of the most important structural reforms is labor market reform. Perhaps I can add one more point regarding this minimum wage, just for an interesting fact, we can refer to Singapore. Fun fact, Singapore doesn't actually have a minimum wage policy. They don't have a minimum wage at all. And to address the point you raised earlier regarding wage compression, they have features quite similar to Malaysia where reliance on foreign workers is much higher than Malaysia. Malaysia, if I'm not mistaken, is... it’s Malaysian workers. Including Joe. Long ago, long ago. If I'm not mistaken, the reliance rate on foreign workers in Malaysia is 14%. But in Singapore, if I'm not mistaken, it's about 40%. So, it is much higher. However, how they address wage compression is to introduce a mechanism called Local Qualifying Salary. If you want to employ a foreign laborer, you have to ensure the salary you pay the local people meets certain requirements, certain levels. So, it's not like Malaysia's case, where you either cut or you don't increase the salary. In Singapore, for you to hire one extra foreign laborer, you have to ensure that the local people's wages not only keep up, but because it adjusts every year for inflation, you actually have to increase it. It disincentivizes people from changing foreign workers. The objective of the policy needs to be clear: the main objective is not to replace the local people, but to supplement them. I think that's what we got confused about here. Many narratives focus on hiring more foreign laborers to replace domestic laborers because they are cheaper, due to cost considerations. But we need to bring into the narrative that we want to supplement our labor market, not just replace it. In that sense, what Nurul Izzah said—and I've said it for a long time—is that the only way to appease the public regarding economic anxiety is actually labor market reform. So, we have several things that need to be given a boost. All the pilot projects are there, but somehow it's still there because it's related to me, or because people don't believe in that principle, or because it's rocking the boat. Rocking the status quo too much. There are several root causes. One, we know the main cost is foreign workers. Officially it's about 14% only, but actually, it's much higher—almost 18–19%. Every year, people race to fill the quota. You have Johari Ghani—before this, now he's the Minister of Finance, before this he was the Minister of Agriculture. So, don't touch foreign workers; agriculture needs foreign workers. Every minister from every sector says, "We need more foreign workers." They are angry at me because I say whatever it is, indeed there are some cultural preferences. But if every single one of them needs foreign workers, that’s how it reaches 18–19%. You ask employers, they will say we actually aren't refusing to take local workers, but the jobs done by foreign workers are not reliable. Because they run off. Two, three months, they run off, you know. Actually, the employer is not wrong. That's correct. In fact, I remember when we did the IPR, the People's Income Initiative, one of the categories was Ehsan, Service Operator Initiative—something like that. We topped up RM200 over and above the minimum wage they get. So, for garbage collection, they should get about RM2,000 a month. When we opened it, 4,000–5,000 people applied.
