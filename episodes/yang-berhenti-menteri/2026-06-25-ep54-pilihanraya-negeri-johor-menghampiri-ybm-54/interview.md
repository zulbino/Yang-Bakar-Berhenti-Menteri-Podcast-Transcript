If they violate the constitution, take action against the party, leave the party, resign, or fail to attend the State Assembly sessions to the point where their membership can be terminated, the party can automatically send that signed letter to the Speaker saying they have resigned. This process is happening, and tomorrow night when we announce the candidates one by one, part of the process is to exchange the official candidate letters [watikah] with the documents they have signed. So they get their *watikah* to contest, and in exchange, the party keeps all the signed documents. 

**Interviewer:** Moving on to the machinery—we want to know a bit about the uniqueness of how *Bersama* operates, putting up flags and posters across the state, especially since you only contest 15 seats. Many might be wondering how that is determined.

**Rafizi Ramli:** As I mentioned, it has been my practice since June 2016 to receive reports on voter support levels every month. When I feel there’s something more interesting, we conduct specific surveys. The data we have regarding the behavior and tendencies of Johor voters has existed for a long time; it’s a time series. Based on those figures, our understanding, and the level of support so far, we chose the seats where we felt there was room for *Bersama* to introduce itself, and we hope these seats will provide an initial boost so we don’t lose our deposits. That’s why we are contesting those 15 seats.

**Interviewer:** And finally, about the machinery. We can promote people to join as volunteers for the state election. How does *Bersama* bring a different practice to managing our machinery, doing everything as a small party that others might think is impossible—putting up flags throughout the state, moving volunteers to install posters, banners, and buntings?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It is challenging. Previous party practices started with the "Sukarela" (volunteer) practice. We entered the party, became part of the committee, and then we could be promoted. Like me, I joined PKR when I was young. Even with a high position and salary, when it came to party level, whatever we could do, we did. If the task was to put up posters, we put up posters. Even at my level, when I became the branch head, I worked in KL, but every month I would manage the discussions in Kemaman. My children were the ones who posted the posters. But I had my father, who was like a Special Branch officer—I would call him to check if the posters were actually put up. He would report back: "That one is up, this one is only put up in one spot." We all went through that process. 

However, over time, the money entering political parties has become excessive. Since all political parties have become the government, back in the day, the tycoons with vested interests only gave money to BN, UMNO, MCA, and MIC because they were always the government. Why help the opposition? But since 2008, all parties—MIC, Gerakan, PKR, DAP, Amanah, PAS, Bersatu, even Muda and Syed Saddiq—have been the government. Because of that, all these parties have the capacity to get funds. How they get them and what they promise, *Wallahualam* (God knows). But as I said, every donation given is never free; if you win, "I have invested in you." 

I have noticed this happening in PKR over the last 10 years. What used to be volunteer-based slowly became paid. It started with "coffee money," buying coffee and oil for the kids. For a long time, they paid per head. Even the parents had to pay to be an agent to vote for 100-200 people. I think that is the main culture in political parties now. 

When *Bersama* decided to contest solo, that was our biggest challenge: to build a party machine without using money and returning to the spirit of the *sukarela*. A lot of friends were afraid, saying, "If you follow Rafizi, you'll die. He doesn't give a cent." We pay for big expenses only, but money for drinks—you have to be resourceful yourself, right? Because we started managing it in a *sukarela* way, it can’t be done just by mouth. We also want to escape the layered structure of the party, as that causes corruption and lobbying. 

How do we organize our army? It can only be done through a system that manages the election campaign in a smooth and precise manner. If I want to go to Johor to help, I have to know where to go, what the campaign is, and who the PIC [Person-in-Charge] is. So when I take a day or two off, it isn't wasted; I can truly help the campaign. In reality, in parties with large machinery, many people who show up at the nomination day become "political tourists." I used to frown upon this in PKR because it felt like a waste when people came but did nothing.

We implemented the "Kancil" system to solve this. It’s operational at *bersama.org*. We need about 5,000 people for the two-week period. We have over 26,000 members, so 20% is enough. Most of them are outside Johor. We want them to be real troops on the ground. They just need to go to *bersama.org*, log in to the Kancil system, enter their IC number, get an OTP, and they can see every campaign currently happening and select one. It doesn’t waste their time. 

My experience as a candidate and manager is that party people in charge of campaigns can sometimes be moody; they might ignore volunteers. People come to the operations room asking, "What can I do?" but no one pays attention to them, or they get chased away because the staff is busy. That’s why we lose out. The Kancil system solves this—they don’t have to ask anyone; the list is there, they can arrange their own schedule. 

If we succeed in this, we will change the political culture in Malaysia that is prone to corruption. After more than 25 years in politics, I don't believe in slogans like "we are anti-corruption" anymore. If people say this person is cleaner than that person, I don't believe it. Look at their actions, the system, and the culture in the party. If we want to fight corruption, enforcing the law isn't enough. A political fund act isn't enough, because politicians are smart—they find loopholes. We must change the entire approach and discipline. Political parties must campaign without using money. 

Registration is important to manage our limited resources. We want to know who is taking the flags and if they are actually being put up. We need to manage the events well; if 50 people register, we set up 50 chairs. We have to be realistic because *Bersama* is only one month old. If there are four people, we organize with four; if twenty, we organize with twenty. This is good management to avoid waste and ensure efficiency.
