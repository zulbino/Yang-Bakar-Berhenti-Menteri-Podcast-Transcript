# Interview (English)

**Host:** Welcome to episode 24 of *Podcast Yang Bermenteri*, together with YB Rafizi Ramli. Before we dive into our regular segments, I have a couple of questions. This week marks the final stretch of the Sabah state election campaign. Are you heading down to campaign? Secondly, has anyone from the party reached out asking you to join the trail?

**Rafizi Ramli:** People have reached out, but I do not believe I would add much value. It ultimately depends on the party machinery. Following our last party elections, the message sent by the leadership, the overall machinery, and everything that transpired behind the scenes regarding the voting system made it clear that leaders like me are not particularly desired. When the campaign machinery on the ground harbors more resentment toward me than toward PAS, PN, or UMNO, my presence would not assist anyone. 

There are other ways to contribute. Many Sabahans tune into this podcast, so I contribute by demonstrating that the spirit of Reformasi still endures within PKR, hoping that keeps their hearts open to voting for us.

**Host:** Exactly. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** There is another reason as well. I have been involved in managing elections since 2011, with the 2011 Sarawak state election being one of the earliest I handled closely. Voters in Sabah and Sarawak generally do not appreciate Peninsular leaders coming over and taking center stage. PKR took a major risk by having almost the entire Sabah campaign driven, led, and spotlighted by big-name figures from Peninsular Malaysia. It reinforces the perception that PKR in Sabah is merely a Peninsular party lacking local leadership. Sabahans want local leaders representing them. 

It creates a Catch-22. As I mentioned in previous episodes, parachuting candidates in weakens PKR's reputation at the grassroots level in Sabah. It gives the impression that Sabah PKR lacks capable homegrown talent. When voters see Nurul Izzah, Saifuddin Nasution, and Datuk Seri Anwar himself heavily campaigning, it risks heightening the feeling that Sabah PKR does not fully represent Sabahan aspirations and remains dependent on signals from the Peninsular. 

During past party elections, I always enjoyed strong support from Sabah without spending money, simply because I did not interfere in their local affairs. Sabah and Sarawak want to make their own decisions. Unless a local decision directly conflicts with national interests—where we must present our arguments—we should not force them. That is the explanation I gave to candidates and campaigners who asked me to come down.

**Host:** Did actual candidates reach out to you as well?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, candidates reached out, but they are all capable figures in their own right.

**Host:** On that note, I read comments from the "Geng Damai" group pointing out that Khairy Jamaluddin—even after being expelled from his party—had the heart to travel to Sabah to campaign, whereas you, a senior leader and sitting MP, did not go. What is your response to that comparison?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I rarely entertain comments at that level. Those complaining and drawing comparisons with KJ were nowhere to be seen when we were building this party from scratch under tough conditions. I do not need to beat my chest to prove my contributions. We were here long before PKR was officially formed. We endured losses, funded operations out of our own pockets, and launched the country's first internet radio using overseas funds to build the foundation of the party we have today. 

Some recent entrants over the past two or three years do not understand the party's history or struggle, which leads to such statements. I think even KJ would feel embarrassed by that comparison.

**Host:** Understood. Let us move to our first segment, "Beria-ia Mat". First up: a husband caught lying to his wife during the recent floods in Hatyai.

**Rafizi Ramli:** What is the background on this story? I do not usually follow viral social media trends, and this one did not pop up on my timeline.

**Host:** Farhan mentioned he was eager to explain this one.

**Farhan:** Hatyai experienced severe flooding, and some friends on Instagram were stuck and unable to return to Malaysia. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** But surely not because they were caught lying to their wives?

**Farhan:** Posts circulated on social media about a husband who told his wife he was heading to KL for work, but got stranded in Hatyai after his secret getaway was exposed by the floods. Another motorist supposedly told his wife he was driving up to Cameron Highlands, only to get caught in Hatyai as well.

**Rafizi Ramli:** That explains the comment from "Lovely Nor Pinky" saying, "No, the meeting was in KL, but the site visit was in Hatyai." Is it pronounced "Hat Yai" or "Hatyai"?

**Host:** Officially it is Hat Yai, but locally people say Hatyai.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Just checking who frequents the place and knows the proper pronunciation.

**Farhan:** Hatyai is the first major stop after crossing the border at Bukit Kayu Hitam if you are riding a heavy motorcycle into Northern Thailand—about 60 kilometers from the border.

**Rafizi Ramli:** How difficult is the rescue operation over there currently?

**Farhan:** Teams like United 6 Malaysia and several Malaysian relief units went in to assist, though reports suggest help arrived a bit late.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Sensationalizing marital affairs in the middle of a disaster devalues the gravity of the situation. People start viewing the flood crisis as a joke about cheating husbands, when in reality thousands of Malaysians are stranded.

**Farhan:** Around 4,000 Malaysians were reported stranded.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Exactly. 4,000 people need urgent rescue. The personal domestic issues can be settled privately between husband and wife later. The focus should remain entirely on how to evacuate those 4,000 citizens.

**Host:** So, how over-the-top is this topic?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Less about the situation itself and more about people exploiting others' misfortune for viral content.

**Host:** Next topic: Election gimmicks. A video went viral showing candidates cleaning drains and repairing roads during the campaign, with voters sarcastically asking for daily elections.

**Farhan:** Videos showed a candidate repairing a bridge in Titiwangsa and another cleaning local areas. Datuk Seri Shafie Apdal was also filmed fixing a road.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Party machinery needs to understand that over-engineered campaign gimmicks backfire. In the past, we saw ministers going down to wash drains publicly. Campaign assistants should ensure their candidates avoid such optics, otherwise politicians end up facing public ridicule. 

Part of the issue stems from our underlying political culture, which remains somewhat feudal. Some voters praise these actions, saying, "Look how humble and caring our representative is, doing manual labor himself." 

I personally dislike content where leaders or youth chiefs go around publicly scolding people or doing manual work for display. It feels feudal. Everyone has a role to play. If someone is not performing their job, there are official administrative channels to address it without publicly humiliating them. But because parts of the public applaud these displays, politicians keep doing them. Society must shift its expectations of public officials instead of glorifying cheap political theater.

**Host:** Definitely over-the-top.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Very much so.

**Host:** Moving on to the next item: the PAS Chukai Assemblyman and state Exco member who issued a direct letter requesting a restricted tender for a project in Terengganu.

**Rafizi Ramli:** This issue highlights a dangerous habit where parties like PKR and Pakatan Harapan sometimes respond to internal slip-ups by pointing fingers and arguing that the opposition is worse. We fought to change the government precisely to eliminate those practices, not to claim we are merely slightly better than the previous administration.

Relying on narratives like "the previous government did nothing for decades" might work during the first year or two in office, but by the third or fourth year, the public grows tired of hearing it. Opposition wrongdoings do not give us a license to repeat the same mistakes.

Regarding the Chukai Assemblyman, this goes beyond a basic letter of support—it is direct political lobbying and an instruction. Using "project urgency" as a justification for restricted tenders is weak administrative logic because any project can be labeled urgent to bypass standard procurement. 

Because Terengganu is an Unfederated Malay State with a closed civil service structure, local civil servants are appointed directly by the state government. Unlike federal officers who can rely on institutional backing when resisting political pressure, state civil servants facing direct instructions from a state Exco member have limited protection.

**Host:** Following that, government communications teams and pro-government commentators defended similar political support letters issued by officers in the Prime Minister's Office, citing MACC guidelines.

**Rafizi Ramli:** In auditing and accounting, there is a core principle known as "substance over form." You do not look merely at what is written on paper; you look at the underlying reality. 

Guidelines can always be cited as a formal defense after the fact. That is the "form." But the "substance" is that when a letter originates from the Prime Minister’s Office—signed by the Senior Private Secretary—listing a select group of contractors, it carries overwhelming weight. Even if a list of six contractors is submitted under the guise of official guidelines, if several are clearly unqualified, the secondary names often serve merely as procedural dummies to ensure a specific candidate gets selected.

When government communications units like J-KOM defend such actions by arguing that MACC guidelines permit them, they are only convincing themselves within an echo chamber. The general public looks at the substance over the form. Defending dubious practices using administrative technicalities mimics the exact arguments used during the Najib era to defend controversial decisions. It makes the public conclude that nothing has fundamentally changed.

**Host:** Next item: Hulu Terengganu MP Datuk Rosol Wahid's speech in Parliament claiming that a volcanic eruption in Ethiopia occurred because Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim visited the country.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I was in Parliament during that sitting on November 26. Why intervene when someone chooses to display absurdity publicly? Interrupting only drags you down to that level. When an MP makes ridiculous statements in Parliament, I simply smile and let them expose themselves. 

It makes me wonder if they review their own speeches or genuinely believe such remarks demonstrate cleverness. Part of the problem is the immediate audience surrounding them. I am grateful that our podcast audience consists of thoughtful, discerning listeners whose intellect keeps us grounded. I worry for politicians who convince themselves that making such claims makes them sound sharp. If bad weather hits the Straits of Malacca, are they going to blame the MP for Jasin just for being nearby?

**Host:** How do you rate this remark?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Extremely over-the-top. Even the MP sitting next to him looked conflicted on camera, struggling not to laugh at the absurdity. If Astro ever produces another season of *Maharaja Lawak*, I would nominate the Hulu Terengganu MP as a contestant.

**Host:** Next, tweets from former Kangar MP Noor Amin Ahmad referencing a "know-it-all bubble" (Gelembung Mat Palatow) alongside news clippings featuring your image.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Figures like that will always exist in politics. If he tweeted without mentioning my name, nobody would pay attention or engage with his posts. Attaching my name or throwing indirect jabs is the only way for him to generate traction and engagement. I do not think we need to waste time on it.

**Host:** Regarding social media accounts, do you write your own posts or do staff handle your account?

**Rafizi Ramli:** All the core content comes directly from me. I review everything personally. Social media for a politician is a critical asset; it reflects your personal character and stance. When I was in Cabinet, my posts focused on policy matters; now, they reflect my views on current issues. We discuss strategy, I draft the content, and team members handle the execution across five or six platforms.

I never use the "it was posted by an admin" excuse. Whatever goes out represents my position. How you respond on social media reflects your personal integrity and brand.

**Host:** Time to select the top over-the-top moment of the week.

**Rafizi Ramli:** It has to go to the Ethiopian volcano claim. The MP seemed genuinely proud of his wordplay, thinking he delivered a masterclass in political comedy.

**Host:** Moving to our next segment, *Standing Order*. The main topic is the low viewership of the J-KOM [Department of Community Communications] Director-General’s podcast, which was raised during the Parliamentary Special Select Committee proceedings.

**Rafizi Ramli:** J-KOM is a continuation of the former JASA unit from the BN era, which operated on an annual budget of RM80 million primarily to maintain paid cybertroopers to attack opposition figures. Pakatan Harapan started on the wrong foot by retaining the structure under a new name—Community Communications Department—instead of dismantling it as pledged.

It worsened when J-KOM turned into a hub for internal party patronage, used to hire and fund political faction loyalists ahead of party elections. Using public funds to hire political operatives who are then deployed against internal party rivals is an abuse of public resources. 

Furthermore, when public communications appointments are given to individuals with no history of Reformasi commitment—some of whom spent years attacking the party leadership—it yields predictable results, like a Director-General hosting a podcast that draws 50 viewers.

I have known the current J-KOM Director-General since 2011. During the 2011 Sarawak state election, when I served as Strategy Director and Azmin Ali as Election Director, he was paid by the party as an election operations manager. When I arrived in Sarawak days before nominations, basic preparations like campaign posters were completely missing. Based on that experience, I learned not to assign critical operational responsibilities to him.

**Host:** Minister of Communications Fahmi Fadzil defended the situation by stating that public communications involves multiple units, including PMO StraCom, UKK, and the Information Department, not just J-KOM.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Focusing solely on J-KOM misses the broader structural issue. J-KOM is merely a symptom of a larger problem: since the rise of digital and social media, successive governments have failed to develop an effective, cost-efficient communication strategy.

When ministers appear frequently across conventional media, those placements are rarely free—they are funded by taxpayers. From the Najib administration to Ismail Sabri's tenure, hundreds of millions were spent on government advertising, consultants, and paying influencers up to RM80,000 for a two-minute clip. 

When I was at the Ministry of Economy, I refused to spend public money on PR agencies or entourage media coverage. I recorded my own updates directly on an iPhone. 

The real solution to public information distribution is leveraging integrated digital platforms like PADU. When the government maintains a secure, consolidated socioeconomic database, targeted information—such as eligibility for the Progressive Wage Policy or SARA aid—can be delivered directly to a citizen's personal app profile without spending hundreds of millions on advertising campaigns. Traditional media players and internal departments resist these ideas because it threatens existing budgets and vendor contracts, but protecting public funds must take priority.

**Host:** Did you raise your concerns regarding J-KOM in Cabinet?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Decisions regarding department structures fall directly under the purview of the Prime Minister and the Chief Secretary to the Government (KSN). Departmental setups are not debated informally in Cabinet unless the supervising Ministry submits an official Cabinet Memorandum proposing structural changes or dissolution.

Power corrupts if leadership lacks institutional discipline. Ministerial offices possess immense administrative authority, and civil servants are trained to execute the political directions of sitting ministers. If political leaders lack the discipline to eliminate redundant propaganda structures, the same cycles of waste will repeat regardless of who holds power.

**Host:** Moving to our next segment, *Hidup Keras*. First topic: Speculation regarding Nurul Izzah potentially becoming the Selangor Chief Minister (Menteri Besar).

**Rafizi Ramli:** Talks regarding Nurul Izzah moving into the Selangor MB role date back to early 2024. The original plan involved Tengku Zafrul joining PKR in April 2024, accompanied by two planned by-elections: vacating the Ampang parliamentary seat for Zafrul and a state Legislative Assembly seat for Izzah, clearing her path to the MB position while current MB Amirudin Shari transitioned to a Federal role. 

However, leaks caused strong pushback from UMNO, delaying the plan. As time progressed into late 2024 and early 2025, internal party elections took center stage, Zafrul's senatorial term expired, and the political landscape shifted.

Public perception of political figures changes once they take explicit sides in internal contests. Politicians who avoid taking controversial stances often remain broad-based and popular because they do not polarize opinion. Prior to contesting in the recent party elections, Izzah enjoyed high general popularity as the "Princess of Reform" (Puteri Reformasi). Entering internal party contests naturally altered those dynamics. 

Internal surveys show that public approval ratings fluctuate based on political clarity. Citizens demand clarity on major policy issues. Executing a state by-election in Selangor now carries political risks. A seat like Bukit Antarabangsa, with a 60% Malay and 40% non-Malay demographic, depends heavily on non-Malay voter turnout, which cannot be taken for granted in the current political climate.

Claims that the Prime Minister was entirely unaware of these maneuvers are inaccurate. He advocated strongly for bringing both Tengku Zafrul and Nurul Izzah into strategic positions within the party structure. While political leaders plan, external circumstances often derail those plans.

**Farhan:** From a voter's perspective, is engineering seat vacancies for internal political maneuvering an abuse of the democratic process?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Context matters. Consider the 2018 Port Dickson by-election that enabled Datuk Seri Anwar to re-enter Parliament after his release from prison. For him to become Prime Minister, he required a parliamentary seat. When we planned our 2018 election strategy, the candidate for Port Dickson understood from the outset that if we won and Anwar received a Royal Pardon, the seat would be vacated for him. That clear political objective was established beforehand.

In ordinary circumstances, forcing an elected MP—such as Roziah Ismail in Ampang, who has served the party diligently since its founding—to vacate her seat simply to accommodate a newcomer is difficult to justify. Political parties must respect the mandate given by voters rather than treating parliamentary seats purely as political chess pieces. The Port Dickson situation was a unique historical exception; generally, the public mandate belongs to the voters, not the party.

**Host:** Let us take audience questions for our final segment. First question, from Sarah: "Will KESUM and FlexiMart open in other states? How does a social enterprise model benefit the public if it still operates as a business?"

**Rafizi Ramli:** A social enterprise benefits the public directly through pricing. At KESUM, a portion of chicken sells for just over RM2, compared to RM5 or RM6 outside. FlexiMart sells one liter of detergent for RM1, whereas commercial platforms charge upwards of RM3. 

The primary benefit to the public is drastically reduced cost of living through razor-thin profit margins. Operating on minimal margins makes balancing the books challenging and requires very high sales volume to remain viable.

Our goal is to prove that business models centered on social welfare rather than profit maximization can be self-sustaining. If we can demonstrate over the coming months that this model works and that surplus yields are reinvested into lower prices and worker wages, it can encourage ethically minded investors to support similar ventures. 

However, expanding to other states requires significant capital, which I personally do not have. We are focusing our efforts on making this prototype store sustainable before considering expansion.

**Host:** Next question, from Lee: "As a Sabahan, what does the 40% special grant entitlement actually mean in practice, and how will it affect federal-state relations and budgets for other states if fully implemented?"

**Rafizi Ramli:** The financial core of the issue depends on how "revenue" derived from Sabah is defined under the Federal Constitution. Does revenue include only direct federal taxes collected in the state, such as Petroleum Income Tax (PITA), or does it also encompass Petronas dividends and federal royalties? 

Establishing a joint committee between the Federal Government and the Sabah State Government to negotiate these definitions is the correct approach. Prime Minister Anwar mentioned that federal revenue collected from Sabah stands at roughly RM10 billion, likely referring to direct tax revenue, while total Federal expenditure in Sabah—covering operational costs, civil servant salaries, schools, and infrastructure—exceeds RM17 billion annually.

If 40% of net revenue (RM4 billion out of RM10 billion) is returned directly to Sabah, those funds must be factored into overall national budget allocations. If Sabah assumes direct financial responsibility for state infrastructure projects using those funds, federal development spending in the state would adjust accordingly. However, if state claims demand the continuation of the full RM17 billion federal allocation plus an additional RM4 billion transfer, the difference would have to be reallocated from federal budgets or other state allocations. The precise outcome will depend on the formal federal-state negotiations.

**Host:** Final question, from Vimal: "Parliamentary sittings frequently descend into shouting matches compared to parliaments in developed nations. What is the solution to this problem?"

**Rafizi Ramli:** The ultimate solution lies with the voters. Politicians respond to incentives; if an MP believes belligerent behavior wins votes, they will continue doing it. Figures who engage in parliamentary grandstanding do so because they assume it boosts their popularity among their constituency base.

Public feedback through social media and the ballot box is the most effective tool to change this behavior. When voters actively reject parliamentary antics and penalize rowdy behavior at the polls, political strategies shift. We have already seen backbenchers from older established parties moderate their parliamentary conduct following electoral losses in 2018. 

In developed democracies, behaving unparliamentarily leads to immediate public backlash and party discipline. Until Malaysian voters consistently penalize disruptive behavior, systemic change in Parliament will remain slow.
