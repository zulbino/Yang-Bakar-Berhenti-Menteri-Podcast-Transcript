**Speaker 1:** Recently, there were reports about the unemployment rate and wage levels increasing slightly, but the people on the ground still feel the pressure of the high cost of living. YB often mentions economic narratives and perceptions. How do you view the difference between official economic figures and what the people on the ground are feeling?

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is a very good question and often forms the main gap in economic discussions in any country. Macroeconomic figures, for example GDP growth of 4.5%, the unemployment rate dropping to 3.3%, and the inflation rate dropping to 1.8%—these are macro average figures. They show the overall health of the country's economy. This means that overall, our economy is moving forward, jobs are being created, and the rise in goods prices on average is controlled compared to previous years. 

BUT, what the people on the ground feel is microeconomics and their own pockets. This is where the perception gap occurs. 

First, wages in Malaysia have been stagnant for decades. When wages are stagnant and prices of goods go up—even if the rise in inflation is slow (1.8%)—the price increase of goods is cumulative on top of previous years' increases. So when wages don't increase or only go up slightly, but prices of goods are already at a high level, the people's disposable income still feels tight. 

That is why the current government is focusing on two main areas: 

First, restructuring the economy so that we create high-income jobs through technology investments, high value-add, and new industries. That is why we formulated the new Civil Service Policy and introduced the Progressive Wage Policy for the private sector. We want to push and encourage employers to raise workers' wages in line with productivity gains. 

Second, we are retargeting subsidies. Because the previous blanket subsidy system actually benefited the wealthy and foreigners a lot. When we target subsidies, those savings are returned directly to the lower-income group in the form of cash contributions (STR), schooling assistance, and better public services. 

So this process takes time and cannot happen overnight. Our task is to ensure these policies are implemented effectively so that good macro figures can ultimately be felt in the pockets of every Malaysian.

**Speaker 1:** Regarding PADU, YB. **Rafizi Ramli:** Regarding subsidy targeting, especially diesel which we have started, the journey is proceeding according to what we planned. The main objective of this diesel subsidy targeting is to stop leakages and smuggling. Previously, billions of ringgit of the people's money leaked because subsidized diesel was sold to the industrial sector that shouldn't receive it, or smuggled out to neighboring countries. 

When we float the price of diesel according to market prices and provide Budi MADANI cash assistance to individual diesel vehicle owners, as well as assistance to the public transport and logistics sectors through fleet cards, we are able to control these leakages significantly. The savings from this diesel subsidy targeting are repurposed to fund development expenditure and other social assistance. 

As for RON95, it is a much bigger step because it involves the majority of the people. That is why the government is being very cautious. We are studying the best mechanism so that the eligible B40 and M40 groups are not burdened, while the T15 group and foreign nationals pay the market price without subsidies. PADU data is used as the basis to identify this eligibility more fairly compared to relying solely on gross income, because we take into account basic expenditure and household size.
