**Host:** Okay YB, you mentioned several election patterns earlier. Many watching our podcast aren't party members either. So when we talk about a "headless body", some might not understand our election system. What phenomenon is actually occurring at our branches that led to all kinds of news coming out?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Well, this is the risk when a party becomes the governing party. If we were just an opposition party, what happens inside the party doesn't affect the government. But when issues arise, naturally it attracts public interest. I'm usually in a dilemma. Before this, I didn't comment to the media at all. Media good luck if they tried calling me, I really didn't comment because these things needed to be managed internally.

But we have entered the campaign period, so this needs to be explained to the general public watching, besides our party members. In Keadilan, right from the first time—in fact in any party—when we contest in a general election, we contest on a party ticket. Because when people vote, they already know the whole team that will steer the administration. In Keadilan it's the same: every time there is an election, there will be teams because we want to make sure that when they contest and assume office, they can hit the ground running together with their team. If it's individual, it becomes a beauty pageant. When steering the party as a single unit at the branch level, Person A isn't on good terms with Person B, Person B doesn't like Person C.

Logically and in practice, elections in Keadilan and other parties follow teams. Typically from before, there were only two possibilities in terms of results: First, one team wins everything. Case closed, it gets managed at the branch level. Second, mixed results. Committee member positions are mixed, and we learn to co-exist and find consensus. That's common sense.

Except this time, for the first time in PKR's history, we have the "head without a body" phenomenon! Meaning, the number one post—the Branch Chief—is won by someone from Team A, but number two on down is won by another group. The votes were consistent. From number two to committee member number 20, they got 800 votes, for example, but the chief who manages all of this suddenly lost 400 votes and only got 400 votes. The other 400 votes went to that head without a body, where he individually got 900 votes while the rest of his team only got 300 votes.

If this happened in one or two places, we could say it's an anomaly, a unique quirk there. But if it happens in nearly 70 to 80 branches, especially in the first and second weeks, this head-without-a-body phenomenon raises questions. We want to understand why this happened, because we know it impacts the branch and the constituency. The chief does not have a mandate from his entire committee. How do you manage the party effectively if the entire committee refuses to follow?

That's why when I started looking into it—and those who work with me know I don't accept things blindly—I checked. Over time, I felt this needed to be looked at, especially when we started seeing a national pattern. It's strange to see a national pattern after 2 to 3 weeks. I told party leadership, we can decide to turn a blind eye as if none of this is happening, but we cannot control people's feelings and understanding. Keadilan is a party that makes efforts to educate the public and its members. If an issue arises, we must manage it properly; don't ask people at the bottom to swallow it blindly.

That is why, if outsiders are scratching their heads wondering why PKR is suddenly noisy, it is because there are things that I as Deputy President cannot deny we have to manage properly, especially based on the existing national pattern.

**Host:** So to summarize, our elections are indeed team-based. A branch has many posts; usually if one team wins, everyone wins. But this time, the head at the top is different, while those below are different. YB mentioned a national pattern earlier. What trend did YB observe nationwide?

If we rock the party too much, the government will wobble, whereas the public needs us to focus on building the nation's economy, fixing the situation, and helping the people. What can be resolved internally, we resolve internally.

My mistake was perhaps that I wasn't firm enough earlier because I wanted to maintain goodwill and think positively. Even though the elections had issues and we brought them to the MPP—in the past 2 weeks, special MPP meetings were held repeatedly for audits and so on—when there were calls to move on, we moved on. Otherwise, the government and party would be disrupted. The choice for me was to swallow it.

This is a lesson for us when we are in government. I admit that in these two and a half years, I really had to balance between the image and unity of the government with my own views regarding government and party decisions. According to my principles previously, priority must be given to maintaining government harmony first so as not to give ammunition to the opposition to weaken the government. What could be kept internal and argued internally, we did. Ultimately, we have to respect consensus or the President's call. That is what has happened so far.

Except right now is the campaign period. Everything that happened, even if I didn't mention it openly tonight, everyone already knows. People asked me—many sent messages, even without WhatsApp, I have SMS—asking "why waste time YB? Better if you just don't contest, everyone already knows."

My view is this: this party has always been respected for our objective culture. If we disagree, we will state that there are parties who disagree. We respect the process and unity of the party, but we have never blindly swallowed whatever is stuffed down our throats. If we disagree, we say we disagree.

That is why if I don't contest for Deputy President—even though I actually don't have much interest in being a minister or Deputy President—my worry is that it validates the public perception that Keadilan is full of yes-men and sycophants. Winning or losing is normal. I was Branch Chief of Kemaman in 2014, winning by just one vote! In 2018 I was unemployed, before being cleared from going to jail. I contested against Azmin even though he was a senior minister; I contested anyway. Winning or losing is not an issue. But defending the party's foundation in the eyes of the public outside—that we cannot be seen, after gaining power, as PKR being filled with yes-men and flatterers—that is what must be delivered.

Our current system has 30,000 delegates. Some were elected in the previous branch elections. When this head-decapitation phenomenon occurs, they didn't win everything. Many branches only won the branch chief post, while the committee below was won by others. It is by no means certain that when a branch chief declares support for a specific person, it reflects the feelings of the grassroots.

Then we have roughly 20,000 additional delegates based on votes obtained by all branch chief candidates. The winner of a branch chief post in a four-way contest sometimes didn't get 100% of the votes; he only got 30% of the votes. Other candidates held the remaining 70% of votes, and these are the ones appointing additional delegates.

What is more dangerous, the noise on social media and WhatsApp isn't about Nurul Izzah or me contesting. They are making noise because they are disgusted seeing this appear staged to victimize someone. That is what makes people angry. This is the problem when instigators and opportunists overstep their boundaries. They race to show loyalty and prowess by hastily issuing statements supporting this or that, while the public outside says "what has happened to you guys? Once you got power, you race to become flatterers?" That is what's dangerous.

Back in 1998, why did reformasi happen? Why did so many of us take to the streets to demonstrate? It wasn't purely because we idolized Anwar Ibrahim. Most of us didn't even know Anwar Ibrahim. We were ordinary citizens, but we were disgusted by the methods when we saw that just for internal party political contests, everything was mobilized to trample someone to the point of overkill. Even if you don't want him to win or don't want him there, say it nicely. I'll leave, no problem. There's no need to mobilize everything to make it look as if he lost support, and then have Ramanan come in saying "he doesn't look after the grassroots."

If this stays within the party, it's fine; you can cheer all you want, I don't mind. I worry when it spills over onto Twitter, social media, and TV—people will say "what is to become of your party?" Once it reaches that stage, if we shout "Reformasi" during the GE16 campaign, I'm not confident people will answer our call. People will say "you were tested with power; we saw that when you got power, we aren't confident you are any different from those before you. Don't bother wasting our time."

I am far more worried about that than this election. Winning or losing this election is your affair. But if it spills over and impacts public perception, ultimately the one bearing the burden will be none other than Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim himself heading into GE16 later. If not resolved properly, this will continue to hamper our campaign and shape public perception heading into GE16.

**Host:** You've explained all sorts of things earlier, YB. But amidst all this, we don't know the agenda behind these numerous joint statements. So there might be speculation or an agenda behind it. People often make accusations against you, YB—perhaps that you are stubborn, don't get along with the President, and so on. Because of that, for certain groups, no matter what you do—even if you drive the Ayuh Malaysia Truck across the entire universe and galaxy—they still won't like it and will want to bring you down. What do you say, YB?

**Host:** YB, you said you've explained various things earlier, but among all of this, we don't know the agenda behind these joint statements. So there might be speculation or people saying there's an agenda behind it. People always make accusations against you, YB, maybe that you are stubborn, don't see eye to eye with the president, and so on. And because of that, for some of these groups, no matter what you do—even if you move the Ayuh Malaysia Truck across the entire cosmos and the Milky Way—they will surely dislike it and still want to bring you down. How about that, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** A million-dollar question. Well, even our wives don't always agree with everything we do, right? I understand. I see that usually, what is planted for people outside the party is to build a perception that I am arrogant, don't listen to people, harsh, and so forth. I accept that. I'm almost turning 50 now, it's hard for me to change, that's just how I am. My issue is with political people, not just in PKR, but in other parties too it's often an issue. There are two problems. When I reflect every time people ask, comment, or give feedback: first, it's actually because I'm an outsider. I didn't come from a typical political background. I didn't enter politics to become a politician, for example. I'm a corporate guy, a professional who stepped in. So my culture is, if you're wrong, you're wrong. I don't know how to twist words to say nice things to your face and talk bad behind your back. I don't know any of that. As an outsider, that is the burden I will carry forever, because as an outsider, we will always be seen as non-conforming, going against the tide. Forever I know that because I'm like this and my character is like this, and if I don't agree with something, I don't agree—so as an outsider, I will remain non-conforming and there will always be people uncomfortable with me. Second, no matter what, there will always be dissatisfied people, not just inside the party, but outside as well. I think inside the government too—I've been a minister for two and a half years today. Because I am an outsider and a non-conformer, I challenge the status quo. Because the way I do things isn't the usual way or existing cultural practice.

I, for instance, do not evaluate someone in the party based on how passionately they kiss my hand. They can kiss my hand until it's wet. But I look at things objectively.
