# Interview (English)

**Host:** Assalamualaikum and peace be upon you. Welcome to the *Yang Berhenti Menteri* podcast, episode seven. Before we begin, I want to ask brother Daniel—coming to this podcast, aren't you afraid of being banned?

**Daniel Jani:** Who can ban me? I am the Mas Gading Branch Chief.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Aside from that, Daniel is a political influencer. He told me earlier that YouTube Malaysia informed him he is among the top 10 political influencers in Malaysia and has already received the Silver Play Button.

**Host:** Daniel, could you share a bit about that experience?

**Daniel Jani:** YouTube Malaysia contacted me via email two or three times to do a video call and provide advisory guidance. At first, I thought it was a scammer, so I ignored it for a few months. When I checked back, it really was from YouTube Malaysia. I was surprised because I felt there were many other political YouTubers who were roughly on the same level.

I truly started from the bottom. It's different for someone like YB Rafizi, who already has a big name. People like us who pave the way from the ground up see many challenges. As an influencer, people want to see what issues we comment on and discuss. On the topic of J-KOM, for example, there are techniques to it. I'm not saying I'm an expert, but these things can be learned.

**Host:** Daniel's channel is already monetised too, right?

**Daniel Jani:** Yes, it's already monetised. How could it not be?

**Host:** So the only person who hasn't monetised yet is brother Rafizi, even though he gets a lot of views.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Exactly, which means I need to monetise. I'll have to learn from Daniel later.

**Daniel Jani:** YouTube's requirements to monetise are easy now: 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours. I think on the very first day YB posted a video, it could have already been monetised.

**Rafizi Ramli:** To those watching out there, besides this podcast, every week we will have documentary-style content and Live TV broadcasts on my YouTube and the Ayuh Malaysia YouTube channel. So please subscribe and watch. When we monetise, that money won't go to me, but will be used as part of the funds to support social enterprises at the grassroots level.

**Host:** All right. Before we move on to the next question, I would like to inform everyone that a lengthier explanation and special discussion regarding the 13th Malaysia Plan (RMK13) will be held next week with a caliber economist, Dr. Muhammed Abdul Khalid.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, the RMK13 discussion requires a dedicated podcast session with an economist, not politicians. Let the Members of Parliament make noise and draw caricatures first. Next week, we will give our panelist the opportunity to comment on and summarize RMK13. So today, Daniel can focus on interviewing me with other hot-button questions.

**Daniel Jani:** This first hot question is regarding the post YB yourself made about the Farhash issue. Even though YB has resigned as minister, YB is still a PKR Member of Parliament and the Pandan Branch Chief. Why is it that when YB mentions the Farhash issue, so many people jump up and start shouting? Even though Farhash is neither a central leadership (MPP) member, a branch chief, nor a state leader. Why are so many people reacting so strongly?

**Rafizi Ramli:** For us in PKR, a large portion of the PKR leadership from the grassroots up to the MPP level who won recently are 'Farhash's boys'. Their loyalty (*walak*) to Farhash is just as high as their loyalty to Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim. Many senior PKR leaders understand this. Except in this party, people like me and Daniel are fighters. If there is something that brings no good to the party, we must speak up. The rest feel that as long as they save their own skins, that's enough.

This is a phenomenon that has been happening since 2019. There are figures who rely on the premise that they are Datuk Seri Anwar's 'right-hand men', supposedly representing his voice, and then orchestrate and dictate political moves from the branches up to the top level. To me, this is dangerous because it is politics riding on the back of Datuk Seri Anwar. They want Datuk Seri Anwar to do all the heavy lifting until becoming the government, but party positions are manipulated so they can place their people in influential spots.

To win an election, we cannot rely on just a single individual. We need to reach out to the people through leaders who are recognized and serve as intermediaries. This style of politics—hiding, riding on someone's coattails, and maneuvering behind the scenes—will bring down the party. That is why when it is touched upon, so many people jump up.

**Daniel Jani:** Usually, whenever Farhash's name is mentioned, he sends a suit letter. That is up to him. But if the party wants to be strong, the main key lies at the branch level—just like division chiefs in UMNO. If a branch isn't strong and fails to produce Members of Parliament or State Assembly Members (ADUNs), what is the point of fighting over top positions only for the party to end up ruined?

Many branch chiefs now actually don't have a PKR Parliamentary or ADUN seat in their areas because those seats belong to Amanah or DAP. PKR only has 30 MPs and 37 ADUNs right now. We are actually not strong enough yet. Is my view correct, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Every party has its strengths and weaknesses. Older parties like UMNO or PAS have strong grassroots strength and sentimental value because they have existed for a long time. But the strength of PKR and DAP is that we are 'issue-driven parties'. PKR's skill lies in the dynamism of leaders who succeed in building narratives that resonate closely with the people.

PKR's strength is its ability to frame and process issues. In elections during the social media era, fence-sitters make up about 60% of total voters, while diehard voters account for only 30% to 40%. Fence-sitters make last-minute decisions based on the issues raised.

However, what happened in PKR—especially in the last party elections—is that the party seems to have made a U-turn away from this strength. Leaders who served as intermediaries to the people were sidelined and lost. Those who won, the public doesn't even know. Now there exists a culture of hiding behind the power of the Prime Minister, hoping that power alone is enough to win elections. I have serious doubts about whether this model can succeed.

**Daniel Jani:** I agree. Many pro-government supporters or pro-government TikTokers nowadays only know how to defend, rather than create issues. They just wait for trending news, then make commentary.

**Rafizi Ramli:** In politics, reacting back and forth to current issues like that is like midfielders frequently passing the ball among themselves without ever mounting an attack on the goal. Whether on the government's side or the opposition's, if it's merely exchanging tweets or TikTok videos, it leads nowhere.

To ensure victory in the upcoming election, an effective offensive requires two elements. First, highlighting the excellence of government administration in a way that truly captures the hearts of the people. Second, fact-based attacks from PKR leadership against the scandals or weaknesses of the opposition.

If it's just TikTok content mocking PAS caricatures, that is merely a minor scratch, not a solid strike. Looking back at GE15, the two major issues that truly dealt a decisive blow and shifted votes were Najib Razak's imprisonment issue and the LCS scandal.

Right now, I see PKR leadership having a bunker mentality—retreating inwards, praising one another, and indulging in self-congratulation, as if the public actually cares about what they discuss among party members.

**Daniel Jani:** True. In Sarawak, for example, Sarawak PKR cannot use this minor-scratch approach if it wants to face GPS. Former Sarawak PKR figures like Baru Bian, Ali Biju, and Willie Mongin won because they were vocal in championing concrete issues like native customary rights (NCR) land issues and 1MDB. That's why we must be brave enough to speak up.

**Rafizi Ramli:** After the last party elections, the identity of the party shifted. The tactics and strategies that previously brought victory to the party were diluted and replaced with a new mantra: "We are now in power."

When we abandon our original strength and rely solely on power, it is extremely dangerous because power is intoxicating. It exposes the party to malpractice and corruption. PKR has represented the progressive voice in society for decades. If PKR loses its progressive core and identity, progressive voters will seek a new platform. We cannot merely say "let's unite behind Datuk Seri Anwar" just for these next 2 to 3 years. After the era of Datuk Seri Anwar, is PKR going to close shop? The party's struggle must transcend any individual.

**Host:** Some supporters offer a pragmatic argument that figures like Farhash are needed to replace the cronyism system of the Tun Mahathir era. Aside from that, compromise was also made for the sake of forming the Unity Government. How does YB view this argument?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Since September 20, 1998, for over 20 years we campaigned against the practices of KKN—Corruption, Collusion, and Nepotism. When the government changes, politicians can give all sorts of arguments claiming "this is just perception" or "it is a personal business." Back then, Tun Mahathir said the exact same thing. But the people are not stupid; the people can see.

When figures suddenly emerge becoming overnight tycoons, joining certain companies, and securing government contracts, it deals a heavy blow to PKR and Datuk Seri Anwar. The people will say: "You claimed for decades to fight corruption, but when you became the government, you did the same thing."

When Nurul Izzah was appointed, people alleged nepotism. Then the Farhash issue emerged along with leaked documents, and people alleged cronyism. For old-guard reformists from 1998, this is deeply disappointing.

I don't care if Farhash's faction wants to issue media statements or sue me. If they sue, even better—I can go to court and put questions forward in the witness box for them to answer. I do not rely on the government or the party to keep speaking out. This advice is for those who cherish their positions and power: if the party loses in the next election, you are the ones who will suffer.

**Daniel Jani:** I joined PKR back then to fight GPS in Sarawak after the Sheraton Move. But seeing the state of the party now, I feel disheartened too. Nevertheless, we have our own platforms to speak up.

I have one important question: What will happen if, in the next general election, the Prime Minister himself or Nurul Izzah loses in their respective seats? This is a risk that party members might not have thought about.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Being in Putrajaya has its pros and cons. The downside is that leaders are constantly surrounded by officials and supporters who only report good news—yes-men.

Let me give an example during the Sungai Bakap by-election. In a meeting with Datuk Seri Anwar, local leaders and ministers expressed high confidence that we would win easily. But when it came to my turn, I stated based on data that we would lose due to local water supply issues and voter sentiment toward the local representative. Reports reaching Putrajaya are usually pleasant to hear, which is why ever since 2016, I have consistently spent my own money to conduct weekly and monthly empirical studies.

Regarding the risk of contested seats: In GE15, I was the one who pushed Datuk Seri Anwar to contest in Tambun. At that time, there was a perception that he was desperate to become Prime Minister but only contested safe seats like Port Dickson. I said if he wanted to create a wave in Perak and across the country, he had to be brave enough to take the risk in Tambun.

However, for the upcoming election, the situation is more complex. If the Prime Minister moves from Tambun to a safer PKR seat in Selangor or Kuala Lumpur, the opposition will attack with the narrative that the Prime Minister is fleeing out of fear. If he stays in Tambun, it will be a fierce, high-risk battle.

The same goes for Nurul Izzah in Permatang Pauh. If she does not contest to reclaim Permatang Pauh and instead moves to a safe seat in Selangor, it reinforces the perception of entitlement as the President's daughter.

**Daniel Jani:** Speaking of GE15 seat strategy, what about Shamsul Iskandar being fielded in Bagan Datuk against Ahmad Zahid Hamidi?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That too was part of the strategy I arranged. At that time, Shamsul had already lost in the Paya Rumput state seat during the Melaka state election, and the Hang Tuah Jaya branch leadership had rejected him. If kept in Melaka, the risk of losing was extremely high. So the best strategy to rebuild his political career was to send him as a 'hero' to compete against a giant in Bagan Datuk. Even though he lost narrowly, he was seen as a party warrior.

However, for the next general election, placing senior leadership in risky seats will be extremely difficult because the majority of PKR seats right now are not in safe territory.

**Host:** Could YB elaborate on the safety data of PKR seats based on the current political scenario?

**Rafizi Ramli:** If Malay vote support for Pakatan Harapan (PH) remains around 30% as seen in several recent state elections and by-elections, and non-Malay voter turnout drops to 40%–50%, the majority of PKR's 30 Parliamentary seats are in danger.

The truly safe PKR seats might only be around 10 seats, such as Pandan, Subang, Bayan Baru, Gopeng, Selayang, and Johor Bahru (and even then, only if Akmal Nasir contests). Voters hold sentimental value—Akmal is a young man who defeated the UMNO giant, Tan Sri Shahrir Samad. If a figure like him is sidelined at the branch level and not nominated, JB voters will feel aggrieved.

Voters today are unpredictable compared to the past. Who would have thought Tun Mahathir could lose his deposit in Langkawi? If party leadership becomes complacent and comfortable with power without revitalizing the grassroots and framing key issues, it is not impossible for a dark history to be made where the Prime Minister candidate or key party leaders fall in the election.
