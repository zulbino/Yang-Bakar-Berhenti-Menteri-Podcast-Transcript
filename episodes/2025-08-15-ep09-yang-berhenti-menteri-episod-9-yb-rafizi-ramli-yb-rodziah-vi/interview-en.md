# Interview (English)

**Victor Tan:** Welcome to episode 9 of *Yang Berhenti Menteri*. I am here with YB Pandan, Rafizi Ramli, and YB Rodziah Ismail, Member of Parliament for Ampang. YB Rafizi, based on what your staff mentioned earlier, you shed tears recently, which is something we rarely see from a politician on television. As a human being, it is tough to imagine what you went through. How are you doing?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Good, alhamdulillah. As a politician or a Member of Parliament, whatever happens, we have to show resilience and maintain composure. When the threat and commotions first occurred, panic arose because we didn't know whether it involved poison or other dangerous substances. That uncertainty was what triggered the panic, especially for my wife and my son. 

In the first 24 hours, I had to bottle up all those emotions. Yesterday's press conference was the point where those pent-up emotions came out because I needed to pause for a moment. However, as I mentioned, this is part of the path that my family and I chose. We are used to going through this, so we learn to adapt quickly. My wife and son are doing fine. 

Daily routines must continue. When my team suggested that we postpone this week's podcast, in my view, that is exactly what bullies want. Bullies want to disrupt our lives until everything comes to a standstill. Therefore, the best course of action is to carry on with our work as usual, including today's podcast.

**Rodziah Ismail:** When the incident happened, it wasn't just Rafizi who was affected; all of us felt it too. Many of us reflected back on various things that we had bottled up all this time.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Even more so for PKR veterans like Kak Uji, who started as an activist and is also a mother. Throughout 27 or 28 years in this struggle, a lot of time with children was sacrificed. The struggle to bring about change demands a very high price, and that price has been paid by many people. For those holding responsibilities today, all we hope for is that the duty is carried out properly.

**Victor Tan:** Thank you to both of you for the sacrifices you continue to make for the nation. Today, we want to address several pressing issues, particularly surrounding bullying, the tragic case of Allahyarhamah Zara Eleena, and broader systemic questions in our education system. Do you remember when you first heard about the Zara Eleena case, and how did it come to your attention?

**Rodziah Ismail:** I first found out about the Zara Eleena case through social media when her story went viral on TikTok, about three or four days after the incident occurred. The first video I watched showed the late girl's mother expressing her anxiety and feeling that something was amiss that required further investigation. 

The deceased did not pass away at the scene; instead, it was a few hours after being brought to the hospital. Through her mother's cries, it is possible that the bullying did not just happen on the day of the incident, but had recurred prior to that. 

The question is, how was this case handled? Schools are supposed to be safe places for children. When various reactions and songs emerge on social media, it reflects public dissatisfaction because people want transparent answers and explanations.

**Victor Tan:** It has even become an international discussion, with users from Thailand and Singapore using the hashtag #JusticeforZara.

**Rodziah Ismail:** At the same time, several other incidents also occurred, such as those involving UTM students. I am worried that issues like this will become a norm where we merely listen without taking serious follow-up action. This is the time for us to re-evaluate the environment we provide for our children. Human life is not cheap; firm action and thorough reviews must be carried out.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I only received detailed information regarding the Zara case about a week ago while I was at home. My family is quite sensitive about the issue of school bullying because we have a child, compounded by the fact that both my wife and I are former residential school students. 

My wife was the first to show me the news. Initially, the information wasn't very clear—there were various versions like falling from a building and issues regarding the lack of an autopsy. When information is not conveyed transparently, it triggers widespread speculation. 

In my case, when an incident involving my son happened, I issued a media statement within two to three hours to clarify what could be explained without compromising the investigation. In the age of social media, it is crucial to curb wild speculation. Because the Zara case was not handled well in the initial stages, it became a deeply emotional issue for all Malaysians. The ministry and stakeholders have a responsibility at the highest level to ensure that SOPs, training, and emergency responses are executed efficiently.

**Victor Tan:** I saw an Instagram post that your son made some time ago regarding Allahyarham Zulfarhan. Your son wrote: *"Dear Abang Zulfarhan, Mommy and I will fight against any form of bullies. No one should die because of bullies. I was bullied in school before because daddy is my father. It took Mommy months to get me out of the trauma. How I wish help was on your way when you needed it the most."* This carries immense depth for a 12-year-old. How did your family navigate that experience?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That post indeed stemmed from his personal experience. Being the child of a controversial politician like me is not easy for him. He is a shy and introverted person. 

We personally went through the process of handling the bullying case he faced at school. Our experience showed that there is a stark difference between teachers who possess skills and empathy versus administrators who tend to blame the student. When a complaint is brought by a student or parents, an untrained teacher might view that student as a troublemaker, thereby adding to the child's emotional burden. 

That is why the issue of bullying is deeply personal to our family. When we moved to Putrajaya, we gave a full briefing to the school that my son did not want any special attention. However, as the child of a politician, he remains exposed to various views from peers coming from diverse political backgrounds. 

When I resigned from the cabinet a few months ago, his friends at school asked why his father was sacked or resigned. We never discuss politics at home, so he didn't have the information to answer those questions. I had to go through the process of explaining the actual situation to him. 

The Zara case shows that the issue of bullying cannot be handled merely with slogans. When I was in the cabinet, I repeatedly raised the issue of school bullying in ministerial committee meetings. I emphasized right from the first year that this matter should not be taken lightly. 

The Prime Minister, Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim, was always supportive and understood this issue because his grandchildren also face similar situations as family members of high-profile political figures. It's just that I was rarely satisfied with the ministry's answers because managing this issue requires a far deeper understanding.

**Rodziah Ismail:** This bullying problem has actually existed for a long time. As a member of the parliamentary select committee on bullying issues, we found that exposure or training for teachers to understand students' characters and the nuances of bullying is insufficient. Teachers are provided with psychology and learning pedagogy modules, but understanding children's rights and the diversity of student personalities is still lacking. 

Bullying also occurs in various non-physical forms. For example, some peers extort money or belongings from the children or grandchildren of elected representatives under the pretext that their families are well-off. Items frequently go missing or money is borrowed without being returned.

The main question is, where can students make complaints safely and quickly? The school authority and teachers need to be more sensitive to changes in student behavior so that early intervention can be taken.

**Victor Tan:** Looking back at your time at MCKK, you received the Carey Baokiah Award from Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim in 1994. There is a well-known quote by Kurt Vonnegut: *"True terror is to wake up one morning and discover that your high school class is running the country."* When you were at MCKK, was bullying a real issue, and how do you differentiate between traditional boarding school discipline and actual bullying?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I have experienced all kinds of physical actions like being slapped or kicked at MCKK. Managing this issue of bullying is actually more complex than managing the nation's economy because it involves various perspectives and situations. 

At MCKK, the culture was inherited from the UK public school system, where the school was largely managed by the student union and prefects, rather than teachers directly. Discipline was enforced very strictly. For example, dining etiquette practice with spoons and forks in the dining hall—if there was a clinking sound, the whole hall had to stand up and face penalties like running around the hall or not being allowed to eat. That training built meticulousness and discipline. 

At age 13, as head of the room or class captain, if there was trash under a bed during night inspection, the entire dorm would be subjected to push-ups until someone confessed. From there, I learned to take responsibility and confess on behalf of my friends. That experience truly shaped my character. 

However, a clear line exists. Collective punishment like push-ups or running was considered discipline, whereas personal physical actions like kicking or slapping someone was considered bullying. 

That is why during Form 3, despite qualifying academically and co-curricularly, I decided not to become a prefect in Form 4. I felt that the responsibility of determining punishments for others was too heavy. When 16- or 17-year-old teenagers are given authority without the proper instillment of values, power abuse and physical abuse easily occur.

**Rodziah Ismail:** Discipline aims to build character according to a proper structure, whereas bullying is an abuse of power outside of that structure. Today, however, some parents view disciplinary actions themselves as a form of bullying.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Correct, that line is getting blurrier now. Most senior teachers feel tied down because they can no longer cane or impose disciplinary measures on students who disrupt class order. There are situations where students openly challenge a teacher's authority, such as vaping in class. When teachers cannot act and parents are overly defensive of their children, teachers ultimately choose to wash their hands of the matter. It is this hands-off attitude that ultimately creates space for bullying among students to happen.

**Victor Tan:** Given how societal expectations have evolved, how should educational policy adapt to these complexities moving forward?

**Rafizi Ramli:** *Every generation blames the one before.* What we have today is the result of the previous generation's needs. Fully residential schools (SBP) and MRSM were created between the 1960s and 1990s because the government back then did not have sufficient resources to provide high-quality education across the country, especially in rural areas. 

But times have changed. I did not send my son to MCKK because I felt he would be taking up the spot of someone who needed it more. Furthermore, nowadays, students do not need to attend a fully residential school specifically to receive quality education. Our focus should be on ensuring that the quality of education in any daily school is on par with SBP or MCKK. 

Parents in the past, like my parents in 1990, placed complete trust in teachers. Today, parents' demands and expectations of schools are different. Policymakers and politicians cannot boast or talk down to the public; we need to be responsive, flexible, and find the most constructive middle ground. 

Education policy formulation must be proactive and agile, not running on auto-pilot. The Ministry of Education cannot be defensive or assume that everything is in perfect condition. Ministries like KDN, KKM, and KPM are on the front lines dealing directly with the people's daily lives. Ministers are responsible for listening openly, accepting current realities, and providing solid training for teachers.

**Rodziah Ismail:** Our main focus must be centered on the overall quality of education. Urban challenges, on the other hand, involve classroom overcrowding of up to 50 students per class and a lack of facilities. Once accessibility and digital infrastructure issues are resolved equitably, the quality of learning across the nation can be elevated.

**Victor Tan:** Moving to your personal background, the 1994 photo showing you receiving an award from Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim is widely circulating. Did you ever discuss that photo or share a casual personal relationship with him over the years?

**Rafizi Ramli:** We never discussed that photo. My relationship with Datuk Seri Anwar is purely professional. We have never discussed family matters or the past. Character-wise, I am not a romantic person; I am more focused on work. 

The day in that picture was the first time I met Datuk Seri Anwar and Datin Seri Wan Azizah during MCKK Speech Day in 1994. Since then, we have never brought it up.

**Rodziah Ismail:** It's the same within the party. Behind the scenes, we are truly focused on carrying out our work and rarely sit down to chat about personal matters.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Actually, that is one of the dynamics within PKR. There is a group that is very focused on tasks and work, and there is also a group that builds more personal relationships. My interaction with Datuk Seri Anwar has always taken place within an official work framework.

**Victor Tan:** I understand you also quit using WhatsApp a few years ago for mental health reasons. How has that affected your work and accessibility?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I exited all WhatsApp groups and stopped using the application around 2020 or 2021. My phone number has never changed since 2003, so it is widely known. People frequently added me to various political groups that argued continuously, alongside receiving endless free calls from unknown numbers. 

We only have 24 hours a day. When too much time is wasted replying to messages in groups, it disrupts work focus. I made the decision to completely leave WhatsApp. As long as I can be reached via SMS, email, or regular phone calls for important matters, that is sufficient. My life has been much calmer since then.

**Victor Tan:** Returning to your family, your son Ben shows remarkable depth and maturity in his writing. How do you manage his privacy as he grows older?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Ben is indeed mature in his own way, but he is very introverted. When he was young, at three or four years old, he often followed us campaigning across the country. However, upon reaching six or seven years old, he began to understand his surroundings and chose to withdraw from public attention. 

Initially, my wife opened an Instagram account merely to keep a record of family photos. But given current circumstances, we are considering setting his account to private mode to safeguard family privacy.

**Victor Tan:** There were also lighthearted comments online asking if Ben would be getting a sibling anytime soon.

**Rafizi Ramli:** We did try. Ben himself once asked why he didn't have a younger sibling. However, our blessing was for just one child because my wife and I married at a relatively late age, and my wife was pregnant in her late 30s. From the perspective of biological factors and political busy schedules, we accept this blessing. Besides, Ben now considers our 20 pet cats at home as his 'younger siblings'.
