# Interview (English)

**Host:** Welcome to *Podcast Yang Berhenti Menteri* episode 17. A special episode tonight. YB, did you just return from Parliament?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Yes, I just got back from Parliament.

**Host:** So no jogging today?

**Rafizi Ramli:** No, didn't go jogging today.

**Host:** All right, YB. To viewers who have just joined, please like, share, and subscribe to Rafizi Ramli's page and YouTube channel, as well as his other social media accounts. We also have a QR code for viewers who wish to ask questions live. All right, YB, let's head straight into our usual segment briefly before discussing the Budget. Let's look at the first issue in *Berimak*. Regarding the local fashion brand entrepreneur facing court action, what is your view, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That is everyone's right through legal channels, and the decision depends on the discretion of the court. I think netizens don't need to be too cruel. Compared to politicians facing breach of trust (CBT) charges and the like, give them a chance. As long as they comply with the rules and the court's requirements, I don't wish to interfere.

**Host:** Great, YB. Next, regarding netizens' responses to politicians joining social media trends or current waves of popularity?

**Rafizi Ramli:** If politicians want to show support, there is nothing wrong with that, as long as they don't overdo it. Personally, I refrain from jumping on trends outside my expertise so that such issues don't arise. Politicians sometimes like to show up when something is trending just to ride the wave. This is a reminder for politicians because the public is always watching. 

If an issue is not something close to your heart or something you have long championed, but you suddenly show up smiling when a trend emerges, people will question it. That is why some politicians dislike me from this perspective; some consider me hard to befriend or judgmental. But that is up to them. Most politicians don't read netizens' comments anyway, so they don't care.

**Host:** Do you yourself read netizens' comments regularly?

**Rafizi Ramli:** I read and scroll through comments. Not because I take things to heart, but as a politician, we need to have a stance. Some of our stances people like, some they don't. If you just want to be popular, become a celebrity, don't become a politician. Those comments offer insights into understanding the viewpoints of various groups.

**Host:** Next, YB, Wan Fayhsal's statement regarding the invitation to US President Donald Trump, linking it to inviting Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Perhaps that was a form of logic for him to counter the government's statement. The government stated that Trump was invited so that we could engage directly and convey our position. From Wan Fayhsal's logic, if that is the case, it would be better to speak directly to Netanyahu. That personal logic might be acceptable and rationalized in his own head, but to the public, it looks childish. 

It is like school children arguing and taunting each other. It is like throwing stones over something trivial. I don't mean the Palestinian issue is trivial, but the assumption that inviting Trump means you must also invite Netanyahu is nonsensical to the majority of Malaysians. But that's Wan Fayhsal.

**Host:** Regarding the controversial issue of land ownership and redevelopment in Kampung Sungai Baru, YB? Member of Parliament Shahidan Kassim stated that if he were still the Federal Territories Minister, this would not have happened.

**Rafizi Ramli:** It didn't happen during his time probably because no one was inciting people back then. Some of the parties playing up this issue have turned it into a partisan matter, indirectly creating a narrative as if under a Pakatan Harapan or DAP government, Malays would be driven out of urban areas. This sentiment was stoked by the opposition, so perhaps that is what Shahidan meant.

**Host:** All right, YB. Let's move on to a hot sports and citizenship issue regarding the registration of national heritage football players. How do you view this issue?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Initially, many gave the benefit of the doubt because detailed information was not yet available. From a football perspective, there are two differing views on the strategy of using import or heritage players. One view holds that the presence of these players elevates quality and enables clubs to compete commercially and sustainably at the regional level. Another view worries that if the football arena is overly dominated by foreign players, it shuts out opportunities for local players. I am not a football expert, so I won't comment further at that level.

However, the second aspect involves citizenship and its registration process at the Ministry of Home Affairs (KDN). When the Minister of Home Affairs, Datuk Seri Saifuddin Nasution Ismail, answered that he exercised his powers under the law after the committee was satisfied, it became a public issue.

When FIFA released evidence and documents showing that the player's family lineage was registered overseas, such as in the Netherlands, and there was no proof of birth in Malaysia, it raised serious questions. First, how was the verification process conducted at the Ministry level? Second, it is an embarrassment to the country. When FIFA produced such evidence, the majority of the public felt disappointed.

Third, many citizens feel dissatisfied because ordinary people or children of Malaysian mothers applying for citizenship have to wait decades, whereas the process for these players was expedited. So from a specific football issue, it turned into an issue of government accountability.

In my view, it is hard to defend this unless there are genuine legal documents proving they were born here. If the response seems to shift the blame for registration onto other parties like FAM while certificates were still issued, it is difficult to defend and only adds to netizens' anger. If I were still in PKR leadership and Saifuddin asked for my view, I would advise that this should not be blindly defended.

**Host:** Interesting, YB. Let's move to the *Fuiyoh* segment. Recently, an issue arose in Australia where consulting firm Deloitte submitted a report to the government containing wrong citations and facts due to the use of Artificial Intelligence (AI). How should the government respond to the use of AI?

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is not just a government issue, but an issue for all of us. I use ChatGPT and DeepSeek every day myself. But we cannot use them blindly. AI makes tasks easier when we know what we are looking for and ask for a summary. The key to using AI is precise prompts or instructions.

This is a human error problem. If we are not careful, whether in the government sector, private sector, schools, or universities, mistakes will happen. The use of AI will actually distinguish between individuals who are truly skilled and understand their work, and those who rely entirely on technology. In the Australian case, other researchers who reviewed it found the data was fake or fabricated. The officers and team involved were negligent because they failed to verify the initial findings.

Every piece of information from AI must be checked. A few months ago, I tried solving Form 5 and university-level mathematics questions, then input the same questions into ChatGPT and DeepSeek. For some algebra questions, DeepSeek claimed there was no solution when there actually was. So AI can give wrong answers. In daily tasks, we must check line by line and ensure AI does not breed a culture of laziness in organizations.

**Host:** Understood, YB. Now let me turn to the main topic of tonight's special edition, which is the Budget presentation. As a Member of Parliament and former Minister of Economy, how do you evaluate a budget?

**Rafizi Ramli:** A budget document is very thick, hundreds of pages long. It depends on the background of the individual evaluating it. When I was in accounting and taxation, accountants would look at the impact on income tax, indirect tax, investment incentives, capital allowances, and tax relief. Ordinary citizens and the media usually focus on the goodies or cash aid announced in the Finance Minister's speech.

As a politician and former minister, my main focus is looking at the overall financial integrity of the government at the macro level. It is like evaluating a company's financial statements—you have to look at the balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow. If the balance sheet is weak, the organization is exposed to risk. If revenue is not solid but expenditure keeps rising, the government is forced to rely on debt, which ultimately impacts cash flow.

In fact, there are five main budget documents besides the Finance Minister's speech:
1. Estimated Expenditure.
2. Fiscal Outlook.
3. Economic Outlook.
4. Federal Expenditure Estimates (a document of thousands of pages detailing staffing and agency breakdowns).
5. Further reports related to public finance.

Budget analysis needs to examine whether government expenditure can be covered by revenue. If it cannot be covered, there are only three possibilities: taxes are raised, more debt is incurred, or announced projects end up not being implemented. Only then do we evaluate whether expenditure distribution and spending priorities balance the need to stimulate the economy with helping the people.

One thing rarely discussed is our short-termism. When Budget 2025 was presented, people only looked at the 2025 figures and forgot about the actual performance in 2024. Yet the 2024 Revised Estimates show whether the government was efficient and able to meet targets or not.

For example, when Budget 2024 was presented last year, projected government revenue for 2024 was set at RM339.7 billion. However, in the latest document, the 2024 Revised Projection dropped to RM334.1 billion—a decrease of RM5.6 billion. This RM5.6 billion revenue decline is a significant amount. For comparison, the total expected savings from diesel subsidy targeting (Budi 95) is only around RM2.5 billion to RM4 billion a year.

When revenue collection shrinks by nearly 2%, it indicates that tax collection—despite the SST expansion—did not reach initial targets. This signals that economic growth is moderating. For Budget 2025, revenue is projected at RM343.1 billion (up RM9 billion from the 2024 revised estimate). However, if the economy moderates, companies make less profit and pay less tax, so that RM343.1 billion projection risks being missed.

**Host:** What about operating expenditure and development expenditure, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** In the public sector, expenditure is divided into Operating Expenditure (OE) and Development Expenditure (DE). 

OE includes emoluments (salaries), pensions, fixed payments, supplies, debt service charges, as well as subsidies and social assistance. Meanwhile, DE is CapEx—investment to build schools, hospitals, roads, and infrastructure that generates future economic value.

My concern is that when the government wants to reduce the deficit, Development Expenditure (DE) gets sacrificed. For 2024, the initial DE approval was RM84.7 billion, but the revised estimate dropped to RM78.7 billion. This means RM6 billion of DE was unspent—either deliberately held back to meet deficit targets or due to delays in project implementation at the ministry level.

Under the Federal Constitution, the government cannot borrow to fund Operating Expenditure (OE); debt can only be taken to fund DE. When we take on new debt, the interest payments (debt service charges) enter OE. The higher the debt, the higher the monthly/annual interest payments, which reduces our fiscal space in the future.

For 2025, DE is allocated RM79.5 billion. Compared to the initial 2024 plan (RM84.7 billion), this is a reduction of around 6%. A 6% cut in development expenditure within a single year is quite substantial and can negatively impact DE's role as a catalyst for economic growth.

The second deeply concerning issue is the Debt Service Charge. For 2024, interest payments on debt alone amounted to RM54.3 billion. Out of an operating expenditure of RM332 billion, interest payments consume around 17%.
It is like someone earning RM2,000 a month spending RM340 of their salary purely to pay credit card interest, without even reducing the principal debt amount.

International standards dictate that debt service charges should not exceed 15% of total government revenue. We have already passed that 15% threshold. For 2025, interest payments on debt are projected to increase further by 7% to RM58.3 billion—an increase of RM4 billion.

Imagine, we push hard to target diesel subsidies to save RM2.5 billion to RM4 billion, but those savings are completely wiped out just to cover the RM4 billion annual increase in debt interest payments.

**Host:** Does this mean that savings from subsidy targeting are not fully allocated where they should be, such as infrastructure development?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Ideally, subsidy retargeting is done to eliminate leakages and smuggling, and redirect funds to those truly in need or toward capital development expenditure.

However, in Budget 2025, total subsidies and social assistance decreased from around RM57 billion (2024) to RM49 billion (2025)—a reduction of RM8 billion. The issue that arises is that when subsidies are cut by RM8 billion, why is a large portion of those savings not felt in the form of additional aid or development projects, but instead absorbed by rising debt interest payments?

The number one problem in our national finances is the high level of debt. Our debt-to-GDP ratio for 2024/2025 has reached around 64.2% to 64.3%, while the statutory debt ceiling set by law is 65%. This debt ceiling was raised from 55% ten years ago to 65% during the pandemic era.

For comparison, other upper-middle-income countries like Indonesia have a debt ratio of around 40%, while Chile, which successfully transitioned into a high-income nation, maintains a debt ratio of only around 30%.

If our economy grows below the target of 4.5%–5.0% next year, our margin to avoid breaching the 65% ceiling is extremely narrow. If a global economic shock occurs, the government will have only two choices: go to Parliament to raise the debt ceiling again, or cut development expenditure. If development expenditure is cut, economic growth will shrink further—becoming a vicious cycle.

**Host:** What about the government's revenue structure, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** On a positive note, the country's dependence on petroleum revenue has decreased significantly. About 20 years ago (around 2008), oil revenue contributed nearly 40% to government revenue. Now it is below 20%. The main contributors now are direct taxes (individual and corporate income tax) and indirect taxes (SST, excise duties). This makes the revenue profile more sustainable against fluctuations in global oil prices.

However, our tax-to-GDP ratio remains low, below 15% (around 12%). This indicates that there are either still leakages and tax avoidance, or the local private sector has not recorded high enough profit margins to contribute larger tax revenues.

What is not yet radically visible in this budget is a strategy to manage expenditure differently from past practices. Much focus is placed on raising revenue (SST expansion, stamp duties on various instruments), but concrete commitments to drastic operating expenditure controls and public procurement governance reforms remain lacking in detail. 

Taxpayers want to see this restructuring yield results in the form of a tangible reduction in public debt.

**Host:** Looking at the breakdown of Development Expenditure (DE) by sector, the transport sector received the largest allocation, while the agriculture sector received only around RM550 million. What is your view on this, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Within the economic sector DE allocation (RM37 billion), the Transport Sector takes up almost half (RM17.5 billion) due to large infrastructure projects like the MRT and LRT.

However, the DE allocation for Agriculture, at only about RM550 million (less than 2% of the overall economic sector DE), is quite low, even though we frequently emphasize food security. Operating expenditure (OE) for agricultural services is indeed large (around RM2 billion for fertilizer subsidies and assistance), but if we only channel subsidies without modernizing the agricultural industry through development expenditure, it will be difficult to transform this sector into a high-productivity industry.

The same applies to the Trade and Industry sector (around RM3 billion). The question is, is RM3 billion sufficient to enable local manufacturing industries to transition toward high technology and automation? 

Overall, the total DE for 2025 (RM79.5 billion revised estimate) shows a downward trend compared to 2023 (RM96 billion) and 2024 (RM84 billion). This decline in public capital expenditure risks slowing down long-term economic growth.

**Host:** Regarding specific announcements such as the free education initiative through PTPTN for low-income groups, what is your commentary, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The intention to provide free education to low-income families is good. However, we need to look at the details of the mechanism.

Government-guaranteed PTPTN debt has now reached RM41 billion. If this initiative means the government directly transfers public funds to PTPTN to pay student tuition fees without fixing PTPTN's funding model and financial structure itself, it does not solve PTPTN's core issue.

The existing government already subsidizes up to 90% of tuition fees at public universities. PTPTN loans are usually used to cover the remaining 10% of fees and student living expenses. 

We do not want a merely "cosmetic and panadol" approach—resolving external symptoms without treating the root financial causes of statutory bodies. The country has previously faced heavy financial implications from contingent liabilities in cases like 1MDB, Tabung Haji, and FELDA, which required multi-billion ringgit government injections for recovery. PTPTN must be managed carefully so that this RM41 billion implicit debt problem does not explode in the future.

**Host:** Thank you, YB, for the comprehensive macro insights. Before we end tonight's episode, could you share the progress of the Social Enterprise projects under Ayuh Malaysia?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Over the past two months, I have focused on two main social enterprise projects:

1. **Kesum** (Value Meal Eatery): Selling complete meals priced at RM5 and below. "Kesum" stands for *Kenyang, Sedap, dan Murah* (Full, Delicious, and Affordable).
2. **FlexiMart**: A convenience store selling basic necessities and ready-to-eat meals according to buyers' affordability.

Insya-Allah, in about 3 to 4 weeks (around late October or early November), we will open the first outlet. The process takes time as it involves site selection, registration, license approvals, and shop renovations.

I have already conducted food tastings twice for the RM5 menu, but I am not yet satisfied with the quality. This weekend, I will discuss it further to ensure the recipes are genuinely delicious and high quality before going on sale.

Once the business models for these two stores stabilize and prove sustainable, we will open this template to interested members of the public. As for other initiatives like the device loan scheme, we have received the first 17 laptops, which are currently being refurbished for underprivileged students. For the human capital agriculture project, we have identified 7 to 8 offered land sites. These projects will roll out in phases starting this November.

**Host:** Thank you very much, YB Rafizi, for your time and sharing tonight.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Thank you. Have a good weekend, everyone.
