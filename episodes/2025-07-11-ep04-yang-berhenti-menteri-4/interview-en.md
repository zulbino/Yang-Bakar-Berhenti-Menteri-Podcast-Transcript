# Interview (English)

**Interviewer:** Assalamualaikum and welcome to the fourth episode of *Podcast Yang Berhenti Menteri*. As always, I am joined by YB Rafizi Ramli. YB, how are you?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Why wouldn’t I be okay?

**Interviewer:** And as always, we have a guest speaker with us, Faiz Ahmad. How are you, Faiz?

**Faiz Ahmad:** I’m doing well, and thank you, YB, for replying to my message. Oh right, you forgot.

**Rafizi Ramli:** It followed the mood, actually. If you happen to be on the FYP [For You Page] or you want to send a message, when I was holding my phone, I replied. There are thousands of people who I don't reply to. I messaged YB Nik Nazmi, and he didn't even reply—he just looked at it.

**Interviewer:** Busted! Alright, Faiz is an influencer and economic commentator on TikTok, so for those who haven’t followed him, please do. Before we dive into the discussion, Faiz, could you tell us a bit about yourself?

**Faiz Ahmad:** I worked as a market analyst for a global company based in Taiwan for five years. I started as a junior analyst, then senior, and finally became a chief analyst. My job was to look at the global economy. Before this, I didn't touch the Malaysian economy at all because I knew if I did, it would turn into politics. I kept myself away. But after I resigned and started doing my own thing, people started asking if the Malaysian economy was okay, because the narrative outside was that it was performing poorly. At the time, YB was still the Minister of Economy, so I responded. I explained that to read the economy, you need the basics. From there, people started attacking me for saying the Malaysian economy was okay. The attacks weren't small—messages saying I was anti-Islam, or that it was because I’m from Kelantan. If I were from Selangor, I wouldn't have been attacked.

**Interviewer:** Do you actually have a degree in economics?

**Faiz Ahmad:** My degree is in medical lab science. Then I did a master’s in forensic medicine. I spent two years at the National Institute of Forensic Medicine doing post-mortems. I got bored with the dead bodies, so I moved on.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I moved too, and I studied by myself, YB. If you’d stayed at the medical examiner’s office, you wouldn't have been beaten up [online].

**Faiz Ahmad:** I would have been beaten up in my sleep!

**Interviewer:** Okay, good. YB, no more ice-breaking. I’ve asked how many times we should connect with our guest. When was the first time you two met?

**Rafizi Ramli:** No, he’s on the FYP. I’ve seen him many times at political talks. I’ve never missed a talk, baby. But I took notice when he was on the FYP. I thought, "This kid is going to get hit." Then it just so happened that Faiz sent a message.

**Rafizi Ramli:** I do encourage more people who invite others to dissect issues objectively. Some are right, some are wrong, so I think it’s a good addition.

**Interviewer:** Since we have Faiz here, YB, a couple of days ago you made a statement about *Ayuh Malaysia* regarding the third-sector economy or social enterprise, which is still quite lacking in Malaysia. Since we have Faiz here, I’ll get straight to the point: Malaysia has very few social enterprises. How will Malaysia make a difference?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Social enterprises in Malaysia are very small in terms of scale and impact. Legally or structurally, they don't really exist yet in Malaysia. There is no specific law for them. You either have companies motivated by profit, which are taxed just like any other business, or you have NGOs that don't promote profit but face many constraints—they can't engage in income-generating activities. We have corporations, but the law is very complex. From the perspective that the state can no longer keep expanding the size of government and the civil service—especially in sectors involving public service like eldercare or food assistance—we need a new form of enterprise. We need economic activities motivated by social good that don't take profit for owners, but are managed efficiently. This will create jobs and foster more entrepreneurs. At the same time, it will train our society to take care of itself without being too dependent on government benefits.

**Interviewer:** Can you give us an example of a model that already exists globally that we can study?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Social enterprises evolve. In the 70s and 80s, the world often looked at the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, led by Prof. Muhammad Yunus. It was micro-credit for the poor. That concept eventually became *Amanah Ikhtiar* in Malaysia, but it still has problems because it isn't sustainable; it relies on government injections. The newer social enterprise models are better because they are more nimble. They are essentially normal businesses, but the concept is very Islamic—using a *musyarakah* [partnership] concept. We have equity partners, asset partners, and operative partners—the people who actually do the work. This has existed in Islamic finance and global oil and gas contracts for a long time. 

**Interviewer:** So, if I want to open a convenience store, is that how it works?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Let’s say you open a store. The usual business model is the owner puts up RM500,000, hires five people, and takes all the profits or losses. In a social enterprise, three partners sit at the table. The equity partner puts up the RM500,000 but doesn't operate it. You, as the operative partner, are the manager, and you have equity too. It’s pre-profit and post-profit. The equity partner takes a minimum return, the asset partner takes their rent, the workers take their salary, and whatever profit is left is split differently, often with more going to the workers.

**Interviewer:** Was this idea brought forward while you were the Minister of Economy?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It should have been in the 13th Malaysia Plan. But because this concept is complicated, and government processes involving feedback and inter-ministerial conflict can take three years, I decided that through *Ayuh Malaysia*, we would just start it ourselves. 

**Interviewer:** This sounds like it’s heading towards socialism.

**Rafizi Ramli:** No, it’s not. Socialism doesn't have the concept of private ownership or differentiated profit. This is charity enterprise. It’s very Islamic. Because the current global system is stuck between pure capitalism and communism/socialism, this Islamic middle ground is a very good way to bring capital and ensure the motivation to strive exists.

**Interviewer:** Can we move forward without government support?

**Rafizi Ramli:** For the beginning, we can. The only difference is we have to pay taxes. Social enterprises, because they aren't profit-motivated, should ideally be taxed less, but for the time being, we can operate. 

**Interviewer:** YB, you’ve mentioned the T15 [top 15% income earners] classification for subsidy rationalization. Is that still pending cabinet approval?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It is the government's intention. The data is already there. But because it relates to a statement about the 85% who won't be affected, it requires cabinet scrutiny. In the electricity subsidy rationalization, we used energy consumption profiles, which was effective. The desire for RON95 rationalization is based on that same logic.

**Interviewer:** When will the RON95 rationalization start?

**Rafizi Ramli:** That’s up to the Minister. I’m not sure because the last time I discussed it, there were different views. The Ministry of Finance makes the final decision on the mechanism—whether it's cash or cards. That, you have to ask the Ministry of Finance.

**Interviewer:** YB, regarding the economy, people often ask if you are helping the government or acting as an opposition.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Whoever is the government will face the same fiscal problems. The issue isn't really about cutting SST [Sales and Service Tax]. If you cut RM40 billion in taxes, you have to find that RM40 billion somewhere else. We have to be frank. If the people have reached a level where they understand these trade-offs, they can make informed decisions because money doesn't fall from the sky. 

**Interviewer:** Regarding national debt, why doesn't it go down? People see the debt accumulating.

**Rafizi Ramli:** There are two perspectives. Don't just look at the absolute numbers like 1.3 trillion. Look at the debt-to-GDP ratio and the debt service charges. Our current situation is not good—the debt-to-GDP ratio is around 67-68%, and our debt service charges are at 16.1%. We have inherited massive legacy debts from previous projects like the ECRL, MRT, and 1MDB, which we have to pay for through our development budget every year. About 50% of our development budget is actually just servicing past debts. We are working to turn this around by 2028.

**Interviewer:** Overall, is the Madani economy performing well or underperforming?

**Rafizi Ramli:** From a macro perspective, 2024 checks all the boxes: stock market performance, 5.1% growth, stable inflation at 2%, and rising wages. These results show that the policies are starting to work. 2025 will be tougher, but we are heading in the right direction. If you ask ordinary people, they might feel the pinch because inflation doesn't go away once prices rise, and salary increases take time to reach them. That's why we have to be careful with policy sequencing to avoid causing unnecessary inflation.

**Interviewer:** Actually, there is a narrative claiming that people have lost 1.9% of their income, and this issue is being played up on social media as proof that the Madani government has failed to handle the cost of living. Furthermore, since you are moving toward a progressive wage policy, how effective will that approach be in addressing this issue?

**Rafizi Ramli:** It takes time. However, first of all, we must look at the context. If we take the average over five years, inflation rose by 9% while salaries only rose by 7%. When these cumulative figures are questioned against the current government, we have to ask: who caused those cumulative figures to spike in 2021 and 2022? 

Successfully controlling inflation is not easy. People might not feel it, but we managed to lower it from 4% in November 2022 when we took over, to around 1.4% currently. If Madani had failed, that inflation would have remained at 4% or higher, making the cumulative figure not 9%, but 15%. We must be fair; that failure is something we inherited. However, the salary issue is a structural issue within our economy.

**Interviewer:** Our society often takes a simplistic approach. It is easy for people to curse employers, accusing companies of being evil for not wanting to pay high wages. How do you see this?

**Rafizi Ramli:** There are indeed such employers, but before we point fingers, we must look at our country's economic composition. Large companies in our country only make up 2%. So, if there are vacancies in large companies like Petronas or Maybank that have thousands of employees, that is only 2% of the total. Next, we have medium-sized companies, which are service companies with revenue of less than RM20 million. Then, 15% are small companies, and 80% are micro-businesses like food vendors or small stalls where the staff consists of only about five people.

So, when people say "evil employers," we have to see which type of employer they mean. If large companies are indeed stingy, that certainly requires firm policies and action. However, the Socialist Party of Malaysia (PSM), for instance, wants the government to just order employers to raise wages. We need to balance this. That is why my previous decision was to protect small and medium enterprises through government intervention. We cannot force them by mandating rates that are too high, other than the minimum wage of RM1,700. To raise the wage ladder to RM2,000, the government needs to provide a subsidy.

**Interviewer:** Isn't the employer also responsible for improving employee skills?

**Rafizi Ramli:** True, employers have a responsibility for upskilling. If we want to provide subsidies for everyone, we would need to spend RM5 billion to RM7 billion a year given our limited capabilities. Current government programs are targeted at large companies (JLC). Large companies have profits in the hundreds of millions; so if they pay wages below RM2,000, they deserve to be reprimanded. Even the progressive wage requirements do not allow foreign or large companies to act arbitrarily.

It is specific to medium-sized companies and below, which are actually the largest employers in our country. You cannot paint all employers with the same brush, because they are also struggling with cost pressures. If forced with a wage ladder that is too high—for example, a fresh graduate salary of RM3,500—there are only two possibilities: either they fold, or they move workers into the informal market as contractors without EPF to avoid costs, or they raise the price of goods, which eventually increases inflation.

**Interviewer:** You have raised many problems, but what is the solution? You are the government now.

**Rafizi Ramli:** First, we need to shift away from the legacy economy. We have great factories, but the contents don't generate the technology itself. We pay royalties to outsiders and produce commodities. In the mid-term review of the 12th Malaysia Plan, we introduced high-growth, high-value sectors such as the digital economy, electronics (E&E), energy transition, critical minerals, and modern agriculture.

The government's task is to restructure our economic sectors to shift to high value. The Ministry of Economy is working with the semiconductor industry so that within five years, Malaysia can produce its own chips. We want to move from just assembling chips to creating chips. We are working with ARM, which holds a monopoly on global chip technology, so that we get that technology IP. When that happens, the types of jobs generated will no longer be engineers looking at chip assembly, but engineers designing chips. Their salaries will be different.

**Interviewer:** What about the issue of FDI? The Prime Minister is often labeled as the "Father of FDI Potential," but the people want to know how to check whether those investments are actually happening or not.

**Rafizi Ramli:** In terms of official reporting, the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) reports FDI annually according to international standards. The FDI reported by DOSM is FDI that has been spent. However, the investments announced by MITI through MIDA are "approved FDI." It’s like a construction company; they announce the value of the projects they get (like an order book), but the actual work or revenue recognition only happens when the project is truly underway.

The process from getting MIDA approval until the factory is operational usually takes three years. So, "potential FDI" is the order book, while DOSM data is what has been recognized because it has been spent. So far, our FDI realization rate is around 80%, which is a good figure.

**Interviewer:** There are also netizens claiming that government data can be manipulated. How is the integrity of data under you?

**Rafizi Ramli:** DOSM serves international standards, not the minister. Previously, there was an issue where the government tried to control inflation data. When I became the Minister of Economy, I insisted that data should be announced directly to the market without needing cabinet or PM approval. This is an issue of integrity. If the market does not trust our official data, our economy will not grow. Countries like China took a long time to build international trust in their statistical data. DOSM officers are very professional; ministers change, but they continue to defend the integrity of the data.

**Interviewer:** Regarding the Ringgit, which is often attacked for being low, why isn't it viewed as a decline that has been happening for the last 15 years?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The drastic decline happened in 2015 due to the drop in oil prices and the 1MDB crisis. It was a double whammy. If we compare economic parameters—currency, GDP growth, and inflation—the current situation is the best compared to 2015 or 2022. The Ringgit has stabilized at the 4.2 level. We want to see it do better, but current economic growth is more stable and inflation is controlled at a low level. It is unfair to say the Madani government is weak because many things we are doing now are to fix the economic structure.

**Interviewer:** If the election is held in two years, will you defend the government or oppose it?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The vote is secret. However, in the next two years, we will face many world economic challenges. As a member of parliament, my responsibility is to speak on behalf of the people. If there is a minister who I feel is slow or whose actions are incorrect, I will criticize them. Some people in the government are uncomfortable when I am vocal, but I think it is necessary. The people will judge. If we adopt a "holier than thou" approach or assume the people are stupid, it will backfire.

**Interviewer:** What is the biggest mistake in the government's economic communication?

**Rafizi Ramli:** The biggest mistake is communication that is not translated into simple analogies. The economy is actually not complex—it's about costs, inputs, and outputs. However, ministers often rely on civil servants who write using technical language like "fiscal glide" or "moderating inflation," which the people do not understand. We need ministers who can be good economic spokespersons. Fahmi Fadzil, as the Minister of Communications, is the most qualified person to translate these things into language that the people understand. Do not let government communication become one-way and arrogant.

**Interviewer:** Can we believe in the integrity of PMX?

**Rafizi Ramli:** Integrity is broad, including the management of money and the way we manage the country. For now, I am the person in the government who can criticize the Prime Minister. If I feel there is something that needs to be improved, that is my job. We in PKR have been fighting for reform for 30 years. Now is the real test. The judge is not Anwar Ibrahim or me, but the people. They will judge how we handle issues like judicial appointments and the concerns of the bar council. We cannot ignore the pulse of the people. If we ignore it, it is like a fever; if the temperature rises and we do nothing, it will get worse.

**Rafizi Ramli:** If we adopt a 1MDB or Najib-style approach—saying everything is broken or a betrayal—then you can be killed off. But if we manage it well and maintain checks and balances, the people will see that it’s acceptable. In modern democracies elsewhere, different people within the same party hold different opinions.

Bernie Sanders never agreed with the Democrats, and Elizabeth Warren, a very senior Democrat senator, held views that were very different from Joe Biden’s because she occupied the left wing of the party. But as a whole, the public sees that there is a check and balance within their party. 

The judge is out there, and my job is to say what I think needs to be said. If, for example, the issue of the judiciary is not a new thing.

This has been going on for a long time. You can talk to lawyers—I have many lawyer friends because I’ve dealt with a lot of cases—and they have been discussing this for a year. It is not really about Tengku Maimun not being the head of the state judges; it is about why this vacuum is being left, and what is happening?

If the Judicial Appointments Commission (JAC) has sent a name and you do not agree, it’s fine; ask for a new name so there is no long-term vacancy. But the next question is: if all the names sent through the JAC process are not accepted, then why does the JAC exist? 

These questions need to be answered. If we in Pakatan Harapan (PH) and the government take a "holier than thou" attitude—acting like we can't do anything wrong—I think we will just be digging our own graves. 

It is constructive criticism for me. It depends; different people see it differently. That is why my advice to you is, one day, let’s say you become a minister or a Member of Parliament, you must do what your conscience tells you is right.

You cannot make decisions based on, "If I do this, people will say that," or "If I do this, people will say I'm trying to bring them down." From a collective angle, we are one organization, and you can’t expect everything to be perfect. 

There are two sides to the collective process. If you are in party leadership, you go through that collective responsibility. Once you are not, take my example. When I didn’t become Deputy President, was it still collective? No. I was just a party member. I am willing to answer for that.

And this issue—how I bring it up now—is it the same as before? It’s quite obvious regarding collective responsibility. My views have been voiced for a long time; for example, regarding the selection of PKR candidates. All the things that happened, we voiced them internally. But once I think you cross a certain line, the decision is up to me. The ball is in my court. I know that the views I have voiced in the past have had no effect. It is impossible for me to sit inside, take office as a minister, and have the president be appointed, while outside I say something else. Because of that, you have to resign. 

Because of that, it is quite obvious that the views I conveyed previously were not accepted. And if I stay inside, then I am bound by collective responsibility no matter how much I shout; it is not going to make a difference. If it has reached a level where I feel, "Okay, this is a red line," then the best thing to do is to step out and no longer be part of that leadership because I am no longer tied to collective responsibility. 

It’s the view of an ordinary person; you become a minister and all that. It’s precisely because if I stay, I have strong views on several matters I have already stated before.

What I have raised in the last week or two, I have already used internal channels for previously. In a party like ours, there are only two official internal channels. One is at the party level—in PKR, it's either the Political Bureau or the Central Leadership Council (MPP). In PH, it’s the Presidential Council. That is the official party channel. The other is the Cabinet. But most of these discussions were one-on-one. 

I feel everything that needed to be said has been said. Finally, the ball was in my court. I know the government’s decision is different from what I proposed—for instance, my stance on targeted subsidies, where I believed the best way was through monthly cash transfers differentiated by district and family size. That view was not agreed upon by the Ministry of Finance. So, I have exhausted everything. If I stay, I have to support that. If I feel, "Okay, I don't want to be in the way," then you go ahead. 

**Interviewer:** One day, let’s say YB becomes Prime Minister, people under you will be—

**Rafizi Ramli:** No, I’m just playing with cats! Hypothetical questions are dangerous. Hypothetically, if YB enters the fray, YB will also face the same thing, right? People under you might not share your opinion in terms of implementation. It will repeat the same thing. 

I think every leader has their own unique way of managing differing views. My view on the best way is to ensure there is enough process and time to accommodate those views. Sometimes, what happens in our country is that the Prime Minister decides many things, so if we aren't careful, even at the party level—it's the same, isn't it? The Central Leadership Council meets, and they go on and on talking, but actually, it was already decided. 

I’ve only been back a month—nobody told me. Actually, it was already decided what was going to happen. There are conditions for choosing candidates. You go through the process, but actually, it's already decided. But you can still fight it, right? If we respect the process, when there is a fight or a difference of opinion, that is where the give and take happens. You go through the process, but if after 10 times, you give 100% and the take is zero—well, forget it. By then, you know. 

It is the same in the Cabinet or anywhere else. The process and the collective conviction in reform will be judged based on what finally happens. 

**Interviewer:** Fair enough. Satisfied? 

**Rafizi Ramli:** Satisfied. That was a productive hour and fifty minutes. I consider this my political education from YB. 

**Interviewer:** This was about the economy and politics.

**Rafizi Ramli:** The economy is for one and a half hours. Don't listen to me; go and learn from Mujahid or Hatta, God willing. If you go and ask Dr. Hatta, he will say he is a fighter. Don't follow him!

**Interviewer:** Very interesting. I think that's it for tonight. Maybe we will meet again next week.

**Rafizi Ramli:** True, I want to go home now. Thank you very much, Faiz, for coming. We’ll see who our next guest speaker will be. That’s all from us. Assalamualaikum, good night.

**Interviewer:** You sound like you're closing the show. Good night. The struggle continues. 

**Rafizi Ramli:** Time to change clothes.
