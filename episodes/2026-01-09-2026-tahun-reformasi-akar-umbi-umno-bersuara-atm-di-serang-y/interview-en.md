# Interview (English)

**Farhan:** Reasonable excuse. Alright YB, let's proceed to our first segment.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Okay, we have to be disciplined with this *Beriya* [overenthusiastic/over-the-top] segment. Some say it's unnecessary, but in reality, there are various audience segments. Some like it, some don't. So we'll try to keep it brief and concise.

---

**Farhan:** Alright YB. For the first *Beriya* issue, we have the title "You've Got a Friend in Me". This concerns the reaction of Kamil Munim, Chief of AMK Keadilan and Political Secretary to the Prime Minister, towards Dr Akmal Saleh's call wanting UMNO to leave the government.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Traditionally, youth wing leaders in any party are supposed to be fierce. The stance taken by Dr Akmal has a big impact on the government and Kamil's own boss, Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim. In the public's view, it should be a lion fighting a lion. This is like a sheep sticking its head out to a lion.

**Farhan:** Meaning he has to defend the government?

**Rafizi Ramli:** For podcast listeners on Spotify, this is regarding Kamil Munim's reaction to Dr Akmal. We thought he would be fierce, but his tone was rather melodramatic and he even wished him "All the best".

**Haziq:** Or maybe they want to portray a new image of political maturity? Maybe some people view it from that perspective.

**Rafizi Ramli:** Being mature doesn't necessarily mean being soft. Maturity is not judged by whether one is melodramatic or not. Maturity means taking into account all perspectives, then making the most fair decision and assessment based on those perspectives.

In this case, the AMK Chief should have stated that Barisan Nasional (BN) and Pakatan Harapan (PH) have made a commitment to ensure political stability for the sake of the people. Whatever the situation, component parties in the Kerajaan Madani must adhere to the agreement that has been signed. Maturity means calling a spade a spade [speaking truthfully and directly]. That's why he is considered *Beriya*—even holding Dr Akmal's belly, and Dr Akmal stroked him back.

**Farhan:** Okay YB, let's look at the next *Beriya* issue. Still regarding Dr Akmal Saleh and the statement from the Bukit Bintang Bersatu Division Chief touching on the Malay unity issue and claiming "Malays are increasingly divided".

**Rafizi Ramli:** Dr Akmal was referring to DAP and infighting within Malay parties. Meanwhile, the statement by the Bukit Bintang Bersatu Division Chief is a sequence of events following the sacking and suspension of leaders within Bersatu itself. Whatever it is, let them fight. The more they fight, the better it actually is for the people of Malaysia.

**Farhan:** Do they really represent the Malay community as a whole, YB?

**Rafizi Ramli:** You'll have to ask the voters. If we look back since GE14 in 2018, and even GE13 in 2013, the Malay vote has never stayed with a single party. The last time the Malay vote was solid for UMNO-BN was in 2004. 

In GE12 in 2008, although UMNO-BN still won, the Malay popular vote for Pakatan Rakyat back then was around 43% to 45%. In GE13 in 2013, it dropped slightly to 43%. Entering GE14 in 2018, the Malay vote split into three main coalitions: BN, PAS, and PH. That situation continues to this day. 

Recent data and survey studies show that 52% of Malay voters feel that all political parties are the same. So if politicians fight because each wants to claim to represent the Malays, that is an unwise action. The fact that this "divided Malays" narrative continues to be played up indirectly shows these parties are aware that no single party can claim to represent the Malay community absolutely.

**Farhan:** Next, the issue of Dr Akmal hinting at stepping down.

**Rafizi Ramli:** There's no need to just drop hints. If you want to step down, step down properly. This tactic of hinting at resigning can only be used once or twice. If it doesn't happen the first time, and the second time you mention it again but don't do it, people won't take you seriously ever again.

But for Dr Akmal, if at 38 years old he is truly convinced that Malay unity is the heartbeat of politics and his contribution to the country, he should stick to that principle. If he doesn't resign while UMNO's top leadership has insisted on remaining in Kerajaan Madani, he will be in a very difficult dilemma. 

But if he really resigns holding fast to his principles, I respect that. Respect is gained when someone consistently defends their views. We'll wait to see if this is truly a principled move or merely a political gimmick.

**Farhan:** And the final *Beriya* issue concerns the viral video of a man wearing a headscarf and drinking alcohol during a Christmas dinner party.

**Haziq:** The incident involves a male employee at a salon in Subang Jaya who wore a headscarf and drank beer during Christmas dinner. It went viral after being uploaded by his employer on social media, and the police have summoned them for investigation under Section 298.

**Rafizi Ramli:** My advice: anyone who is not a woman shouldn't go all *Beriya* wearing a headscarf. Men are not obligated to cover their heads, it's not aurat. Appreciate the hair you have. Secondly, if you are a Muslim, do not drink alcohol. 

For this week's *Beriya* segment, the winner is the issue of the man wearing a headscarf. The employer was equally *Beriya* to go and upload that video publicly on social media.

---

**Farhan:** Alright YB, let's move into our main topic. Revolving around the discussions at the recent UMNO Youth Convention, various grassroots voices have emerged. Does this rhetoric or stance by UMNO have a positive impact in terms of boosting support for them?

**Rafizi Ramli:** We have to look at voter segments. The priority of Malay unity from the perspective of die-hard UMNO supporters is more about political strategy: if Malay voters are united under one roof, since Malay voters are the majority, then they can win many seats and return to power easily. They have nostalgia for the past, where joining UMNO meant a high chance of becoming a minister.

However, the reality of Malay voters today is different and more complex:
1. **Educated urban Malay voters:** Since 2008, this group has rejected UMNO. They prioritize governance, transparency, and accountability over party dominance.
2. **Voters who want an Islamic agenda:** This group has stayed with PAS since its establishment in 1951 as a result of clashing directions with UMNO.
3. **Fence-sitter voters / former UMNO voters:** This group is estimated at around 40% of Malay voters.

Even if UMNO pushes the Malay unity narrative as far as possible, the maximum potential they can achieve on their own is around 40%. If a three-cornered fight occurs between PAS, BN, and PH, it will not restore the dominant number of seats like in the pre-2008 era. Even if they revive Muafakat Nasional, PAS will not hand over seats it has already won. BN's seats would likely peak at around 50 to 60 seats at most.

That is why Dr Akmal dares to speak out—because he is voicing the true sentiment of the grassroots. To date, UMNO's top leadership has not succeeded in convincing the grassroots that working with Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim and PH is the right move. An estimated 80% of division leaders and grassroots activists feel uncomfortable because they view this cooperation as contradicting their original foundational struggle.

The real test for Datuk Seri Zahid Hamidi is how to lead UMNO into the next general election when the majority of grassroots leaders disagree with that coalition strategy.

---

**Farhan:** The second topic touches on the announcement of a round of reforms by the Prime Minister, Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim, such as PM term limits, the Freedom of Information (FOI) Act, the separation of powers between the Public Prosecutor and Attorney General, as well as the Ombudsman. Has this put to rest criticisms that Kerajaan Madani's reforms have been slow?

**Haziq:** Based on observations on social media, there have been positive reactions, especially regarding the PM term limit. However, there are still skeptical voices asking why this announcement is only being made now after three years in power, and whether it is due to certain political pressures or election factors.

**Rafizi Ramli:** If you ask ordinary citizens who might only spend one hour a week keeping up with politics, to what extent do these announcements of four institutional reforms change their daily lives?

**Farhan:** Directly, perhaps not at all, but for urban fence-sitters, it provides a feel-good factor.

**Rafizi Ramli:** This is similar to macroeconomic achievements—strong growth, incoming investments, and a strengthening currency, yet it doesn't translate into cheers of joy at the grassroots level because the public does not feel it directly.

A reformist government must identify low-hanging fruits [easily attainable, high-impact targets] that are at the top of the people's priorities. Regarding institutional reform, the number one thing the public wants is the independence of enforcement agencies.

Monthly survey findings show an interesting trend: Even though the Prime Minister frequently emphasizes governance issues, public perception regarding anti-corruption performance has been declining. At the start of the administration, only about 8% of respondents expressed concern about corruption, but within two and a half years, that figure rose to around 35%—a fourfold increase.

The main reason is that the public judges a government's anti-corruption resolve through the courage and independence in prosecuting and imprisoning high-profile figures. 

The impact of structural announcements like FOI or PM term limits wears thin when the public sees high-profile politician cases frequently delayed, granted a discharge not amounting to an acquittal (DNAA), or like the recent case where Datuk Seri Zahid Hamidi was fully acquitted of 47 charges. When reform announcements were made last week and a week later a full acquittal decision was delivered, public perception easily concluded that it was merely a political game to balance pressure from DAP and UMNO.

The real low-hanging fruit issues are the independence of institutions such as the Malaysian Anti-Corruption Commission (SPRM), the separation of the roles of Prime Minister and Finance Minister, and the Political Funding Act. As long as implementation details are not proven to be transparent and high-profile figures continue to be freed, public perception of the government's anti-corruption efforts will not change dramatically.

**Farhan:** Our final topic is naturally about Bersatu and Perikatan Nasional (PN). Divisions are occurring in some branches, while Tan Sri Muhyiddin Yassin's resignation as Chairman of PN is said to be unconfirmed. Is this another political strategy?

**Haziq:** Many view this hanging resignation as Muhyiddin's bluff message to PAS that PN needs Bersatu.

**Rafizi Ramli:** There are Bersatu leaders like Datuk Saifuddin Abdullah who are trying to take advantage of this situation. In my view, Saifuddin's political journey has been deeply regrettable. He was first featured in GE12 in 2008 as an intellectual internal reformist within UMNO. After leaving UMNO, he joined PKR, and then moved to Bersatu. Unfortunately, the intellectual and reformist image he previously built has gradually eroded. 

When Bersatu faces a leadership and direction crisis like the current one, his remaining political space becomes extremely limited.
